Ashoka’s inscriptions survive in copies at several sites. The article list below gives one page per **distinct edict text**, rather than one page for each stone. The numbered Prakrit articles show an original-script facsimile, the Roman transliteration of a named site version, the complete surviving text translated using Hultzsch’s 1925 edition, and a short explanation. In localized articles, the translation appears in the language of the page. Damaged passages are marked as they appear in the edition. The Greek and Aramaic pages use the separate scholarly editions cited there.

The fourteen major rock edicts and seven major pillar edicts are numbered in modern scholarship. The separate Kalinga edicts, shorter rock and pillar messages, and Barabar cave dedications use their conventional names. The image on a page may show neighboring texts on the same stone or plate.

## Reading the inscriptions

Ashoka commonly calls himself Devānāṃpriya Priyadarśin (“Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi”). The surviving texts are mainly in regional forms of Prakrit; the northwestern copies use Kharoṣṭhī script, while many other copies use Brāhmī. Spellings, damaged passages, and even wording can vary between sites. The transliteration and translation in each article follow the version named there.

The three Barabar cave texts record gifts of caves. The Greek and Aramaic inscriptions from the northwest survive in different forms from the Prakrit edicts; those texts are listed separately below with their own sources and readings. The Kandahar Greek fragment covers portions of Rock Edicts XII and XIII, while the Kandahar Aramaic fragment parallels Pillar Edict VII. Their pages preserve those distinct language versions and clearly mark the stone’s missing portions.

Several northwest texts that mention King Priyadarśin have also been called “Ashokan edicts.” For Laghman I and II, Pul-i Darunta, and Taxila, attribution and interpretation are disputed. Laghman I, Pul-i Darunta, and Taxila appear below as **associated inscriptions** so readers can see the evidence without treating every inscription as a royal proclamation. Laghman II is still being checked against its published transcription and is not yet included as an article. In particular, Harry Falk argues that the two Laghman records may be legal or administrative texts dated under Priyadarśin, and that the Taxila fragment does not establish royal authorship.
