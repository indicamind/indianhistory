## About this inscription

Laghman I was found on a rock in Afghanistan. Its first line names King Priyadarśin, and its last names a judge. The intervening words mention measures or distances, a place name, and a road. André Dupont-Sommer read the date as year ten and the place name *Tdmr* as Tadmor (Palmyra), and translated the opening as a royal moral command. Franz Rosenthal instead read the date as year seventeen and treated the record as a legal verdict. Both the place-name reading and the nature of the text are disputed. Harry Falk likewise cautions that its royal authorship cannot be established. It is included here as an **associated inscription**, not presented as a certain Ashokan edict.

## English translation

All six inscribed lines survive, but their sense is not settled. The rendering below follows Dupont-Sommer’s reading where words can be interpreted and leaves obscure groups untranslated. Rosenthal’s date and legal reading differ substantially.

> 1. In year ten [or seventeen in another reading], [the inscription] names King Priyadarśin. The rest of the line is uncertain.
> 2–3. Words read as a statement about vanity, friends, and fishing; the syntax and meaning are disputed.
> 4. A distance of 200 [units] is given, followed by a place written *Tdmr* and a road name. Whether *Tdmr* refers to Palmyra is disputed.
> 5. Further numbers and distances are recorded; their units and destinations are uncertain.
> 6. The final line names Wʾšw, a judge.
