Arjuna asks why Krishna urges him into terrible action if understanding is superior to action. Krishna explains the inevitability of action, sacrifice and the gods, the example set by leaders, and the performance of one’s own duty. The chapter ends by identifying desire and anger as enemies that obscure knowledge and must be overcome.

Each **Meaning** renders the Sanskrit in English. Each **Explanation** follows the verse’s statements, terms and argument. Named traditional interpretations are distinguished where they differ. [1–2]

## 3.1

### Meaning

Arjuna said: Janardana, if you consider understanding higher than action, why do you direct me toward this dreadful action, Keshava?

### Explanation

Arjuna asks why Krishna commands him to fight after praising understanding above action. The dreadful action is the battle against his relatives and teachers. He takes the praise of knowledge to imply that he should renounce action, and asks why Krishna directs him toward war instead.

## 3.2

### Meaning

Your apparently mixed words seem to confuse my understanding. Tell me one thing decisively, through which I may attain what is best.

### Explanation

The repeated sense of “seem” leaves room for a distinction Arjuna has not yet grasped. He asks for a clear course because competing instructions leave him unable to act. His word *śreyas* concerns what is ultimately beneficial, continuing the question he raised in 2.7. Krishna’s answer must therefore explain how knowledge and action relate, as well as identify an immediate choice.

## 3.3

### Meaning

The Lord said: Blameless one, I previously taught a twofold commitment in this world: the discipline of knowledge for the discerning, and the discipline of action for yogins.

### Explanation

Krishna names two orientations of sustained practice, using *niṣṭhā* for a settled commitment. *Sāṃkhya* here is connected with discriminating understanding, while *karma-yoga* concerns disciplined action. Their distinction answers Arjuna’s impression that every statement must prescribe the same immediate practice for everyone. The following verses examine the preparation and inner freedom that outward renunciation alone cannot supply.

## 3.4

### Meaning

A person does not attain freedom from action merely by leaving actions unbegun, nor attain perfection simply through renunciation.

### Explanation

*Naiṣkarmya* is freedom from action and its binding effects. Leaving duties undone does not produce it, and taking up renunciation does not by itself produce perfection. Krishna rejects the assumption that ceasing outward activity is sufficient for liberation.

## 3.5

### Meaning

No one ever remains without acting, even for a moment. Everyone is made to act, without full independence, by qualities born of nature.

### Explanation

Action includes more than the deliberate work Arjuna is considering. Embodied life involves movement, perception, breathing and the continuing activities of mind. Krishna locates these processes in *prakṛti*, nature, working through its *guṇas*. This wider account makes a simple escape through bodily inactivity inadequate. The question becomes how a person understands and directs participation in activity that is already taking place.

## 3.6

### Meaning

One who restrains the organs of action but keeps recalling sense objects in the mind is confused and is called a practitioner of false conduct.

### Explanation

*Mithyācāra* means false conduct or hypocrisy. The person restrains the bodily organs while continuing to dwell mentally on the pleasures those organs could obtain. Krishna condemns this division between outward restraint and inward attachment.

## 3.7

### Meaning

But one who governs the senses with the mind and undertakes the discipline of action through the organs of action, without attachment, is superior, Arjuna.

### Explanation

Krishna ranks the person who controls the senses and performs action without attachment above the hypocritical renunciant of 3.6. The organs continue to act under the governance of the mind. Relinquishing attachment accompanies the performance of action rather than merely the appearance of restraint.

## 3.8

### Meaning

Perform the action that is yours to do. Action is better than inaction; even sustaining your bodily life would not succeed through inaction.

### Explanation

*Niyata karma* is prescribed or obligatory action. Krishna commands Arjuna to perform it and ranks action above inaction. Even maintaining the body requires action, so complete inactivity would prevent bodily subsistence itself.

## 3.9

### Meaning

Except for action undertaken for sacrifice, this world is bound by action. Therefore, son of Kunti, perform action for that purpose, free from attachment.

### Explanation

*Yajña* means sacrifice. Action binds the world unless performed for sacrifice, so Krishna commands Arjuna to act for that purpose without attachment. The following verses describe offerings to the gods and the benefits they provide in return.

## 3.10

### Meaning

In the beginning, the Lord of creatures created beings together with sacrifice and said: Through this you shall flourish; let it yield the things you desire.

### Explanation

*Prajāpati*, the Lord of creatures, creates beings together with sacrifice. He appoints sacrifice as the means by which they are to flourish and obtain their desires. The comparison with a wish-yielding source of abundance presents sacrifice as part of the divinely established order of creation.

## 3.11

### Meaning

Nourish the gods through this, and let those gods nourish you. Nourishing one another, you will attain the highest good.

### Explanation

Human beings are to nourish the gods through sacrifice, and the gods are to nourish human beings in return. Krishna says this mutual nourishment leads to the highest good. The relationship is specifically between worshippers and gods, sustained through sacrificial offerings and divine provision.

## 3.12

### Meaning

The gods, nourished by sacrifice, will give you desired enjoyments. Whoever enjoys their gifts without returning an offering to them is indeed a thief.

### Explanation

The gods give desired enjoyments after being nourished through sacrifice. A person who consumes those gifts without offering anything back to the gods is called a thief. The charge concerns withholding the sacrificial return owed to the divine givers.

## 3.13

### Meaning

Good people who eat what remains after sacrifice are freed from faults. Those who cook only for themselves eat wrongdoing.

### Explanation

Those who eat the food left after sacrifice are released from sins. Those who cook only for their own consumption eat sin itself. Krishna distinguishes sacrificial food from food prepared for private enjoyment, and attributes purification to eating the sacrificial remainder.

## 3.14

### Meaning

Beings arise and live through food; food comes through rain; rain comes through sacrifice; sacrifice arises through action.

### Explanation

The verse sets out a causal sequence: beings depend on food, food depends on rain, rain is produced through sacrifice, and sacrifice is performed through action. Sacrificial activity thus sustains rainfall and the food on which living beings depend.

## 3.15

### Meaning

Know action to arise from Brahman, and Brahman to arise from the imperishable. Therefore all-pervading Brahman is continually established in sacrifice.

### Explanation

*Brahman* can denote sacred utterance or revelation as well as supreme reality. In this sequence it is commonly understood as the Veda, the sacred knowledge grounding sacrificial action, whose source is the imperishable. The chain therefore connects prescribed activity with its sacred foundation. Sacrifice is presented as a place where this revealed order becomes enacted in the human world.

## 3.16

### Meaning

One who does not follow the cycle thus set in motion, but lives wrongly and delights only in the senses, lives in vain, Partha.

### Explanation

The cycle is the sacrificial order described in 3.10–15. Krishna calls the person who does not follow it sinful, devoted to sensual enjoyment and living in vain. Refusal to participate in sacrifice is condemned together with a life directed toward the pleasures of the senses.

## 3.17

### Meaning

But for a person who delights in the self, is satisfied in the self and is content in the self alone, there is nothing that must be done.

### Explanation

Krishna recognises a condition beyond action motivated by an unmet personal need. The verse repeats satisfaction in the self to emphasise its completeness. This supplies a contrast with dependence on enjoyment and reward in the preceding passage. It also raises the next question: how might someone without private needs still act for the welfare and stability of others?

## 3.18

### Meaning

Such a person has no personal end to gain through action or through leaving action undone, and depends on no being for the attainment of a private purpose.

### Explanation

The person satisfied in the self has nothing to gain through either performing or omitting action. No other being is needed to obtain an end for that person. This describes the absence of any unfulfilled purpose that would make action necessary for personal attainment.

## 3.19

### Meaning

Therefore always perform the action that should be done, without attachment. By acting without attachment, a person reaches the highest.

### Explanation

Krishna commands Arjuna to perform the action that ought to be done, always and without attachment. Such action leads to the highest attainment. The instruction joins the continued performance of duty with relinquishment of attachment to its results.

## 3.20

### Meaning

Janaka and others attained perfection through action itself. You should also act with the holding together of the world in view.

### Explanation

Janaka and other royal sages are cited as having attained perfection through action. Krishna also gives *loka-saṃgraha*, holding the world together, as a reason for Arjuna to act. The performance of prescribed duties sustains the order followed by other people.

## 3.21

### Meaning

Whatever a leading person does, others do as well. Whatever standard that person establishes, the world follows.

### Explanation

Conduct communicates a standard even when no formal teaching is offered. A respected person’s choices become evidence for others about what deserves effort, honour or imitation. This explains why *loka-saṃgraha* matters for someone in Arjuna’s position. His action or withdrawal would become a public example, and its effects would include the patterns of behaviour that other people learn from it.

## 3.22

### Meaning

Partha, there is nothing I must accomplish in the three worlds, nothing unattained that I need to attain; nevertheless I continue in action.

### Explanation

Krishna declares that he lacks nothing in any of the three worlds and has no duty to fulfil for his own attainment. Nevertheless, he acts. His activity supplies the divine example of action performed without a personal end yet to be gained.

## 3.23

### Meaning

For if I ever failed to engage tirelessly in action, people would follow my course in every way, Partha.

### Explanation

The teaching about exemplary conduct now reaches its divine example. Krishna imagines what others would learn if his own activity stopped, showing that a model has consequences through imitation. His tireless engagement explains why freedom from personal need does not entail indifference to action. The verse also returns responsibility to the visible teacher: what he does must support the understanding he asks others to develop.

## 3.24

### Meaning

If I did not act, these worlds would fall into ruin. I would cause disorder and bring harm to these creatures.

### Explanation

Krishna says the worlds would perish if he ceased acting. He would become the cause of *saṅkara*, disorder or intermixture, and would destroy these creatures. The term echoes the breakdown of social and family order feared by Arjuna in Chapter 1. Krishna attributes the preservation of the worlds to his continued action.

## 3.25

### Meaning

As those without understanding act with attachment, Bharata, so the person of understanding should act without attachment, wishing to hold the world together.

### Explanation

Those without knowledge perform action out of attachment. The knower is to perform action without attachment, intending to maintain the world. Krishna prescribes continued action for both, while distinguishing the knower’s purpose from the attached person’s desire for results.

## 3.26

### Meaning

The wise person should not unsettle the understanding of people attached to action. Acting with discipline, the wise should encourage them to carry out their actions.

### Explanation

The wise person is told not to unsettle people who remain attached to action. Instead, the wise person should perform actions with discipline and encourage them to perform theirs. Krishna makes the preservation of their commitment to action part of the knower’s conduct.

## 3.27

### Meaning

All actions are performed by the qualities of nature. A person whose understanding is confused by ego thinks: I alone am the doer.

### Explanation

The *guṇas* of nature perform all actions. The person deluded by *ahaṃkāra*, the sense of “I,” attributes those actions to the self and thinks “I am the doer.” Krishna locates action in nature and identifies the ego’s claim of agency as a mistake.

## 3.28

### Meaning

But one who knows the truth about the distinctions of qualities and actions understands that qualities operate upon qualities, and does not become attached, mighty-armed one.

### Explanation

The knower distinguishes the qualities of nature and their actions. Senses and their objects belong to this field, so their interaction is understood as qualities acting upon qualities. Knowing this, the person does not become attached to the activity as the self’s own doing.

## 3.29

### Meaning

Those confused by nature’s qualities become attached to the actions of those qualities. One who knows the whole should not disturb those who know only a part.

### Explanation

People deluded by nature’s qualities attach themselves to the actions those qualities produce. Krishna tells the complete knower not to disturb these people, whose knowledge is incomplete. This repeats the prohibition in 3.26 against unsettling those still committed to action.

## 3.30

### Meaning

Entrusting all actions to me, with the mind directed toward the self, free from expectation and possessiveness, fight without the fever of agitation.

### Explanation

Krishna commands Arjuna to surrender all actions to him and fight. The required disposition is freedom from expectation, possessiveness and agitation, with attention directed toward the self. Offering action to Krishna and knowing the self accompany the command to enter battle.

## 3.31

### Meaning

People who continually follow this teaching of mine, with trust and without resentful fault-finding, are also released from the bonds of action.

### Explanation

Krishna promises release from the bonds of action to those who continually follow his teaching with faith and without fault-finding. The promise is attached to the acceptance and practice of the teaching he has just given, including surrender of action to him.

## 3.32

### Meaning

But those who find fault with this teaching of mine and do not follow it—know them to be deluded in all knowledge, lacking discernment and ruined.

### Explanation

Those who find fault with Krishna’s teaching and refuse to follow it are condemned as deluded concerning knowledge, lacking discernment and ruined. The verse states the consequence opposite to the release promised in 3.31 to faithful followers.

## 3.33

### Meaning

Even a knowledgeable person acts according to their own nature. Beings follow nature; what will forceful suppression accomplish?

### Explanation

Even a person with knowledge acts according to their own *prakṛti*, or nature. Beings follow their nature, and Krishna asks what restraint can accomplish against it. The following verse nevertheless commands resistance to attraction and aversion, distinguishing the power of nature from submission to those two impulses.

## 3.34

### Meaning

Attraction and aversion are established in each sense’s relation to its objects. Do not come under their control, for they are obstacles on one’s path.

### Explanation

Attraction and aversion belong to the senses in their relations with objects. Krishna commands the person not to submit to either, calling them enemies or obstacles on the path. Their presence in embodied life is distinguished from allowing them to govern action.

## 3.35

### Meaning

One’s own dharma, even imperfectly fulfilled, is better than another’s well performed. Death in one’s own dharma is better; another’s dharma brings danger.

### Explanation

*Svadharma* is one’s own prescribed duty. Krishna ranks its imperfect performance above the successful performance of another’s duty, and says even death in one’s own duty is better. For Arjuna, this concerns his duty as a Kshatriya to fight. Another’s duty is declared dangerous.

## 3.36

### Meaning

Arjuna said: Then what drives a person to commit wrongdoing, even unwillingly, as though compelled by force, descendant of Vrishni?

### Explanation

Arjuna shifts from choosing a path to understanding a failure of self-direction. A person may know what should be done and still experience a pressure that carries conduct elsewhere. His phrase “as though compelled” captures that gap between intention and performance. Krishna’s answer will identify desire and anger within the person’s own experience, making them the next objects of disciplined attention.

## 3.37

### Meaning

The Lord said: It is desire, it is anger, born from the quality of passion; consuming and deeply harmful. Know this as the enemy here.

### Explanation

Krishna links desire and anger as related expressions of *rajas*, the restless, impelling quality of nature. Desire presses toward possession or gratification; frustration can turn that movement into anger. Describing it as consuming explains why satisfaction alone does not settle it. The enemy in this part of the argument is the force that clouds understanding and takes command of action from within.

## 3.38

### Meaning

As smoke covers fire, dust covers a mirror and a covering surrounds an embryo, so this is covered by that desire.

### Explanation

The images show a capacity obscured rather than simply absent. Fire can be hidden by smoke, a mirror’s reflecting surface by dust, and an embryo by its surrounding membrane. Read with the following verse, what is covered is knowledge. The examples invite attention to the conditions that conceal understanding, making purification and disciplined practice intelligible as ways of removing an obstruction.

## 3.39

### Meaning

Knowledge is covered by this constant enemy of the discerning: desire, which is difficult to satisfy and burns like fire, son of Kunti.

### Explanation

The previous images now receive their explicit reference: desire conceals knowledge. Fire supplies a second aspect of the metaphor, since feeding a fire can increase its demand for fuel. Even a discerning person therefore has reason to remain attentive to desire’s workings. The verse explains why recognition must become a continuing discipline instead of remaining an isolated intellectual insight.

## 3.40

### Meaning

The senses, mind and understanding are said to be its dwelling places. Through them it confuses the embodied person by covering knowledge.

### Explanation

The senses, mind and understanding are the dwelling places of desire. Operating through them, desire covers knowledge and deludes the embodied self. Krishna identifies the faculties through which this enemy acts before commanding restraint of the senses in 3.41.

## 3.41

### Meaning

Therefore, best of the Bharatas, first govern the senses and overcome this harmful force, which destroys knowledge and realised understanding.

### Explanation

Krishna commands Arjuna first to restrain the senses and then destroy desire. It is called sinful and destructive of both *jñāna*, knowledge, and *vijñāna*, realised understanding. The senses were named among its dwelling places in 3.40 and are the starting point of the commanded restraint.

## 3.42

### Meaning

The senses are said to be higher; higher than the senses is the mind, higher than the mind is understanding, and beyond understanding is the self.

### Explanation

The verse orders the faculties from the senses through mind and understanding to the self beyond understanding. Each successive term is higher than the preceding one. The self’s superiority provides the basis for the command in 3.43 to master desire.

## 3.43

### Meaning

Thus knowing the self as beyond understanding, steady the self through the self, mighty-armed one, and overcome the difficult enemy that takes the form of desire.

### Explanation

Knowing the self to be beyond understanding, Arjuna is to steady the self through the self and kill desire, the difficult enemy. The repeated *ātman* refers both to the self that must be steadied and to that by which it is steadied. The chapter ends with a command to overcome desire through knowledge and self-mastery.
