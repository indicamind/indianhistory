Arjuna again asks whether he should renounce action or practise yoga through action. Chapter 5 answers by examining the kind of relinquishment that can accompany an active life. It moves from agency and the senses to equal vision, inward contentment and peace, ending with Krishna’s declaration that he is the friend of all beings.

Each **Meaning** gives an original English rendering of the displayed Sanskrit. Each **Explanation** follows the verse’s wording, its place in the dialogue and its philosophical or devotional context. These are original reader explanations; named traditional interpretations are identified where they are compared. [1–2]

## 5.1

### Meaning

Arjuna said: Krishna, you praise the renunciation of actions and also yoga. Tell me decisively which one of these two is better.

### Explanation

The question returns in a more focused form after Chapter 4. Arjuna has heard that knowledge burns action and that he should rise into yoga, so he again asks how renunciation and disciplined activity fit together. He seeks a practical decision, not merely agreement that both have value. The chapter’s answer will clarify what is relinquished when a person acts with understanding.

## 5.2

### Meaning

The Lord said: Renunciation and the discipline of action both lead to the highest good. Of the two, however, the discipline of action is superior to renunciation of action.

### Explanation

Krishna acknowledges the liberating aim of both orientations while giving Arjuna a definite preference. The later verses explain why: the inner freedom appropriate to renunciation is difficult to establish without discipline, while action can prepare and express that freedom. The comparison therefore belongs to a conversation about actual spiritual practice, including the conditions under which a person can undertake it well.

## 5.3

### Meaning

Recognise as a continual renunciant the person who neither hates nor craves. Free from opposing pulls, that person is readily released from bondage, mighty-armed one.

### Explanation

Renunciation is now identified through the person’s relationship to attraction and aversion. The description makes it possible to recognise relinquishment within an active life. A person no longer has to organise conduct around securing the pleasant and excluding the unpleasant. This freedom from opposing pulls supplies the inner basis for the claim that disciplined action can lead toward the same highest good.

## 5.4

### Meaning

The immature speak of the way of knowledge and the way of action as entirely separate; the wise do not. By becoming firmly established in either one, a person obtains the fruit of both.

### Explanation

The verse considers the two ways through the fulfilment to which properly undertaken practice leads. Krishna criticises a division that loses sight of their relationship. “Established” matters: the claim concerns a way actually carried through, with its required understanding and discipline. It therefore expands the earlier distinction of approaches while preserving the need to practise the orientation appropriate to one’s condition.

## 5.5

### Meaning

The state reached by followers of knowledge is also reached by followers of yoga. One who sees knowledge and yoga as joined in their goal truly sees.

### Explanation

Krishna restates the convergence through the word *sthāna*, the state or destination reached. Action disciplined by yoga and the pursuit of knowledge are brought together through that fulfilment. In the chapter’s flow, this resolves Arjuna’s assumption that praising one must simply discredit the other. The next verse returns to the preparation that makes genuine renunciation possible.

## 5.6

### Meaning

Renunciation is difficult to attain without yoga, mighty-armed one. The contemplative who is joined to yoga reaches Brahman before long.

### Explanation

The difficulty concerns attaining the reality of renunciation, including freedom from craving and possessive identification. Outward withdrawal can leave those habits untouched. Yoga supplies the preparation through which mind and action become more coherent. Krishna connects that disciplined preparation with approaching Brahman, explaining why active practice can be recommended to someone whose goal includes the freedom associated with renunciation.

## 5.7

### Meaning

One joined to yoga, inwardly purified, self-governed and master of the senses, whose self is the self of all beings, remains unstained even while acting.

### Explanation

The description gathers together practice, purification, restraint and a widened understanding of self. Its final clause makes the relation to action explicit: activity continues, but does not cling to the person as a stain. Seeing self in relation to all beings also prepares the equal vision of 5.18. Inward freedom and a less possessive relationship with others develop within the same account.

## 5.8

### Meaning

A disciplined knower of reality should understand: I do nothing at all, even while seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, walking, sleeping and breathing.

### Explanation

The examples cover ordinary embodied life, including activities that continue without deliberate effort. The knower distinguishes their occurrence from the ego’s claim to be the sole owner and author of everything happening. The sentence continues into 5.9, where Krishna explains this understanding through the relation between senses and their objects. It applies the earlier distinction of self and nature to familiar daily experience.

## 5.9

### Meaning

Even while speaking, releasing, taking, opening and closing the eyes, the knower understands that the senses are operating among their objects.

### Explanation

This completes the extended sentence begun in 5.8. Bodily functions and outward engagements are understood through the capacities involved, instead of being appropriated without examination into “I am doing everything.” The verse carries the analysis of *guṇas* and action in 3.27–28 into the knower’s daily awareness. Its aim is a change in identification accompanying activity.

## 5.10

### Meaning

One who places actions in Brahman and acts after relinquishing attachment is not stained by wrongdoing, just as a lotus leaf is not wetted by water.

### Explanation

The lotus image shows contact without clinging. The leaf remains in its watery setting, while water does not adhere to it in the ordinary way. Similarly, the practitioner acts while offering activity into Brahman and relinquishing possessiveness. The metaphor gives a visible form to the chapter’s argument that spiritual freedom can be lived through a transformed relationship to action.

## 5.11

### Meaning

Yogins act through body, mind, understanding and the senses themselves, relinquishing attachment, for the purification of the self.

### Explanation

The different instruments of activity are all included. Purification gives their use a coherent direction, so neither bodily work nor intellectual effort stands outside practice. Krishna again specifies the relinquishment of attachment as essential. The verse also distinguishes a purpose of spiritual preparation from the pursuit of private rewards, helping explain why disciplined action has been recommended to Arjuna.

## 5.12

### Meaning

The disciplined person relinquishes the fruit of action and attains settled peace. The undisciplined person, driven by desire and attached to the fruit, becomes bound.

### Explanation

The same central contrast is expressed through two different outcomes: established peace and bondage. Desire turns the result into something on which the person’s fulfilment depends. Relinquishment loosens that dependence while allowing the work to be done. The verse therefore describes the inner consequence of an orientation in action, building on the earlier distinction between action as purification and action as acquisition.

## 5.13

### Meaning

Mentally relinquishing all actions, the self-governed embodied one dwells happily in the city of nine gates, neither acting nor causing action.

### Explanation

The body is pictured as a city with openings through which embodied life communicates with its surroundings. The indwelling self is distinguished from the activities associated with those openings. The renunciation is expressly located in the mind, continuing the chapter’s concern with identification and agency. The city image makes it possible to describe ongoing bodily life together with the knower’s inward freedom.

## 5.14

### Meaning

The lord creates neither agency nor the actions of the world, nor the joining of action with its fruit. Rather, one’s conditioned nature operates.

### Explanation

The word *prabhu*, lord or sovereign, appears within the discussion of agency and the indwelling self. The sentence directs attention toward *svabhāva*, nature or the conditioning through which action unfolds. Its point is to question the attribution of the entire process to a sovereign self that stands apart from it. The following verse connects mistaken attribution with ignorance that covers knowledge.

## 5.15

### Meaning

The all-pervading one takes on neither anyone’s wrongdoing nor their good action. Knowledge is covered by ignorance; through that, beings are confused.

### Explanation

The verse extends the preceding distinction between the sovereign principle and the workings of embodied nature. It names ignorance as the covering through which people misunderstand that relation. The image also recalls 3.38–39, where knowledge was obscured by desire. Krishna’s answer therefore moves toward illumination: freedom requires the removal of a mistaken understanding that makes appropriation and confusion seem natural.

## 5.16

### Meaning

But for those whose ignorance of the self has been destroyed by knowledge, that knowledge illuminates the supreme as the sun does.

### Explanation

Sunlight provides the counterpart to the covering described in 5.15. Knowledge makes clear what ignorance had concealed, bringing a change in vision rather than merely adding another desired possession. The object illuminated is called *tat param*, the supreme. The following verse develops what happens when understanding, commitment and the direction of a person’s life become established in that reality.

## 5.17

### Meaning

With understanding directed to that, self established in that, steadfast in that and taking that as their highest refuge, they reach the state of no return, their faults cleared away by knowledge.

### Explanation

The repeated “that” draws several dimensions of a person together: discernment, identity, steadfastness and ultimate orientation. The promised state of no return belongs to the Gita’s account of release from repeated birth. Knowledge’s purifying role is again central. The verse describes a whole life organised around the reality illuminated in 5.16, preparing the account of how such a person sees other beings.

## 5.18

### Meaning

The wise look with equal vision on a learned and humble brahmin, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a person who cooks dogs.

### Explanation

The list crosses distinctions of social standing, species and ritual valuation. The final term, literally a dog-cooker, names a stigmatised human position in the verse’s setting. Equal vision reaches across these contrasts by attending to the reality that bodily or social descriptions do not exhaust. The following verse identifies Brahman’s equality as the foundation for this way of seeing.

## 5.19

### Meaning

Those whose minds rest in equality have overcome the cycle of becoming here itself. Brahman is faultless and equal; therefore they are established in Brahman.

### Explanation

“Here itself” places the transformation within the present life. Equality of vision is grounded in how Brahman is understood, and the person’s mind becomes established in that sameness. The verse thus supplies a philosophical basis for the striking list in 5.18. Its focus is a way of seeing that frees a person from the divisions and reactive judgments through which attachment is continually renewed.

## 5.20

### Meaning

One who knows Brahman and is established in Brahman should neither exult on receiving what is pleasant nor be shaken on meeting what is unpleasant, remaining clear and steady in understanding.

### Explanation

The equal vision of the preceding verses now appears in the person’s response to events. Pleasant and unpleasant experiences still occur, while understanding remains steady through their arrival. This returns to the description of stable wisdom in Chapter 2. Establishment in Brahman is expressed in the manner of meeting changing circumstances, giving the metaphysical teaching an immediate connection with lived experience.

## 5.21

### Meaning

Unattached to external contacts, a person finds happiness in the self. With the self joined to Brahman through yoga, that person experiences an enduring happiness.

### Explanation

Krishna contrasts dependence on changing contacts with a happiness discovered inwardly. The verse’s movement reaches beyond a temporary pleasant experience to the stability associated with yoga in Brahman. This develops the theme of contentment that made renunciation intelligible at the chapter’s beginning. The following verse explains why enjoyment dependent on contact cannot provide the same settled basis for fulfilment.

## 5.22

### Meaning

Enjoyments born from contact are sources of suffering, for they have a beginning and an end, son of Kunti. The discerning person does not make their delight depend upon them.

### Explanation

The reason given is the changing character of such enjoyments. An experience begins, alters and ends; attachment asks it to supply a lasting security that its form cannot sustain. Krishna connects that demand with suffering. The argument therefore concerns the place assigned to pleasure in a life, explaining the previous verse’s turn toward a happiness whose basis is less dependent on external conditions.

## 5.23

### Meaning

One who can withstand, here and before leaving the body, the surge arising from desire and anger is disciplined and is a happy person.

### Explanation

The image is a surge or force that presses toward action. Desire and anger have already been linked in 3.37 as movements capable of covering knowledge. Here the capacity to bear their pressure is connected with happiness and yoga in this present life. The verse makes the practice concrete: freedom includes developing a steadier response while the impulses of embodied existence are still encountered.

## 5.24

### Meaning

One whose happiness is inward, whose delight is inward and whose light is inward is a yogin who, established in Brahman, reaches liberation in Brahman.

### Explanation

The repeated turn inward joins happiness, fulfilment and illumination. *Brahma-nirvāṇa* names the liberation or peace in Brahman that completes this movement. The verse gathers earlier images of inward contentment and the sun of knowledge into a portrait of realised life. Its neighbouring verses also connect this inward freedom with the welfare of other beings, keeping inner fulfilment within a wider ethical horizon.

## 5.25

### Meaning

The seers whose faults have diminished, whose divided doubts are cut away, whose selves are governed and who delight in the welfare of all beings attain liberation in Brahman.

### Explanation

Concern for the good of all beings belongs directly to this description of the seer. Purification, clarity and self-governance develop together with that concern. The verse therefore gives equal vision an active ethical dimension: the wider understanding of self is accompanied by delight in others’ welfare. It continues the concern for sustaining the world introduced through *loka-saṃgraha* in Chapter 3.

## 5.26

### Meaning

For striving renunciants who are free from desire and anger, whose minds are governed and who know the self, liberation in Brahman is near on every side.

### Explanation

The surrounding nearness of liberation conveys the completeness of the orientation being described. Desire and anger no longer dominate, the mind has been trained, and knowledge of self has matured. The verse gathers these conditions around the renunciant’s practice. It also leads naturally into the following description of meditation, where the governance of senses, breath and attention is considered more specifically.

## 5.27

### Meaning

The practitioner keeps external contacts outside, directs the gaze between the eyebrows and balances the movements of prana and apana within the nostrils.

### Explanation

This begins a description completed in 5.28. External objects are set aside as the organising focus, and attention is gathered with the help of bodily and breathing discipline. *Prāṇa* and *apāna* are the traditional terms for the vital movements named here. The passage introduces a contemplative setting that Chapter 6 will develop through a fuller discussion of posture, steadiness and the training of mind.

## 5.28

### Meaning

The sage who governs senses, mind and understanding, who is intent on liberation and free from desire, fear and anger, is always free.

### Explanation

The preceding bodily description receives its larger purpose: the sage’s orientation toward liberation. Governing the faculties is joined with freedom from the emotional pressures named repeatedly in the chapter. The passage therefore connects meditation with the same transformation of understanding and attachment that has characterised disciplined action. Its claim concerns a settled freedom expressed through the whole person’s way of being.

## 5.29

### Meaning

Knowing me as the recipient and enjoyer of sacrifices and austerities, the great Lord of all worlds and the friend of all beings, a person reaches peace.

### Explanation

The chapter ends by identifying Krishna personally within the sacred order it has described. He is the one toward whom offerings are directed, the Lord of the worlds and the friend of beings. These roles bring together worship, cosmic sovereignty and care. The final promise of peace therefore joins knowledge and renunciation with a relationship to Krishna, preparing the devotional orientation within the next chapter’s meditation teaching.
