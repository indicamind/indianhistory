After describing meditation, Krishna turns to the reality toward which practice is directed. He speaks as the source and support of the world, distinguishes two aspects of his nature, and asks why beings fail to recognise him. The chapter moves between ordinary experience and comprehensive theological claims: water, light, desire, divine forms and the prospect of liberation all enter the argument.

Each **Meaning** gives an original English rendering of the displayed Sanskrit. Each **Explanation** follows the verse’s wording, its place in the dialogue and its philosophical or devotional context. These are original reader explanations; named traditional interpretations are identified where they are compared. [1–2]

## 7.1

### Meaning

Krishna said: With your mind attached to me, practising yoga and taking refuge in me, hear how you will know me fully and without doubt, Partha.

### Explanation

The emphasis falls on a relationship that shapes inquiry. Arjuna is asked to listen while directing his mind toward Krishna and practising the yoga already discussed. Knowledge is presented as something approached through sustained orientation, trust and discipline. The promise of knowing “fully” gives the chapter its scope: Krishna will discuss both the world's diversity and its dependence on him, rather than only an isolated technique of meditation.

## 7.2

### Meaning

I shall explain to you this knowledge together with realised understanding, completely. Knowing it, nothing further remains here to be known.

### Explanation

The pairing of jñāna and vijñāna joins knowing with a more fully discriminated or realised understanding. The sweeping final phrase concerns the fundamental reality the teaching is seeking, rather than a catalogue of every worldly fact. Within the chapter, understanding the source also clarifies the status of the many things that arise from it. This is why a teaching about Krishna can make such a comprehensive claim.

## 7.3

### Meaning

Among thousands of people, perhaps one strives for perfection; even among those who strive and attain, perhaps one knows me in truth.

### Explanation

The verse distinguishes striving, accomplishment and knowing Krishna as he really is. Spiritual effort is not treated as automatically identical with its final fulfilment. The repeated “one” conveys rarity, while the following verses begin supplying the understanding that the rare knower possesses. Read in sequence, the statement heightens the seriousness of listening and practice; it does not provide a numerical census of who can be liberated.

## 7.4

### Meaning

Earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, understanding and the sense of “I”: this is my nature divided into eight aspects.

### Explanation

Krishna's account of prakṛti includes mental functions alongside the elements. Mind, discriminating understanding and ego-sense belong within the world's articulated nature, rather than standing outside everything that changes. The list uses the categories of its philosophical setting. Its role is to map embodied experience and dependence on Krishna; the eight aspects are not a list of the chemical elements used by modern physical science.

## 7.5

### Meaning

This is my lower nature. Know another nature of mine, higher than it, which becomes living being and sustains this world, mighty-armed Arjuna.

### Explanation

The “higher” nature introduces life or conscious being into the account. Both natures are called Krishna's, so the contrast is made within an encompassing divine source. The verse also says that the living principle sustains the world: a world of material and mental processes is understood in relation to its experiencers. The precise relation between individual selves and the Lord receives different philosophical interpretations, while this verse stresses their dependence on him.

## 7.6

### Meaning

Understand that all beings arise from these. I am the origin of the whole world and also its dissolution.

### Explanation

The two natures of the preceding verses supply the immediate reference of “these.” Krishna then brings their combined activity under one claim about himself as beginning and ending. Prabhava and pralaya refer to arising and dissolution on a cosmic scale. The argument is moving from an inventory of experience toward its ultimate ground: the world's many beings have a shared dependence, even though their particular forms differ.

## 7.7

### Meaning

There is nothing whatsoever higher than me, Dhananjaya. All this is strung on me like clusters of gems on a thread.

### Explanation

The thread image makes dependence visible through a familiar object. The gems remain multiple, yet their arrangement as a necklace requires the support running through them. Krishna identifies himself with that supporting reality and also claims that nothing surpasses him. The comparison therefore joins divine immanence and supremacy: the world rests upon him even when the support is less immediately noticeable than the things it holds together.

## 7.8

### Meaning

I am the taste in water, son of Kunti, the radiance in the moon and sun, Om in all the Vedas, sound in space and human vigour in people.

### Explanation

The teaching now enters ordinary perception. Taste, light, sound and human capacity become occasions for recognising Krishna's presence. Om connects that recognition with sacred recitation, while water and sunlight place it within daily life. Pauruṣa carries the sense of human or manly capacity and vigour in this setting. The examples encourage attention to enabling qualities: what makes water refreshing, a light luminous, or a person capable of action?

## 7.9

### Meaning

I am the pure fragrance in the earth and the brilliance in fire. I am life in all beings and austerity in those who practise it.

### Explanation

The examples join the elemental world, living existence and deliberate spiritual effort. “Pure fragrance” directs attention to the earth through smell, while fire appears through its heat and splendour. Life and tapas then extend the same recognition to vitality and disciplined striving. These are distinct experiences, yet Krishna presents their sustaining or characteristic power as a way of encountering his presence throughout the world.

## 7.10

### Meaning

Know me as the eternal seed of all beings, Partha. I am the understanding of the intelligent and the splendour of the splendid.

### Explanation

The seed describes a generative source: the many beings depend on a principle from which their becoming is possible. Calling it eternal separates that source from the short life of any particular seed or organism. The second line gives a corresponding account of excellence. Intelligence and brilliance can be acknowledged as real capacities while also being understood as dependent expressions of the divine source.

## 7.11

### Meaning

I am the strength of the strong, free from desire and attachment. In beings I am desire that does not conflict with dharma, best of the Bharatas.

### Explanation

The qualifications matter as much as the examples. Strength is praised in its freedom from possessive craving, and desire is recognised where it accords with dharma. The chapter therefore gives an evaluative account of divine manifestation; it does not simply approve whatever power or impulse happens to arise. This distinction connects its theology with the earlier teaching about acting without being driven by attachment.

## 7.12

### Meaning

Whatever conditions arise from sattva, rajas or tamas, know them as coming from me alone. Yet I am not in them; they are in me.

### Explanation

The three guṇas organise the qualities of nature and experience. Krishna locates even these diverse conditions within his sourcehood, then adds an asymmetry: their dependence upon him does not exhaust what he is. The language permits both presence and transcendence. It prepares the next question, because beings can become absorbed in particular qualities and fail to recognise the reality on which those qualities themselves depend.

## 7.13

### Meaning

Deluded by these three conditions made of the guṇas, this whole world does not recognise me, who am beyond them and imperishable.

### Explanation

The problem is one of misrecognition. People encounter the qualities through which experience takes shape, but take those changing conditions as the final measure of reality. Krishna identifies himself as beyond their alteration. The chapter's earlier examples can now be read as a corrective: a quality such as brilliance can point toward its source, instead of becoming an object in which attention stops completely.

## 7.14

### Meaning

This divine māyā of mine, made of the guṇas, is difficult to cross. Those who take refuge in me alone cross beyond this māyā.

### Explanation

Māyā here belongs to Krishna and operates through the guṇas; it is more than an isolated mistake made by one person. Its power explains why recognising the source is difficult despite that source's presence everywhere. Refuge provides the response the verse recommends. The movement beyond māyā is consequently presented through a change of dependence and orientation, as well as through the acquisition of correct concepts.

## 7.15

### Meaning

Those who act wrongly, the deluded, the lowest among people, whose knowledge is carried away by māyā and who adopt an asuric disposition, do not take refuge in me.

### Explanation

The verse uses forceful moral and religious classifications. Its immediate contrast is between taking refuge and a disposition that resists it through harmful conduct and obscured understanding. Āsura points toward the destructive qualities discussed at length in Chapter 16. Keeping this connection visible helps explain the rhetoric: the description concerns a pattern of character and action, not simply a person's lack of familiarity with a divine name.

## 7.16

### Meaning

Four kinds of people who act well worship me, Arjuna: the distressed, the seeker of knowledge, the seeker of material benefit and the knower, best of the Bharatas.

### Explanation

Devotion can begin from several needs. Suffering, curiosity, desire for benefit and understanding are all acknowledged as motives that can bring a person toward Krishna. The four are described as people of good action before their motives are distinguished. The following verses establish a hierarchy among them, but this opening classification makes room for forms of seeking that begin within ordinary human vulnerability and aspiration.

## 7.17

### Meaning

Among them the knower excels, always united with me and devoted to me alone. I am exceedingly dear to that knower, and that knower is dear to me.

### Explanation

The knower's distinction lies in constancy and undivided devotion. Knowledge and love are joined rather than assigned to unrelated spiritual paths: understanding Krishna shapes attachment to him, and Krishna speaks of a reciprocal dearness. This gives personal expression to the chapter's earlier language of source and support. The knower seeks Krishna himself as the end, rather than only something that Krishna might provide.

## 7.18

### Meaning

All these are noble, but I regard the knower as my very self. With the self integrated, that person abides in me alone as the unsurpassed goal.

### Explanation

Krishna preserves the dignity of all four kinds of devotees while describing the knower's exceptional intimacy. “My very self” is stronger than a statement of approval: it expresses a closeness understood differently across traditions that debate unity and enduring distinction. The second sentence explains the knower's orientation in the dialogue. Krishna has become the ultimate destination, so devotion is no longer governed by a separate desired reward.

## 7.19

### Meaning

At the end of many births, the person of knowledge takes refuge in me, realising, “Vasudeva is all.” Such a great soul is very rare.

### Explanation

The chapter's opening claim about rare knowledge returns as the conclusion of a long process. Vasudeva names Krishna, while “all” gathers up the world's many experiences and sources of attraction under one understanding. The statement links the breadth of knowledge with the act of taking refuge. Its reference to many births also places this transformation within the Gita's account of rebirth, rather than within the duration of a single achievement.

## 7.20

### Meaning

Those whose understanding is carried away by particular desires turn to other deities, adopting their respective observances and directed by their own nature.

### Explanation

The focus is the way a specific desired outcome can organise religious practice. A person seeks a corresponding divine power and undertakes its observances. Krishna describes this as limited understanding because attention remains governed by the particular reward. The verse introduces a theological hierarchy among forms of worship; the next verses explain how Krishna understands his own relationship to those other forms and to the faith of their worshippers.

## 7.21

### Meaning

Whatever form a devotee wishes to worship with faith, I make that person's faith in that form steady.

### Explanation

The divine forms are not presented as operating entirely outside Krishna's sustaining role. Even the steadiness of the worshipper's faith is attributed to him. This creates a layered account of religious action: the devotee attends to a particular form, while Krishna claims to support the capacity through which that worship continues. The following distinction between its immediate fruit and the highest goal remains important to the argument.

## 7.22

### Meaning

Endowed with that faith, the person undertakes the worship of that form and obtains the desired things from it, though those benefits are ordained by me alone.

### Explanation

The verse recognises that particular worship can yield the sought benefit. Its explanation of that success, however, returns to Krishna as the ultimate giver. Religious effectiveness and final understanding are therefore separate questions within the chapter. A desired result may be obtained while the worshipper still sees only a limited part of the divine dependence that Krishna has been describing since the account of his two natures.

## 7.23

### Meaning

But the fruit of those people of small understanding is finite. Worshippers of the deities go to the deities; my devotees come to me.

### Explanation

Finite fruit supplies the criterion behind the chapter's hierarchy. The concern is the enduring character of the destination, not merely whether a rite works. Krishna contrasts reaching a particular deity with reaching himself, whom the chapter describes as the imperishable source. The phrase about small understanding is part of that theological judgement, and should be read together with the explicit recognition that these worshippers possess real faith and receive benefits.

## 7.24

### Meaning

Those without discernment think of me, the unmanifest, as having become manifest, failing to know my higher nature, imperishable and unsurpassed.

### Explanation

Krishna distinguishes his appearance within experience from the full reality of his being. Seeing a visible person and assuming that the divine source began only with that appearance misses the “higher nature” asserted here. Interpretive traditions develop different accounts of manifestation, embodiment and divine form. The immediate claim is that Krishna's reality exceeds the limits that ordinary perception would place around a particular visible life.

## 7.25

### Meaning

I am not revealed to everyone, being veiled by yoga-māyā. This deluded world does not recognise me as unborn and imperishable.

### Explanation

Recognition is not automatic simply because Krishna is present. Yoga-māyā describes the power through which his presence can remain concealed from ordinary understanding. The terms “unborn” and “imperishable” answer the mistaken account of manifestation in the previous verse. The passage holds together disclosure and concealment: Krishna speaks to make himself known, while explaining why the same reality is not equally evident to every observer.

## 7.26

### Meaning

I know the beings that have passed, those that exist now and those yet to come, Arjuna; but no one knows me.

### Explanation

The asymmetry between divine knowing and ordinary knowing becomes explicit. Krishna's knowledge embraces the beings of past, present and future, while their usual understanding fails to comprehend him. Read beside the chapter's promises, the closing claim describes the situation overcome through the knowledge and refuge he is teaching. It gives theological depth to the earlier image of beings depending on a support they do not readily perceive.

## 7.27

### Meaning

Through the delusion of opposites, born of desire and aversion, all beings enter confusion at their coming into existence, descendant of Bharata, scorcher of foes.

### Explanation

Desire and aversion divide experience into what must be acquired and what must be rejected. Through that division, the opposites of pleasure and pain or gain and loss can become an exhaustive world. The chapter connects this familiar pattern with its larger account of delusion. Recognising Krishna requires a loosening of the compulsive preferences through which beings ordinarily interpret their situation and its possibilities.

## 7.28

### Meaning

But people of meritorious action whose wrongdoing has come to an end, freed from the delusion of opposites, worship me with firm resolve.

### Explanation

The response to delusion includes conduct as well as insight. Puṇya-karma connects the transformation with beneficial or meritorious action, while firm resolve describes the devotion that becomes possible as wrongdoing and compulsive opposition lose their hold. The verse thus joins ethical formation, clearer understanding and worship. These themes are not separate qualifications added at random; they describe mutually supporting aspects of the person's changed orientation.

## 7.29

### Meaning

Those who strive for freedom from old age and death, taking refuge in me, come to know Brahman, the whole reality of the self and action in its entirety.

### Explanation

The desire for liberation now brings several technical terms into view. Brahman, adhyātma and karma name dimensions of the reality the seeker must understand. The chapter presents refuge in Krishna as the basis of this comprehensive inquiry. Old age and death connect its goal to the vulnerability of embodied existence, while the breadth of the promised knowledge prepares Arjuna's request for more precise definitions in the next chapter.

## 7.30

### Meaning

Those who know me together with the domains of beings, divine powers and sacrifice know me, with an integrated mind, even at the time of departure.

### Explanation

The closing verse links an encompassing understanding of Krishna with remembrance at death. Adhibhūta, adhidaiva and adhiyajña are introduced without their definitions being supplied here; their explanation follows in Chapter 8. The final phrase places a demanding question before the dialogue: how can knowledge and practice remain effective at the moment when the ordinary supports of embodied life are falling away?
