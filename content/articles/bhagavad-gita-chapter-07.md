Krishna explains his lower and higher natures and identifies himself as the source and support of the world. He describes how the gunas and maya obscure knowledge of him, distinguishes those who refuse and those who seek him, and discusses worship of other gods. The chapter ends with knowing Brahman, action and Krishna at the time of death.

Each **Meaning** renders the Sanskrit in English. Each **Explanation** follows the verse’s statements, terms and argument. Named traditional interpretations are distinguished where they differ. [1–2]

## 7.1

### Meaning

Krishna said: With your mind attached to me, practising yoga and taking refuge in me, hear how you will know me fully and without doubt, Partha.

### Explanation

Krishna asks Arjuna to attach his mind to him, take refuge in him and practise yoga. These are the conditions under which he promises complete and doubtless knowledge of himself. The teaching that follows concerns his nature, his relation to the world and the worship through which he is attained. Listening is joined to the devotion and discipline commanded at the opening.

## 7.2

### Meaning

I shall explain to you this knowledge together with realised understanding, completely. Knowing it, nothing further remains here to be known.

### Explanation

Jñāna and vijñāna join knowledge with its realised or fully discriminated understanding. Krishna promises to teach both completely. The declaration that nothing further remains to be known gives this knowledge the highest place: knowing Krishna includes understanding the source of the world and the beings within it. The following account of his two natures begins to explain the scope of that claim.

## 7.3

### Meaning

Among thousands of people, perhaps one strives for perfection; even among those who strive and attain, perhaps one knows me in truth.

### Explanation

Krishna describes a succession of increasingly rare attainments. Few people strive for perfection, and even among those who strive and attain, few know him in truth. Spiritual accomplishment and true knowledge of Krishna are distinguished. The account of nature and divine supremacy that follows supplies the knowledge he is now offering Arjuna.

## 7.4

### Meaning

Earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, understanding and the sense of “I”: this is my nature divided into eight aspects.

### Explanation

Prakṛti, Krishna's nature, is divided into eight aspects. Five are the great elements: earth, water, fire, air and space. The other three are mind, discriminating understanding and ego, the sense of “I.” Mental activity and self-identification thus belong to the same nature as the elemental world. Krishna calls this his lower nature in the next verse.

## 7.5

### Meaning

This is my lower nature. Know another nature of mine, higher than it, which becomes living being and sustains this world, mighty-armed Arjuna.

### Explanation

Krishna distinguishes the eightfold lower nature from his higher nature, the living principle that sustains the world. The higher nature is described as jīva-bhūta, existing as living being. Both belong to him. The distinction joins the account of material and mental constituents to that of living selves, whose union with those constituents gives rise to the beings described next.

## 7.6

### Meaning

Understand that all beings arise from these. I am the origin of the whole world and also its dissolution.

### Explanation

The two natures of the preceding verses supply the immediate reference of “these.” Krishna then brings their combined activity under one claim about himself as beginning and ending. Prabhava and pralaya refer to arising and dissolution on a cosmic scale. The argument is moving from an inventory of experience toward its ultimate ground: the world's many beings have a shared dependence, even though their particular forms differ.

## 7.7

### Meaning

There is nothing whatsoever higher than me, Dhananjaya. All this is strung on me like clusters of gems on a thread.

### Explanation

The thread image makes dependence visible through a familiar object. The gems remain multiple, yet their arrangement as a necklace requires the support running through them. Krishna identifies himself with that supporting reality and also claims that nothing surpasses him. The comparison therefore joins divine immanence and supremacy: the world rests upon him even when the support is less immediately noticeable than the things it holds together.

## 7.8

### Meaning

I am the taste in water, son of Kunti, the radiance in the moon and sun, Om in all the Vedas, sound in space and human vigour in people.

### Explanation

Krishna identifies himself with qualities found throughout the world: water's taste, the light of sun and moon, sacred Om, sound in space and pauruṣa, human or manly vigour. The examples place elemental qualities, celestial radiance, Vedic utterance and human capacity under the same declaration. He is describing how his presence is found within the world whose origin and support he has just claimed to be.

## 7.9

### Meaning

I am the pure fragrance in the earth and the brilliance in fire. I am life in all beings and austerity in those who practise it.

### Explanation

The examples join the elemental world, living existence and deliberate spiritual effort. “Pure fragrance” directs attention to the earth through smell, while fire appears through its heat and splendour. Life and tapas then extend the same recognition to vitality and disciplined striving. These are distinct experiences, yet Krishna presents their sustaining or characteristic power as a way of encountering his presence throughout the world.

## 7.10

### Meaning

Know me as the eternal seed of all beings, Partha. I am the understanding of the intelligent and the splendour of the splendid.

### Explanation

Krishna calls himself the eternal seed of every being. Seed expresses the power from which beings arise, while “eternal” distinguishes this source from the perishing beings it produces. He also identifies himself with intelligence in the intelligent and splendour in the splendid. Both the existence of beings and their distinguished powers are attributed to him.

## 7.11

### Meaning

I am the strength of the strong, free from desire and attachment. In beings I am desire that does not conflict with dharma, best of the Bharatas.

### Explanation

The strength identified with Krishna is strength free from desire and attachment. His further identification with desire is qualified by dharma: he is the desire in beings that does not oppose it. The verse therefore specifies which strength and desire he means. Dharma remains the measure governing the second identification, just as freedom from craving and attachment governs the first.

## 7.12

### Meaning

Whatever conditions arise from sattva, rajas or tamas, know them as coming from me alone. Yet I am not in them; they are in me.

### Explanation

The three guṇas organise the qualities of nature and experience. Krishna locates even these diverse conditions within his sourcehood, then adds an asymmetry: their dependence upon him does not exhaust what he is. The language permits both presence and transcendence. It prepares the next question, because beings can become absorbed in particular qualities and fail to recognise the reality on which those qualities themselves depend.

## 7.13

### Meaning

Deluded by these three conditions made of the guṇas, this whole world does not recognise me, who am beyond them and imperishable.

### Explanation

Sattva, rajas and tamas produce the conditions that delude the entire world. Beings fail to recognise Krishna because they are deluded by those three qualities. He describes himself as beyond them and imperishable. The contrast is between the changing conditions of his nature and the Lord from whom they arise, whom the world does not know.

## 7.14

### Meaning

This divine māyā of mine, made of the guṇas, is difficult to cross. Those who take refuge in me alone cross beyond this māyā.

### Explanation

Krishna's divine māyā is composed of the three guṇas and is difficult to cross. He names refuge in himself alone as the means of crossing it. The possessive “my” makes māyā his power, while “me alone” specifies the refuge by which beings pass beyond it. The following verses distinguish those who refuse that refuge from the devotees who seek him.

## 7.15

### Meaning

Those who act wrongly, the deluded, the lowest among people, whose knowledge is carried away by māyā and who adopt an asuric disposition, do not take refuge in me.

### Explanation

Krishna names those who do not take refuge in him as wrongdoers, deluded people and the lowest among human beings. Their knowledge has been taken away by māyā, and they have adopted an asuric nature. These descriptions explain their failure to seek him. Chapter 16 will set out the asuric disposition and its consequences in greater detail.

## 7.16

### Meaning

Four kinds of people who act well worship me, Arjuna: the distressed, the seeker of knowledge, the seeker of material benefit and the knower, best of the Bharatas.

### Explanation

Krishna distinguishes four kinds of righteous worshippers according to what they seek. The distressed seeks relief, the inquirer seeks knowledge, the seeker of benefit seeks prosperity or advantage, and the knower already possesses understanding. All four worship him and are called people of good action. The next verse singles out the knower as the highest among them.

## 7.17

### Meaning

Among them the knower excels, always united with me and devoted to me alone. I am exceedingly dear to that knower, and that knower is dear to me.

### Explanation

The knower's distinction lies in constancy and undivided devotion. Knowledge and love are joined rather than assigned to unrelated spiritual paths: understanding Krishna shapes attachment to him, and Krishna speaks of a reciprocal dearness. This gives personal expression to the chapter's earlier language of source and support. The knower seeks Krishna himself as the end, rather than only something that Krishna might provide.

## 7.18

### Meaning

All these are noble, but I regard the knower as my very self. With the self integrated, that person abides in me alone as the unsurpassed goal.

### Explanation

All four kinds of devotees are called noble, but Krishna identifies the knower as his own self. The knower's integrated self is established in Krishna alone as the unsurpassed destination. “My very self” has been understood through different accounts of unity and enduring distinction between the Lord and the knower. The verse gives the knower a standing beyond the other three kinds of devotees.

## 7.19

### Meaning

At the end of many births, the person of knowledge takes refuge in me, realising, “Vasudeva is all.” Such a great soul is very rare.

### Explanation

Many births precede the knowledge described here. At their end, the knower takes refuge in Krishna with the understanding that Vasudeva is everything. Vasudeva is Krishna's name, and the declaration gathers the entire world under that identification. Krishna calls such a great soul very rare, returning to the rarity of knowing him in truth stated at the chapter's beginning.

## 7.20

### Meaning

Those whose understanding is carried away by particular desires turn to other deities, adopting their respective observances and directed by their own nature.

### Explanation

Desires carry away the understanding of those who turn to other deities. Directed by their own nature, they undertake the observances associated with those gods. Krishna thus connects the choice of deity and rite with the particular object desired. The following verses explain that he steadies their faith and grants their results, while judging those results to be finite.

## 7.21

### Meaning

Whatever form a devotee wishes to worship with faith, I make that person's faith in that form steady.

### Explanation

The devotee chooses a divine form and wishes to worship it with faith. Krishna says that he makes that faith firm. His power therefore operates even in the worship of other deities: the steadiness through which the devotee continues is given by him. The worshipper proceeds toward the chosen form, while Krishna supplies the faith supporting that worship.

## 7.22

### Meaning

Endowed with that faith, the person undertakes the worship of that form and obtains the desired things from it, though those benefits are ordained by me alone.

### Explanation

With faith made firm, the devotee worships the chosen form and receives the desired objects. Krishna says that these benefits are ordained by him alone. Both the devotee's faith and the result of the worship thus depend upon him. The person approaches another deity, but Krishna identifies himself as the ultimate giver of what that deity's worship obtains.

## 7.23

### Meaning

But the fruit of those people of small understanding is finite. Worshippers of the deities go to the deities; my devotees come to me.

### Explanation

Krishna calls these worshippers people of small understanding and their rewards finite. Their destination corresponds to their worship: those who worship gods reach those gods, while Krishna's devotees reach him. This follows his assertion that he alone ordains the benefits obtained through other deities. The passage distinguishes their limited results from attainment of Krishna himself.

## 7.24

### Meaning

Those without discernment think of me, the unmanifest, as having become manifest, failing to know my higher nature, imperishable and unsurpassed.

### Explanation

Krishna says that those without discernment regard the unmanifest as having become manifest. They do not know his higher nature, which he calls imperishable and unsurpassed. The distinction concerns the relation between his manifestation and his enduring being. Different traditions explain that relation through their accounts of divine form and embodiment; the following verse adds his concealment through yoga-māyā.

## 7.25

### Meaning

I am not revealed to everyone, being veiled by yoga-māyā. This deluded world does not recognise me as unborn and imperishable.

### Explanation

Recognition is not automatic simply because Krishna is present. Yoga-māyā describes the power through which his presence can remain concealed from ordinary understanding. The terms “unborn” and “imperishable” answer the mistaken account of manifestation in the previous verse. The passage holds together disclosure and concealment: Krishna speaks to make himself known, while explaining why the same reality is not equally evident to every observer.

## 7.26

### Meaning

I know the beings that have passed, those that exist now and those yet to come, Arjuna; but no one knows me.

### Explanation

Krishna claims knowledge of every being across past, present and future. Against this comprehensive knowledge he states that no one knows him. The contrast continues the preceding declaration that yoga-māyā conceals his unborn, imperishable nature from the deluded world. Knowledge runs from the Lord to all beings, while those beings do not recognise the one who knows them.

## 7.27

### Meaning

Through the delusion of opposites, born of desire and aversion, all beings enter confusion at their coming into existence, descendant of Bharata, scorcher of foes.

### Explanation

Desire and aversion produce the delusion of opposites. Through that delusion, all beings fall into confusion when they come into existence. Pleasure and pain, and what is desired and disliked, divide their experience. Krishna places this delusion at the beginning of embodied life; the next verse describes those whose meritorious action and freedom from wrongdoing allow them to escape it.

## 7.28

### Meaning

But people of meritorious action whose wrongdoing has come to an end, freed from the delusion of opposites, worship me with firm resolve.

### Explanation

Those who have performed meritorious actions and whose wrongdoing has ended are freed from the delusion of opposites. They then worship Krishna with firm vows. The verse directly connects the exhaustion of wrongdoing, release from delusion and steadfast worship. It supplies the counterpart to the wrongdoers described in 7.15, whose knowledge has been carried away by māyā and who do not take refuge in him.

## 7.29

### Meaning

Those who strive for freedom from old age and death, taking refuge in me, come to know Brahman, the whole reality of the self and action in its entirety.

### Explanation

The desire for liberation now brings several technical terms into view. Brahman, adhyātma and karma name dimensions of the reality the seeker must understand. The chapter presents refuge in Krishna as the basis of this comprehensive inquiry. Old age and death connect its goal to the vulnerability of embodied existence, while the breadth of the promised knowledge prepares Arjuna's request for more precise definitions in the next chapter.

## 7.30

### Meaning

Those who know me together with the domains of beings, divine powers and sacrifice know me, with an integrated mind, even at the time of departure.

### Explanation

Knowledge of Krishna includes adhibhūta, adhidaiva and adhiyajña: the domains of beings, divine powers and sacrifice. Those whose minds are integrated in this knowledge know him even at death. These terms, together with Brahman, adhyātma and karma in the preceding verse, lead to Arjuna's questions at the opening of Chapter 8, where Krishna defines them.
