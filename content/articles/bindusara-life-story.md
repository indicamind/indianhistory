## A future emperor with an uncertain beginning

Bindusara was Chandragupta Maurya’s son and Ashoka’s father. His birth date and birthplace are not securely established by contemporary evidence. The vivid account of his arrival comes instead from later religious storytelling, which makes a family catastrophe the explanation of a royal name. [1, 2]

## Durdhara, remembered through a tragedy

Hemachandra’s Jain narrative identifies Bindusara’s mother as **Durdhara**. It makes her pregnancy and death central to the dynasty’s future. That gives her an important place in the remembered family, but it does not provide a contemporary biography from which her full life can be recovered. [1, 2]

## The poisoned meal in the Jain account

The story says Chanakya added poison to Chandragupta’s food to protect him against poisoning. Durdhara shared a meal without that acquired resistance. The catastrophe follows from the secrecy surrounding the king’s protection. It is the mechanism of a later legend, not a medically verified episode. [1, 2]

## A child survives; his mother does not

Chanakya’s rescue of the unborn child supplies the legend’s terrible turning point. Saving the heir does not restore Durdhara. The narrative should be understood as an account transmitted by Jain authors centuries later, rather than as reliable documentation of ancient surgery or a procedure to reproduce. [1, 2]

## Why the story calls him Bindusara

In the Jain telling, a drop of poison touches the child’s head and becomes the explanation of his name. A Buddhist commentary preserves a different version. These competing stories show the appeal of explaining a ruler through a memorable beginning; neither turns the name into proof of the narrated event. [1–3]

## He inherited an empire already made

Bindusara’s accession is conventionally placed around **297 BCE**. He followed the founder of the Mauryan dynasty, rather than beginning from a small independent court. His central task was to sustain the ruling house and its extensive authority after Chandragupta, although the details of his administration survive unevenly. [1, 4]

## The court at the centre of the kingdom

Bindusara belonged to the Mauryan monarchy centred on **Pataliputra**, near modern Patna. The capital connected the royal household with officers and provinces. Archaeological remains of the broader Mauryan city help establish that setting, but a surviving pillar or hall cannot automatically be assigned to a particular act of his reign. [1]

## A reign without Ashoka’s surviving voice

Bindusara left us no comparable series of first-person edicts setting out his personal programme. That absence makes his government harder to describe than Ashoka’s. It does not justify treating his reign as empty, or transferring every later Mauryan institution and policy backwards into his personal biography. [1, 4]

## The rescued child becomes the minister’s judge

Hemachandra’s account continues into conflict involving Chanakya and **Subandhu**. Bindusara is made to confront an accusation about the circumstances of his mother’s death. The episode explores the danger of partial knowledge at court. It should not be presented as a contemporary transcript of a royal inquiry. [1, 2]

## The adviser’s fate belongs to that tradition

The tale moves through anger, explanation, withdrawal, and the destruction of Chanakya. Its moral tension depends on the gap between protecting a dynasty and winning its heir’s gratitude. The story is valuable as transmitted political memory, while its dramatic particulars remain far less secure than the existence of Bindusara himself. [1, 2]

## Princes remembered beyond the capital

Later accounts connect the young Ashoka with provincial responsibilities in **Taxila** and **Ujjain** before his accession. These traditions place Bindusara’s household within a kingdom requiring trusted representatives at a distance. They are later narratives of the prince’s career, not surviving appointment orders issued by his father. [1, 4, 6]

## How a Buddhist chronicle remembers his court

The **Mahavamsa** describes Bindusara providing hospitality to Brahmins, then contrasts that setting with Ashoka’s later Buddhist patronage. The passage reveals the chronicler’s religious perspective. It should not be converted into a complete account of Bindusara’s personal beliefs or a catalogue of every community supported during his reign. [4]

## The king called Amitrochates

An Indian king called **Amitrochates** in Greek testimony is generally identified with Bindusara. Different spellings and titles complicate the surviving accounts. The identification connects his court with Hellenistic rulers, while reminding us that names often reach historians through translators and later authors rather than through the king’s own words. [1, 5]

## Wine, figs, and a philosopher

Athenaeus, citing **Hegesander**, says Amitrochates asked Antiochus for sweet wine, dried figs, and a sophist. The anecdote offers a strikingly different image from the endangered child of the birth legend: an adult ruler participating in exchanges of prestigious goods and learned company across a wider political world. [1, 5]

## A memorable answer, not a full archive

The reported reply promises the wine and figs but says a sophist cannot be sold under Greek custom. The humour helps explain why the exchange survived in literature. It is not a complete diplomatic archive, and the conversation’s details should remain attributed to the source transmitting them. [5]

## A royal household with competing futures

Buddhist narratives make the rivalry among Bindusara’s sons central to Ashoka’s rise. Their speeches, preferences, and dramatic violence are not contemporary palace minutes. The secure dynastic relationship is simpler: Ashoka followed Bindusara, while the exact path through the succession must be reconstructed from sources with different purposes. [1, 4, 6]

## Even the chronology needs qualification

Bindusara’s death is commonly placed around **273–272 BCE**, although chronologies differ. The Mahavamsa gives him twenty-eight regnal years within its own chronological scheme. Those figures should not be forced together as if all dates came from the same contemporary calendar. No reliable deathbed scene survives. [1, 4]

## His son left a much louder historical record

Ashoka’s inscriptions made the following reign unusually visible. That difference in evidence can cause Bindusara to disappear between a famous founder and a famous reformer. He remains the dynastic bridge between them, even where the surviving sources cannot recover his decisions with comparable precision. [1, 4]

## The king and the story must stay distinct

The poison-drop legend explains why Bindusara’s name remained memorable. His reign, succession, family, and contact with a wider political world establish a historical person beyond it. A responsible life brings those strands together without turning the legend into medical fact or pretending the missing administrative record has been found. [1–6]

## Sources

1. **The approved family-history article:** [Born from Poison](/article/bindusara-name-legend-poison-drop). Its complete source notes distinguish the Jain birth story, Buddhist alternatives, political context, and the Greek anecdote.

2. **Hemachandra, Parishishtaparvan, Canto VIII, verses 436–469**, [Jacobi’s edition and English synopsis](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Sthaviravali_Charitra_or_Parisista_Parva_Romanized/010713). A twelfth-century Jain narrative, not a contemporary record of Bindusara’s birth or court.

3. **Mahavamsa commentary tradition**, discussed in [Malalasekera’s “Bindusara” entry](https://www.palikanon.com/english/pali_names/b/bindusara.htm). The commentary’s alternative naming story is distinguished from the chronicle’s succession notice.

4. **Mahavamsa, chapter V**, [Geiger’s translation](https://vipassana.org/resources/mahavamsa/mhv05.html). Dynastic sequence, the stated reign length, and the chronicle’s account of religious patronage; its absolute chronology is not silently harmonised with other reconstructions.

5. **Athenaeus, Deipnosophists XIV.67, 652f–653a**, [Yonge’s translation](https://www.attalus.org/old/athenaeus14d.html). The exchange about wine, figs, and a sophist, attributed there to Hegesander; it is a transmitted literary anecdote.

6. **Later Buddhist traditions and their source problems**, compared in [Bram Fauconnier’s research article](https://histos.org/index.php/histos/article/download/288/282/291) with the northern narratives collected in [Vincent Smith’s public-domain chapter on Ashoka’s legends](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Asoka_-_the_Buddhist_Emperor_of_India/Chapter_7) and [Mahavamsa XIII](https://mahavamsa.org/mahavamsa/original-version/13-coming-mahinda/). These later texts supply the Taxila and Ujjain traditions; they are not contemporary appointment records.
