**The sultan's court offered an old Brahmin a choice: abandon his faith or face the fire. According to the man who recorded the execution, he refused.**

His name does not appear in the account. Neither does the date of his death. What survives is a charge, a royal consultation, an ultimatum, and a refusal that the power of the court could not overcome.

The chronicler **Shams-i Siraj Afif** describes an elderly Brahmin in Delhi who held worship in his house. Hindus and Muslims reportedly came there. An accusation reached Sultan **Firuz Shah Tughlaq** that the Brahmin had drawn Muslim women away from their religion. The sultan summoned him, together with a painted wooden object used in his worship. A gathering of religious and legal authorities declared that he should convert or be burned. He would not convert. <sup>[1]</sup>

The account ends with the Brahmin's execution before the darbar—and with praise for the ruler who ordered it.

The brutality comes down to us through a writer who presents the act as an achievement of righteous kingship. His approval is part of the evidence.

The ruler in this account is **Firuz Shah Tughlaq, who reigned from 1351 to 1388**. He belongs to the Tughlaq dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate. The episode should not be transferred to Muhammad bin Tughlaq, his predecessor, or to a Mughal emperor from a later century. <sup>[2][3]</sup>

Firuz's reign left an extensive architectural and administrative record. His citadel at Firozabad survives in the ruins known as Firoz Shah Kotla. His own account of his rule describes building, repairs, endowments, a hospital, tax reforms, and the enforcement of religious restrictions. These activities belonged to the same government. <sup>[3][4]</sup>

To understand the Brahmin's death, we must enter the political world in which a ruler's religious authority could reach into a subject's house, determine which gatherings were permissible, and make a person's continued life conditional upon changing his faith.

## A witness who admired the execution

The principal narrative is in Afif's ***Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi***. In the English extracts published by H. M. Elliot and John Dowson in 1871, the episode appears under the heading **“Burning of a Brahman before the Royal Palace.”** Afif expressly states that he was present at the darbar and witnessed the execution. This is an eyewitness claim made within a historical chronicle. <sup>[1]</sup>

Afif came from a family connected with the Tughlaq court. The *Encyclopaedia Iranica* describes his strong attachment to Firuz and observes that his account can turn the ruler's shortcomings into virtues. That matters here: the narrator does not hide his admiration for the decision. <sup>[2]</sup>

A court-connected witness can preserve information that otherwise disappears. He can also select events, accept official accusations, and frame coercion as justice. We therefore need to distinguish the event he says he witnessed from the allegations that brought the Brahmin before the court.

The account is Afif's. Another important historian, **Ziauddin Barani**, wrote a work with the same title, *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi*. Confusing the two authors can make a single report look like multiple independent testimonies. The execution narrative discussed here is the passage attributed to Afif in Elliot and Dowson's collection. <sup>[1]</sup>

## The house that drew worshippers across a boundary

Afif describes the Brahmin as old and as a wearer of the sacred thread. He says that the man continued to conduct image worship in his house, and that people of both religious communities came there to worship. The account also describes a wooden tablet, called a *muhrak* in the translation, covered with painted figures. <sup>[1]</sup>

The setting is important. This is a house where a religious practice had acquired a following. The chronicle treats what happened there as sufficiently public to demand intervention, even while saying that the gatherings had escaped the notice of officials.

The painted object receives its description through a hostile religious vocabulary. The English translation calls its figures demons and other objects. That wording reflects the account's judgement of the worship; it does not establish the identity of the deity or deities depicted. The passage supplies no secure basis for naming them.

What alarmed the authorities, in the story Afif tells, was the crossing of a religious boundary. Muslim participation at the house made its worship a question for the sultan's court. The accusation concerning women sharpened the charge: the Brahmin was said to have brought them out of Islam.

## Was he accused of preaching?

The incident is often remembered as a Brahmin being killed for preaching Hinduism. Afif's description is more specific. It alleges public worship in the man's house, attendance by Hindus and Muslims, and the religious influence he supposedly exercised over Muslim women. <sup>[1]</sup>

The passage does not preserve a sermon, a debate, the names of the women, or their testimony. It does not tell us how the allegation was investigated. We cannot determine from this account whether the women formally changed their religious identity, joined particular acts of worship, or were described as converts by those who denounced the gathering.

These limits do not make the reported punishment less grave. They identify what the surviving narrative actually gives us: a man brought before the ruler because of his worship and alleged influence on Muslim participants, then threatened with death unless he accepted Islam.

**The charge reached the court as a report about religion. The sentence made religion a condition of survival.**

## The court's ultimatum: conversion or death

Firuz ordered the Brahmin and the painted tablet brought before him at **Firozabad**. Afif says that judges, religious scholars, elders, and lawyers were assembled to give an opinion. The word “doctors” in the older English translation refers in this setting to learned religious authorities. <sup>[1]</sup>

The gathering's conclusion, as translated, was that the Brahmin must **“either become a Musulman or be burned.”** The account says that the faith was explained to him and that he refused to accept it. <sup>[1]</sup>

This was a demand backed by the power to kill. The man was not being offered a religious conversation between equals. His refusal was followed by an order to prepare a fire before the door of the darbar.

The chronicle's assertion that the decision followed the law tells us how this court justified the act. It should not be expanded into a claim that every Muslim ruler, jurist, or community applied an identical punishment in comparable circumstances. Here, a particular sultan and an assembled body of authorities are described as endorsing this particular coercive sentence.

## A refusal that survived the court's account

Afif gives the Brahmin no extended speech. There is no recorded declaration to reproduce, no final message to his followers, and no reliable portrait of his thoughts. His refusal is brief in the text, but it is the act on which everything turns.

Faced with conversion or burning, he did not accept conversion.

That is the courage the account permits us to recognise. We need not invent a defiant speech to give the refusal weight. Nor must we turn the man into a political rebel to explain why he matters. In the narrative, the authorities can summon him, judge him, restrain him, and kill him. They fail to obtain the profession of faith they demand.

His name is absent. His refusal remains.

## The fire before the darbar

The account describes a pile of dry wood prepared outside the court. The Brahmin was bound hand and foot and placed on it. The painted tablet was thrown onto the pile, and the fire was lit. Afif then describes the flames consuming him and repeats that he witnessed the execution. <sup>[1]</sup>

The killing was accompanied by the destruction of an object associated with his worship. In the sequence the chronicler presents, both the worshipper and the material focus of the prohibited practice were delivered to the fire.

Afif places the event at the royal darbar in Firozabad. He does not provide the Brahmin's personal name, an exact date, or enough topographical detail to identify a particular surviving doorway as the site. The preceding section of the translated chronicle mentions an event dated 1375; that date should not simply be carried over to this separate episode.

The restraint needed in reconstructing the setting does not obscure the central report: Afif describes an execution by burning after a refusal to convert, ordered through the sultan's court.

## The chronicler's praise is part of the horror

Afif closes by commending Firuz's adherence to religious law and rectitude. The judgement asks the reader to admire the ruler for carrying the sentence through. <sup>[1]</sup>

This is why the passage deserves close reading. The execution is preserved inside an account of good government as the chronicler understood it. The same record that exposes the persecution also supplies the language used to approve it.

A reader can recognise that framing without adopting it. The historical task is to recover the machinery of the persecution: the report, the summons, the consultation, the ultimatum, the refusal, and the killing. Each stage made a religious accusation more dangerous to the accused.

Calling the act persecution follows from that sequence. In the account itself, a man's worship and alleged religious influence brought him before the ruler, and acceptance of a different faith was presented as the way to escape death.

## Jizya and the Brahmins who resisted together

Afif places a separate account of **jizya imposed on Brahmins** immediately after the execution narrative. Jizya was a poll tax levied on non-Muslim subjects. According to Afif, Brahmins had previously enjoyed exemption, and Firuz decided to end it. <sup>[5]</sup>

The sultan's reasoning, as the chronicler presents it, singled out their religious authority. Brahmins guided the worship of the wider Hindu population; that role became an argument for taxing them first. Learned authorities supported the decision.

Brahmins from the cities of Delhi assembled at the **Kushk-i Shikar**, where construction work for the sultan was underway, and protested. Afif records a threat to burn themselves rather than submit to the tax, followed by several days of fasting. The sultan refused to restore their exemption. Other Hindus of the city then offered to meet the payment for them. The account ends with a petition concerning the amount and a specified assessment. <sup>[5]</sup>

The story preserves resistance and solidarity as well as coercion. The protesters challenged a policy aimed at their community. Other residents intervened to prevent their deaths and share the financial burden.

This is a distinct episode. The unnamed Brahmin executed for refusing conversion should not be turned into the leader of the tax protest, and the protesters' threatened self-burning should not be confused with the execution ordered by the court. Their proximity in the chronicle does, however, reveal how religious authority and royal power could collide in more than one way.

## The sultan's own record: temples, images, and books

A second primary work widens the picture. The ***Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi***, attributed to Firuz himself, presents actions he wished remembered as accomplishments of his reign. It supplies direct evidence of the religious policy the ruler claimed to enforce. It does not independently retell the unnamed Brahmin's execution. <sup>[3]</sup>

In its numbered sections 11–13, Firuz describes action against newly erected Hindu temples and religious gatherings. These restrictions were presented as obligations under the religious law he upheld.

At **Maluh**, the account describes a tank or *kund*, temples, and a gathering attended by large numbers of worshippers. Women and children came; market sellers set up stalls; some Muslims also took part. Firuz says that he went there, ordered the leaders killed, destroyed the temples, and raised mosques in their place. He also states that he prohibited severe punishment of the Hindus generally at that gathering. That claimed distinction between leaders and other participants belongs in the account alongside the executions and destruction. <sup>[3]</sup>

At **Salihpur**, he records sending men to destroy a new temple and stop the worship. At **Kohana**, he says that people associated with a newly erected temple were brought before him, that the leaders were to be executed before the palace gate, and that seized religious books, images, and worship vessels were to be publicly burned. Others were subjected to threats and punishments. <sup>[3]</sup>

These are the ruler's statements. We should retain the place names given in the translation without pretending that the brief notices establish every site's precise modern location or every victim's identity. The Kohana passage is also separate from Afif's account of the old Brahmin. Similar settings and the burning of worship objects do not make the two reports the same event.

Together, the passages show several instruments of persecution: interference with gatherings, destruction of places and objects of worship, punishment of religious leaders, and exemplary public violence.

## Conversion could remove a tax—and a threat could demand it

Elsewhere in the *Futuhat*, Firuz says that he encouraged Hindus to accept Islam by announcing exemption from jizya for converts. He also describes giving presents and honours. He claims that many came forward; his statement is a ruler's account of success, without figures that would allow us to measure the scale. <sup>[3]</sup>

This policy attached a material advantage to a religious change. Set beside Afif's jizya narrative, it shows why taxation belongs in a discussion of religious pressure. The government could withdraw an exemption from one group while presenting conversion as a route out of the tax.

The Brahmin's reported ultimatum went further: it placed life itself at stake. A financial incentive and a threat of execution are different forms of pressure. Keeping them distinct helps us see the range of powers available to the state.

Firuz's account also describes repression of religious opponents within Islam, including Shia teaching and books. That broader enforcement of orthodoxy helps explain the government's methods. The specific targeting of Hindu worship and Brahmin religious authority remains visible within it. <sup>[3]</sup>

At Firoz Shah Kotla, an ancient Ashokan pillar still stands on the structure Firuz had built for it. The monument is a striking reminder of the labour, organisation, and ambition his government could command. Its survival makes royal achievement tangible. <sup>[4]</sup>

The Brahmin has left a different kind of trace: a short passage in a chronicle, preserved through manuscripts and translation. There is no name in that passage to put beside the imposing remains of the ruler's capital.

Firuz's own work praises mercy and records his rejection of tortures used under earlier rulers. It also describes a hospital supported by endowments and repairs to older buildings. Those claims must be read alongside his accounts of executions, suppression, and the destruction of worship objects. The same text contains both. <sup>[3]</sup>

Public works and persecution are parts of this reign's surviving record. Understanding its administration does not require us to make the victim disappear from its history.

## What the record lets us remember

The strongest account of this incident is already powerful without an invented name, an unsupported date, a fabricated sermon, or a modern reconstruction of the Brahmin's last words.

Afif says an elderly Brahmin attracted worshippers of different communities, was accused of drawing Muslim women into his religion, refused the conversion demanded by the court, and was burned on the sultan's orders. Afif claims to have watched. He praises the decision. <sup>[1]</sup>

The accusation reaches us through the official side of the encounter. The Brahmin's own explanation and the women's voices are absent. That absence limits what we can know about the gatherings. It also reminds us how unequal the struggle over memory can be: the court's admirer gets to describe the man whom the court kills.

Yet the victim has not vanished entirely. In the record intended to honour the ruler, his refusal still interrupts the story of righteous power.

**The sultan could order the fire. He could not obtain the old man's consent. Across the centuries, that refusal remains the part of the account the flames could not consume.**
