**Chittor fell. Mewar rose again.**

Stand beneath the Vijay Stambha and look up. Nine storeys of carved stone climb into the Rajasthan sky. This is Rana Kumbha's answer to victory: a monument built to carry the memory of his triumphs beyond the battlefield. The army of a sultan could be defeated. A kingdom could turn its success into something that would outlast its warriors.

Yet the fortress around the tower tells a harsher story. Chittor was stormed, its defenders overwhelmed, its people subjected to terrible violence. Mewar's greatness lay in the courage to recover after such catastrophes as much as in the splendour of its victories.

Across centuries, that struggle took different forms: Kumbha's campaigns against Malwa, Jaimal and Patta's defence against Akbar, Maharana Pratap's tenacious war from the hills, and the harder bargains made by later rulers. Maratha demands and British supremacy would narrow the kingdom's freedom. Independent India would finally replace princely government with a different political ideal: citizenship. **Mewar's story is a long fight to endure—and a reminder that survival itself could demand extraordinary courage.**

## The land that made resistance possible

Mewar lay around Chittor and Udaipur in southern Rajasthan. Its ruling traditions developed around the Guhila dynasty and the Sisodia branch that later held the kingdom. Chittorgarh was its mighty fortified capital. Beyond it stretched the Aravallis: hills, passes, and strongholds that gave a battered army somewhere to withdraw, regroup, and strike again.

The strength behind those walls came from more than royal swords. Reservoirs sustained settlements; farmers fed armies; merchants, craftspeople, village communities, and local chiefs kept the country functioning. The forts were living centres of worship, trade, and government. UNESCO's account of Rajasthan's hill forts describes this interdependence of landscape, water, towns, and courtly culture. **A king could lose a fortress and still fight on if the country behind him held together.** <sup>[1]</sup>

## 1303: Chittor falls—and a dynasty rises again

In **1303**, Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi besieged and captured Chittor during the reign of Rawal Ratan Singh. The fortress succumbed to an expanding imperial power. Its strategic importance placed it directly in the path of Delhi's ambitions.

The familiar story that Alauddin attacked to obtain Queen Padmini comes from a much later literary tradition. Malik Muhammad Jayasi composed *Padmavat* in **1540**, more than two centuries after the siege.

Conquest proved easier than permanent possession. During the fourteenth century, **Hammir Singh** of the Sisodia branch restored a local ruling power at Chittor. From a fortress lost to Delhi, another age of Mewar's power emerged. <sup>[3]</sup>

## Kumbha's triumph: a sultan defeated, a victory raised to the sky

Under **Rana Kumbha, who ruled from 1433 to 1468**, Mewar became a formidable regional power. Here was a ruler who could take the struggle beyond the defence of his own walls. Among his great opponents was **Mahmud Khalji I of Malwa**, ruling from Mandu.

The shared name can obscure the history. Kumbha's defeated Khalji opponent was a different sultan from the Alauddin who had captured Chittor more than a century earlier:

| Ruler | Connection with Mewar |
| --- | --- |
| Alauddin Khalji of Delhi | Captured Chittor in 1303. |
| Mahmud Khalji I of Malwa | Fought Rana Kumbha in the fifteenth century and suffered a celebrated defeat. |

Kumbha's celebrated victory associated with **Sarangpur is commonly dated to 1437**. Mewar's historical tradition describes Mahmud being taken prisoner and subsequently released: a dramatic reversal for a sultan who had challenged the kingdom. The rivalry continued, and chroniclers sometimes disagreed over later campaigns. But Kumbha had established that Mewar could inflict a major defeat on a powerful neighbour. <sup>[4, 5]</sup>

At Chittor, that age of confidence took its most arresting form: **the Vijay Stambha, the Tower of Victory**. Nine storeys of deities, sculpture, and inscriptions proclaim the achievements of Kumbha's reign. The craftsmanship deserves its own share of the glory. Generations remember the king; the tower also bears witness to the skill of the people who carved his triumph into stone.

The monument belongs to the **middle of the fifteenth century, well before Akbar**. Published dates differ: Rajasthan Tourism gives 1440–1448; Har Bilas Sarda's older study describes work from 1448 to 1458. Its attribution to Kumbha and association with victory over Malwa are clear. The older Jain **Kirti Stambha** at Chittor is a separate tower. <sup>[6, 5]</sup>

Kumbha's ambition reached beyond a single monument. **Kumbhalgarh**, associated with the architect Mandan at his court, became one of Mewar's great fortified refuges. Military strength, architecture, religious patronage, and scholarship flourished together. His reign gave Mewar both a shield and a magnificent public statement of its power. <sup>[1]</sup>

The wars also reached Mewar's sacred images. **Firishta's chronicle**, written in the early seventeenth century, describes Mahmud Khalji's forces burning a temple in the **Kumbhalgarh district**, then throwing cold water over its heated stone images to break them. In John Briggs's translation, the fragments were handed to the army's butchers for use as weights. **Har Bilas Sarda's later account names the Bana Mata temple at Kelwara**, its defender Dip Singh, and a destruction dated to **1442**. These accounts differ in detail, but both record violence against the shrine and its images. <sup>[18, 5]</sup>

## Sanga's great gamble—and Chittor's second catastrophe

In the early sixteenth century, **Rana Sanga** carried Mewar's ambitions onto a still larger stage. His campaigns against the rulers of Delhi and Malwa made him a serious contender in northern Indian politics.

Then Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi in 1526 and established Mughal power. Sanga led a coalition against this new force, staking his wider ambitions on a tremendous confrontation. At **Khanwa in March 1527**, Babur prevailed. Mewar survived, but the defeat checked its bid for greater power. Sanga's courage cannot erase the outcome; the scale of his challenge remains part of his stature. <sup>[3]</sup>

Internal struggles weakened the ruling house. In **1535, Bahadur Shah of Gujarat captured and sacked Chittor**: the second of its three best-known sieges, after 1303. The memory of these catastrophes became inseparable from *jauhar*, the mass self-immolation of women facing capture, and the defenders' final fighting. These were desperate human tragedies. Any tribute to the defenders must also leave room for the grief of those whose lives ended inside the fort. <sup>[6]</sup>

## 1568: Jaimal, Patta, and Chittor's terrible last stand

Udai Singh II founded Udaipur in **1559**, before Akbar's capture of Chittor. Its location in the Girwa valley provided another centre for the ruling house. The familiar sequence in which Chittor fell and only then Udaipur was founded compresses two separate developments. <sup>[7]</sup>

When **Akbar began his great siege in 1567**, Chittor faced the concentrated resources of a growing empire. Udai Singh withdrew into the hills. Within the fortress, commanders including **Jaimal and Patta** led its defence.

Their stand became one of Mewar's most enduring memories of valour. Mughal artillery, mining, and siegeworks ground down the stronghold. The defenders could prolong the struggle; they could not ultimately prevent the breach. **Chittor fell in February 1568.**

The victory was followed by a massacre. Accounts describe the deaths of defenders and large numbers of civilians, alongside the jauhar associated with the fall. Exact casualty totals vary; no precise figure should be treated as a secure count. The human cost was appalling. Yet even after this devastating victory, Akbar had not captured Udai Singh or extinguished the ruling house. **The fort had fallen. The struggle for Mewar remained unfinished.** <sup>[8]</sup>

### The victory proclamation: jihad in the imperial record

A proclamation issued in Akbar's name from **Ajmer on 9 March 1568**, the *Fathnama-i Chittor*, casts the conquest in explicitly religious terms. In historian Taymiya R. Zaman's translation, it declares: **“we are engaged to the best of our abilities in jihad”**. It celebrates taking forts from those it calls unbelievers and raising Islam's banners over them, while linking conquest to the eradication of idol worship. This was the religious justification of an imperial victory declaration, issued before Akbar's later policy of *sulh-i kul*, or universal peace. <sup>[19]</sup>

## Pratap at Haldighati: a lost field, an unfinished war

**Maharana Pratap succeeded in 1572**, inheriting a kingdom wounded by conquest. He refused the terms of submission sought by Akbar. In **June 1576**, the armies met at Haldighati. The imperial force was commanded by **Raja Man Singh of Amber**, not by Akbar personally.

The Mughal army won the immediate battlefield encounter. Pratap escaped, and his resistance continued. **His achievement was to survive defeat and make the imperial victory insufficient.** Holding Haldighati did not give Akbar lasting control over all Mewar.

From the hills, Pratap's forces kept the struggle alive, sustained by local support, including **Bhil communities**. Their contribution belongs at the heart of this history. The resistance depended on people who knew and inhabited the country as well as on the ruler who became its emblem. Man Singh's command of the imperial army also reminds us that political loyalties crossed the religious divisions later storytelling often imposes. <sup>[9]</sup>

The years after Haldighati reveal the full measure of Pratap's tenacity. During the **1580s and 1590s**, he recovered substantial territory and established a base at **Chavand**. **Chittor and Mandalgarh remained outside his recovery.** That limit matters, but so does what he rebuilt: an independent centre of power after years of imperial campaigning.

Reclaiming land meant more than attacking garrisons. War had damaged villages and cultivation; recovery required land grants, revenue, and administration. Pratap's endurance mattered because it made rebuilding possible. He died in **1597 without submitting to Akbar**, leaving a legacy grounded in persistence after adversity. <sup>[3]</sup>

## After Akbar: the peace that preserved a battered kingdom

Akbar died in **1605**. The pressure on Mewar outlived him. Under Jahangir, Mughal campaigns against Pratap's son **Amar Singh I** continued. Prince Khurram, the future Shah Jahan, intensified the military effort. Years of warfare exhausted the countryside that had sustained resistance.

In **1615, Amar Singh accepted Mughal supremacy**. Mewar retained hereditary rule, and the Rana was excused from personally attending the imperial court; his son Karan Singh represented the dynasty there. Chittor was restored with a restriction on repairing or refortifying it. Independence had been curtailed, but the ruling house survived and prolonged war gave way to a settlement. The story of resistance includes this painful choice to preserve a ravaged kingdom. <sup>[10, 3]</sup>

The following decades included cooperation, court service, cultural exchange, and disputes. War returned under **Rana Raj Singh and Aurangzeb in 1679–1681**. Even writings produced at the Mewar court could portray the emperor differently as relations changed. Historian Cynthia Talbot's comparison of these texts shows why the entire seventeenth century cannot be reduced to one continuous, unchanging confrontation. <sup>[11]</sup>

### 1680: the assault on temples and sacred images

During **Aurangzeb's invasion in 1680**, Mewar's shrines became targets. Saqi Musta'id Khan's Mughal chronicle, the *Ma'asir-i-Alamgiri*, describes an attack on the **great temple before the Rana's palace at Udaipur**. It records twenty Rajput defenders fighting until all were killed, then states, in Jadunath Sarkar's translation: **“The hewers broke the images.”** <sup>[20]</sup>

Further entries record Aurangzeb's order to demolish **three temples beside Udaisagar on 24 January 1680**; Hasan Ali Khan's report on **29 January** that **172 other temples around Udaipur** had been destroyed; and **63 temples demolished at Chittor by imperial order on 22 February**. These are the chronicle's reported totals. The invasion struck places of worship and the sacred images within them, alongside the kingdom's military strongholds. <sup>[20]</sup>

## The Marathas: new powers, mounting demands

As Mughal authority weakened in the eighteenth century, Mewar faced a fresh struggle for security. Maratha expansion into Malwa brought forces associated with the **Peshwa, Holkar, and Scindia** into Rajasthan. Regional rulers' appeals for outside help in their own disputes opened further routes for intervention.

In **1736**, Peshwa Baji Rao I visited Udaipur, and Maharana Jagat Singh II agreed to tribute. Further demands, military intervention, and disruption followed in later decades. Contests between Mewar's rulers and nobles compounded the pressure. The court still stood, but payments to outside armies and struggles over revenue sapped the state's strength. A dynasty with centuries of victories behind it now faced the relentless arithmetic of survival. <sup>[12]</sup>

The dynasty continued to rule from Udaipur, but its survival increasingly depended on dealing with stronger powers. By the early nineteenth century, that search for security led to a treaty with the East India Company.

## 1818: the heavy price of British protection

On **13 January 1818**, Maharana Bhim Singh's government concluded a treaty with the **East India Company**. Protection came at a heavy political price: Mewar acknowledged British supremacy, accepted restrictions on relations with other states, and undertook financial and military obligations.

The treaty preserved hereditary internal government within a larger imperial order. Udaipur became a **princely state under British paramountcy**; it was not converted into a directly administered British province. The dynasty survived, but substantial powers over external affairs lay elsewhere, and tribute added a financial burden. The old ruling house had secured its continuation by accepting another superior power. <sup>[13]</sup>

By the twentieth century, the demand for freedom also came from **the people governed by the princes**. Movements for representation and responsible government challenged princely authority. Mewar's **Praja Mandal** participated in demands for reform; the Rajasthan Assembly records constitutional negotiations and changes during 1946–1947. The courage to demand a voice in government belongs beside the older stories of military endurance. Democracy required struggles within princely states as well as the ending of British rule. <sup>[14]</sup>

## From the Maharana's state to the people's Rajasthan

With the end of British paramountcy in **1947**, Udaipur acceded to India. Accession and the subsequent merger of princely administrations were separate stages. The Government of India's *White Paper on Indian States* records Udaipur among the states whose rulers had executed accession instruments in August 1947. <sup>[15]</sup>

Under Maharana **Bhupal Singh**, Udaipur joined the reconstituted **United State of Rajasthan on 18 April 1948**. Jawaharlal Nehru inaugurated the union at Udaipur. Bhupal Singh became its Rajpramukh, a constitutional office in the new arrangement. <sup>[16]</sup>

The formation of **Greater Rajasthan on 30 March 1949**, followed by further territorial integration, brought the former princely territories into a larger state. Mewar's separate administration disappeared through this process. In **1971**, the Twenty-sixth Constitutional Amendment ended official recognition of former rulers and abolished privy purses. Dynastic descent continued, but hereditary rulership no longer supplied a constitutional authority to govern. <sup>[14, 17]</sup>

Mewar endures today as a historical and cultural region within Rajasthan. The kingdom has passed into history. Its places remain: Chittor's walls, Kumbhalgarh's defences, Udaipur's palaces, and the extraordinary tower Kumbha raised to victory.

**Look up at Vijay Stambha once more.** Its stonework recalls an age when Mewar defeated a sultan and proclaimed its strength to the sky. Around it lies the memory of a people who suffered conquest, sustained resistance, rebuilt shattered lives, and eventually entered a democratic state. The valour of Mewar belongs to that whole history: the triumphs, the last stands, the recoveries, and the resolve to begin again.
