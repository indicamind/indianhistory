## A Sisodia commander in a difficult record

**Patta**, also rendered **Pata** or **Fatta**, belonged to the Sisodia world of Mewar’s service and defence. His fame rests on Chittor in **1567–1568**. Exact childhood details and a precise age are less secure than that public role in the principal sources used for this account. [1–3]

## A young defender in the remembered story

Later accounts emphasise Patta’s youth and the participation of his household in the crisis. Those traditions help explain his lasting appeal. Their detailed scenes should not be treated as a contemporary diary, or used to supply an unquestionable birth date that the principal imperial account does not provide. [3, 4]

## He served the Rana rather than replacing him

Patta’s defence took place during **Udai Singh II’s reign**. The Rana withdrew into the hills, while commanders including Patta and Jaimal remained at Chittor. The dynasty’s wider survival and the garrison’s immediate danger were linked, but they did not place every decision in the same person’s hands. [1, 2]

## His story overlaps Jaimal’s without being identical

Patta and **Jaimal** became closely associated in the memory of the siege. They were different commanders from different clan backgrounds. The imperial narrative’s claim about Akbar’s shot concerns Jaimal; its specific reference to Patta concerns a household within the fort. Their biographies should preserve those distinctions. [1–3]

## A prolonged defence against imperial resources

The siege involved artillery, mining, batteries, and protected approaches. Holding the fort required repeated responses to damage and pressure. Patta’s remembered courage belonged within that collective work, carried by soldiers and inhabitants as well as the commanders whose names later became most familiar. [1, 2]

## A commander’s household shared the danger

The fort was an inhabited place whose households could not be separated neatly from the military crisis. The sources preserve only fragments of their experiences. Patta’s association with a named household in the imperial chronicle makes the human setting more concrete without recovering every person’s decisions or circumstances. [1, 2]

## The fall of another commander changed the situation

Abu’l Fazl connects Jaimal’s shooting with the weakening of the defence before the final assault. Patta’s role continued within the same catastrophe. The sources do not allow every hour to be assigned confidently to him, nor every later story to be joined into one fully verified account. [1–3]

## The chronicle places a jauhar fire in his house

The **Akbarnama** explicitly names the house of **Pata of the Sisodia clan** among the sites of the final night’s **jauhar**. Its description includes coercion. This is evidence of a catastrophe within households, not a reliable basis for celebrating every death as an individually documented voluntary choice. [2]

## Patta died in the defence’s final phase

Patta is remembered among those who died defending Chittor as imperial forces took the fortress. Later accounts give particular forms to his final fighting. The secure outline is the loss of a commander within a defeated defence; exact reported movements and last words require attribution to the narratives preserving them. [1–3]

## Victory brought killings beyond the named defenders

The conquest was followed by mass killing, with totals that cannot be treated as a secure modern count. The suffering extended beyond Patta and Jaimal. Mewar’s ruling house survived elsewhere, but that later continuity did not reverse what happened to the people inside the captured fortress. [1, 2]

## The pair became emblems of resistance

Patta and Jaimal remain central to Chittor’s heroic memory. That remembrance is itself part of history, but it is not identical with contemporary evidence. The surviving monuments and modern profile photograph evoke the place of their defence; they do not show Patta’s verified personal appearance. [1, 3, 4]

## A brief record can still preserve a consequential role

Patta’s recoverable life is concentrated around a major siege. The limited earlier record should not be padded with invented achievements. What survives is enough to identify a commander who helped defend Mewar’s principal fortress, died in its fall, and became inseparable from the memory of that resistance. [1–4]
