## An heir after the age of Pratap

**Raj Singh I**, a son of **Jagat Singh I**, belonged to the later Sisodia ruling house of Mewar. He was not Maharana Pratap or the ruler who first agreed to the 1615 settlement. His political inheritance already included decades of dealings with the Mughal court. [1, 2]

## He became Rana within an imperial order

Raj Singh succeeded in **1652**. Mewar retained its hereditary government while acknowledging the relationship established after the long war. The reign therefore began inside an existing political arrangement, not as an entirely independent kingdom whose first encounter with Mughal demands occurred only under Aurangzeb. [1, 2, 3]

## Repairing the old fortress provoked a dispute

Attempts to repair Chittor raised the restrictions connected with the **1615** settlement. The Rajasthan textbook describes Mughal intervention against the repairs during Shah Jahan’s reign. The dispute shows why the return of the fortress had not restored an unrestricted right to fortify it. [2, 3]

## The relationship changed across the reign

Cooperation, negotiation, dynastic interests, and conflict all belonged to Mewar’s dealings with the empire. The court’s own writings did not always portray Aurangzeb identically. Treating the entire reign as one uninterrupted war erases changes that help explain the much more serious confrontation at its end. [1, 4]

## A ruler’s work could be a reservoir

Raj Singh’s reign is associated with major water works. Such projects connected court resources with cultivation and settlement, even while inscriptions celebrated the patron. Their importance is not only the later beauty of the lake: water storage formed part of the country’s material security. [1, 5, 6]

## The lake that carried his name

The construction of **Rajsamand** is conventionally placed between **1662 and 1676**. A dam and worked embankment transformed the landscape. The surviving ghats and pavilions connect the reservoir with an elaborate courtly statement, making the work a source of royal reputation as well as water. [5, 6]

## The court placed its history on stone

The **Rajprashasti** inscriptions at Rajsamand praise Raj Singh and recount Mewar’s history. They are important courtly evidence, not a neutral account from outside the monarchy. Their public setting brought genealogy, patronage, and political claims into a durable landscape readers can still encounter. [4–6]

## Images found refuge within Mewar

The reign is remembered for providing refuge to Vaishnava images and their custodians moving from the Mathura region. Their establishment in Mewar became part of its religious landscape. The broad movement is significant, while the exact dates and details of each shrine require the specific tradition and record concerned. [2, 6]

## The conflict was not confined to Mewar

The crisis in neighbouring **Marwar** and the struggle over its ruling succession affected the wider region. Raj Singh’s confrontation with Aurangzeb developed within those dynastic and military pressures. Religious policy mattered too, but it was not the only force shaping the changing relationship between the courts. [1, 4]

## The return of jizya sharpened opposition

Aurangzeb reimposed **jizya** in **1679**. Raj Singh became associated with opposition to the policy in later historical accounts. The tax belonged to a larger contest over authority and religious obligation; a biography should not treat it as the sole cause explaining every military decision that followed. [1, 2, 4]

## The imperial army entered the kingdom

War brought Mughal forces into Mewar. Raj Singh’s government used the hill country and withdrew from positions it could not securely defend. Such movement preserved capacity to fight, while exposing towns, shrines, and inhabitants to the consequences of an invading army’s operations. [1, 2, 7]

## The palace temple appears in the chronicle

The **Ma’asir-i-Alamgiri** records an attack on the great temple before the Rana’s palace at Udaipur. Its account includes the killing of defenders and breaking of images. This is an imperial chronicle preserving destructive acts as part of the campaign, not a later invention added solely by opponents. [1, 7]

## Further temple destruction was reported

The chronicle reports orders and large totals for temple destruction at Udaisagar, around Udaipur, and at Chittor. The detailed article sets out the dated entries. These are the source’s reported figures, not a modern archaeological count; the violence against places of worship nevertheless belongs explicitly to its narrative. [1, 7]

## Resistance depended on more than a fortress

Mewar’s forces continued acting from difficult country despite the devastation of urban and sacred sites. The terrain could complicate the invader’s operations without shielding every settlement. The military history and the suffering of inhabitants should be read together rather than treating survival in the hills as proof that no damage mattered. [1, 2, 4]

## He died before the conflict was settled

Raj Singh died in **1680**. His son **Jai Singh** inherited the continuing confrontation, and the subsequent **1681** settlement belonged to that next reign. The distinction matters: Raj Singh did not personally make every later agreement attached to the war begun during his rule. [1, 2]

## The sources preserve different political moments

The Rajprashasti, court poetry, Mughal chronicle, and later histories record different purposes and moments. A praise inscription and a hostile military account cannot simply be merged into one unqualified voice. Comparing them helps recover a ruler who built, negotiated, patronised, and fought under changing conditions. [1, 4–7]

## Public works and late war shaped his legacy

Raj Singh left a major reservoir and an unusually durable statement of courtly history, while his final years brought destructive conflict with Aurangzeb. Those different parts of the reign belong together. The lake and inscriptions show what the court wished to preserve; the war shows the costs of its political struggle. [1–7]
