## A prince of the Sisodia house

**Sangram Singh**, remembered as Rana Sanga, was a son of **Rana Raimal** of Mewar. Later traditions describe dangerous rivalry among the princes before his accession. Their dramatic scenes belong to the history of his remembered beginnings, rather than to a contemporary account of every childhood injury or private conversation. [1, 3, 4]

## He inherited Kumbha’s formidable landscape

Sanga’s kingdom included the fortified centres and dynastic traditions strengthened by earlier rulers, including Kumbha. The hills offered refuge, but Mewar was not simply an isolated mountain state. Its rulers pursued influence across a wider region of competing kingdoms, alliances, commercial routes, and military opportunities. [1, 3, 5]

## A ruler with ambitions beyond survival

Sanga’s reign began in the early sixteenth century, conventionally around **1509**. He became a major power in northern Indian politics. His later fame rests not merely on resisting invasion, but on the scale of the coalition and regional influence he built before his confrontation with Babur. [1, 3, 4]

## He challenged the Delhi rulers too

Sanga’s campaigns brought him into conflict with the **Lodi** rulers of Delhi. These struggles preceded Babur’s conquest and helped establish his military standing. They show that Mewar’s ambitions were already directed beyond the defence of Chittor before a new Timurid power appeared in the Gangetic plain. [1–3]

## Regional victories enlarged his influence

His interventions in **Malwa**, associated with allies such as **Medini Rai**, increased his weight in regional politics. The political landscape crossed simple religious boundaries. Alliances depended on rivalries, succession, and strategic interests as well as identity, and could change when a new claimant to power altered the balance. [1–3]

## The wounded body in historical memory

Later portraits of Sanga emphasise his wounds and physical endurance. Such details contributed to a formidable warrior image. Exact totals of wounds should not be treated as a clinical record. The firmer historical point is that his opponents recognised a leader capable of assembling a serious challenge to their power. [2, 3]

## Babur transformed the contest

Babur’s defeat of **Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat in 1526** changed the political field. A victorious newcomer now occupied Delhi and Agra. Sanga’s confrontation with him was therefore a contest for power in a changing north India, not merely the continuation of an older dispute with the Lodis. [1–3]

## Rajputs and Afghans joined the challenge

Sanga’s coalition included Rajput chiefs and **Hasan Khan Mewati**, while **Mahmud Lodi** represented an Afghan dynastic alternative. The alliance cannot accurately be described as a single religious community facing another. Its members had different interests, united by the need to oppose Babur’s growing position. [2, 3]

## Early encounters worried the Mughal camp

Babur’s memoir describes anxiety in his camp as Sanga’s coalition advanced and imperial detachments suffered reverses. This is testimony from Sanga’s opponent, not from his own headquarters. It nevertheless shows that Khanwa’s outcome was not assumed in advance by the army that eventually won. [2]

## His opponent prepared for a decisive battle

Babur describes defensive preparations, the organisation of his forces, and a public renunciation of wine before the confrontation. His memoir gives religious meaning to those decisions. It supplies a detailed imperial perspective, but cannot reveal every calculation made by Sanga and the leaders on the opposing side. [2]

## The coalition met Babur in battle

At **Khanwa**, west of Agra, Sanga’s forces confronted Babur’s army in **March 1527**. The encounter tested a coalition against a force using prepared positions, firearms, and coordinated tactics. Babur won. Sanga’s courage and the scale of his challenge do not alter that military result. [1–3]

## A great ambition was checked

The defeat damaged Sanga’s bid for wider power, although Mewar itself survived. The outcome should not be turned into either the disappearance of every Rajput state or a victory secretly won by the defeated side. Its importance lies in how it strengthened Babur’s position and broke a major challenge to it. [1–3]

## The memoir is the victor’s voice

Babur celebrates the victory and describes his enemies through his own political and religious language. Later traditions offer additional explanations, including accusations of betrayal. Such claims require individual scrutiny. They should not be combined into a confident account that pretends every participant left matching testimony. [2, 3]

## A short aftermath with disputed details

Sanga survived the battlefield defeat but died soon afterwards, conventionally in **1528**. Accounts of poisoning by men opposed to renewed war belong to the later narrative tradition. The uncertainty surrounding his last days should remain visible instead of being filled with an invented final speech or precisely reconstructed private conspiracy. [3, 4]

## His family faced another age of crisis

Mewar continued under Sanga’s successors. The **1535 siege by Bahadur Shah of Gujarat** occurred after his death, and Akbar’s **1567–1568 siege** later still. Distinguishing those generations prevents the fortress’s several catastrophes from being compressed into one event attached to Sanga’s life. [1, 4]

## A regional ruler who contested a new empire

Sanga’s career made Mewar a central participant in the struggle for northern Indian power. Khanwa limited those ambitions without erasing the coalition he had built. His life is best understood through both parts of that record: the extraordinary reach before the battle and the consequences of the defeat. [1–5]
