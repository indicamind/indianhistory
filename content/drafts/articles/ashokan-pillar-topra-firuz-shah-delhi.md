**Firuz Shah Tughlaq could order thousands of men to move a stone giant. Getting its ancient letters to speak was another matter.**

At his command, workers lowered a towering pillar onto a cushion of silk cotton, wrapped its polished surface, hauled it on a carriage with forty-two wheels, and transferred it to boats on the Yamuna. In Delhi, builders raised it above a monumental structure beside the congregational mosque of his new capital. It was a feat of organisation, labour, and nerve. One broken shaft would have turned a royal triumph into a wreck. <sup>[1]</sup>

Then came a different test. The pillar carried writing. Brahmins and Hindu devotees were invited to read it. According to the chronicler Shams-i Siraj Afif, none could do so. Yet a wonderfully convenient interpretation circulated: the stone supposedly foretold the coming of a Muslim king named Firuz who would succeed in moving it. <sup>[1]</sup>

The real inscription was older than the sultan by some sixteen centuries. It belonged to **Ashoka**, the Mauryan emperor. It spoke about moral conduct, government, living beings, and the welfare of his subjects. The pillar had arrived in a new empire while carrying the voice of an old one. <sup>[3]</sup>

## First, identify the right pillar

Delhi has **two Ashokan pillars associated with Firuz Shah's transport projects**, and their stories are easily confused.

- **The Delhi–Topra pillar** came from Topra, in present-day Haryana. Firuz installed it at **Firozabad**, his capital on the Yamuna. It still stands at **Firoz Shah Kotla**, above the stepped structure beside the mosque ruins.
- **The Delhi–Meerut pillar** came from near Meerut. Firuz placed it at his **Kushk-i Shikar**, or hunting establishment, associated with the Delhi Ridge. It has a separate history of damage and restoration. <sup>[1][3][4]</sup>

This article chiefly follows the **Topra pillar**. It is the one for which Afif supplies the elaborate account of silk cotton, protective wrapping, a forty-two-wheeled carriage, boats, and the final raising of the stone. He treats the Meerut pillar more briefly, saying that it too was moved with great skill and effort. <sup>[1]</sup>

There are also two different historical constructions in front of the visitor: the ancient stone column and the medieval building supporting it. Ashoka did not build the Tughlaq platform. Firuz did not compose Ashoka's inscriptions.

## A message from a world sixteen centuries older

The Topra shaft is a sandstone monolith: a column made from a single piece of stone. The archaeological inventory describes its characteristic Mauryan polish and gives its height as **12.98 metres**. Its surviving Ashokan text comprises **seven Major Pillar Edicts**. <sup>[4]</sup>

Ashoka ruled in the third century BCE. His inscriptions present a ruler concerned with *dhamma*: moral behaviour, restraint, duties towards others, and the practical responsibilities of kingship. The pillar texts include instructions about administration, animals, punishment, and the treatment of different religious communities. <sup>[3]</sup>

The stone also carried a history between Ashoka and Firuz. Hultzsch records later inscriptions of the Chahamana ruler **Visaladeva, or Vigraharaja IV**, dated **1164 CE**, as well as shorter records left by other visitors. Different generations had encountered the same shaft and added their own marks. <sup>[3]</sup>

That matters when we speak about an inscription being unreadable. The pillar was not one uniform text from a single moment. Its Ashokan writing and its later records belong to different periods. Knowing how to read one script does not automatically provide access to every inscription on the stone.

By the fourteenth century, this monument had already survived the end of the Mauryan Empire, the passing of many dynasties, and the appearance of new scripts and political languages. Its physical endurance had outlasted the ability of the people consulted at Firuz's court to explain its oldest message.

## Before Ashoka returned, there was Bhima

Afif begins with a very different explanation of the pillars. He places them in the age of the Pandavas and recounts the belief that they had been the walking sticks of **Bhima**, the mighty hero of the *Mahabharata*. In this story, the gigantic Bhima used the stone columns while tending cattle of similarly enormous size. <sup>[1]</sup>

It is a striking transformation. A royal proclamation had become an object of epic memory. People could recognise that the stone was extraordinary without knowing who had originally commissioned its writing.

Afif retells the tradition through his own hostile religious vocabulary. That vocabulary belongs to the chronicler; the Bhima story belongs to the history of how people understood the monument. Neither provides a reading of the Ashokan edicts.

The legend does, however, explain something about the column's power over the imagination. A shaft that seemed too large and too finely made for ordinary human work invited a story large enough to match it.

Firuz saw an object that could serve another kind of story: the greatness of a living king.

## Why a Delhi sultan wanted an ancient column

**Firuz Shah Tughlaq ruled from 1351 to 1388.** His building programme helped reshape Delhi, and Firozabad gave his monarchy a new setting on the Yamuna. Afif describes the Topra column as a trophy and as a memorial intended for future generations. After its re-erection, it was known as the **Minara-i Zarin**, the Golden Column. <sup>[1]</sup>

The attraction was not dependent on understanding Ashoka. The pillar's scale, finish, antiquity, and formidable weight could make a statement before anybody read a letter. Placed beside the royal mosque and palace, it became a visible demonstration of what the sultan could command.

Afif's account explicitly connects lasting monuments with the reputation of kings. The column was therefore more than a beautiful thing transported for private enjoyment. It helped turn a new capital into a place that claimed a place in history. <sup>[1]</sup>

Reading the episode this way is an interpretation of its setting and of the chronicler's language. Firuz's admiration and desire to memorialise his reign are recorded; a detailed account of every private motive is not.

## The first danger: bringing the giant down

Moving an upright monolith begins with a dangerous problem: it has to stop standing without breaking.

Afif says that royal orders summoned the people of the surrounding area, together with cavalry and infantry, and required the tools and materials needed for the work. This was a mobilisation backed by state power. The narrative's thousands of workers had to supply the force that royal ambition alone could not provide. <sup>[1]</sup>

The preparation was unexpectedly soft. Large quantities of **silk cotton from the semal tree** were placed around the pillar. Workers removed the earth at its base, and Afif says that it came down gently onto the prepared cushion. The packing was removed gradually until the column lay safely on the ground. <sup>[1]</sup>

The square stone discovered beneath it was taken out too. Firuz's project involved both the conspicuous shaft and the base that had supported it.

The next concern was its surface. The column was encased in **reeds and raw hides**, a protective covering intended to prevent damage during movement. Before the spectacular public procession came careful work on the ground: lowering, cushioning, securing, and wrapping.

## Forty-two wheels, thousands of hands

Afif describes the construction of a carriage with **forty-two wheels**. Ropes were attached to the wheels, and the pillar was loaded onto it with immense effort. He then gives a more specific picture of the haul: **200 men at each wheel's rope**. <sup>[1]</sup>

Taken literally, that arrangement implies 8,400 pullers. That is an arithmetic implication of the chronicler's figures, not an independently verified workforce count. It conveys the scale he wished his readers to imagine.

The engineering challenge was to move one enormous load without losing control of it. Many wheels distributed that load across a larger vehicle. The ropes allowed large groups to act together. Protective wrapping helped guard the polished stone as the carriage travelled over land.

A sultan might give one order. Turning it into movement required carpenters, rope-makers, handlers, planners, and thousands of coordinated bodies. Most of those people remain unnamed in the account. Their work survives inside the story of the ruler's achievement.

The carriage was heading for the **Yamuna**, called the Jumna in the English translation. It did not simply roll all the way into Delhi.

## The river became part of the machine

At the river, a new stage began. Afif says that large boats had been collected, and that Firuz came to meet the transport. He describes the boats' capacities in historical units of grain, emphasising that these were substantial vessels. The pillar was transferred to them and conveyed to Firozabad. <sup>[1]</sup>

Transferring the load was itself a critical operation. A column resting safely on a carriage had to become a column resting safely on floating supports. The men involved had to control its weight and keep the vessels balanced.

The detailed story therefore follows several different transport problems, not one continuous act of dragging:

1. Lower and protect the shaft at Topra.
2. Carry it over land to the Yamuna.
3. Transfer it to boats and bring it to Firozabad.
4. Unload it, take it to the intended site, and raise it above a new building.

Afif marvels at the labour and ingenuity involved. Even allowing for the language of royal praise, the sequence reveals a project that required advance planning and several kinds of technical expertise.

## Raising a stone into the skyline

Arrival in Firozabad was not the end. The pillar now had to go upwards.

Afif says that a building was begun near the **Jami Masjid**, and that skilled architects and workers were employed. As stages of the supporting structure were completed, the column was raised in succession. Thick ropes and windlasses then helped bring it towards the vertical. <sup>[1]</sup>

The lifting was incremental. After a small rise, logs and bags of cotton were inserted to prevent the shaft from sinking back. This continued over several days. Large beams supported it as a cage of scaffolding formed around the stone. The square base stone taken from its earlier setting was placed beneath it. <sup>[1]</sup>

The finished appearance was richer than the bare shaft a visitor sees today. Afif describes ornamental black-and-white stonework and a **gilded copper cupola or finial**, called a *kalas*. The name Golden Column referred to this embellished medieval monument; the ancient shaft itself is sandstone. <sup>[1]</sup>

The spectacle was complete: a pillar from an older kingship, lifted above the architecture of a new one.

## A second contemporary account—and a date worth checking

Afif is not the only early source. The anonymous ***Sirat-i Firuz Shahi*** also describes the operation. In the archaeological memoir edited by **J. A. Page**, **Mohammad Hamid Kuraishi** translated the relevant portion of this Persian work. The memoir's preface identifies its composition date as **772 AH**, rendered there as **1370 CE**. <sup>[2]</sup>

The *Sirat* attributes the ingenious plans to the king himself. It describes wooden supports, ropes, pulleys, and the balancing of the shaft. It also says that elephants failed to drag the fallen pillar, after which longer ropes and **20,000 men** succeeded in taking it to the river. <sup>[2]</sup>

That is not the same numerical account as Afif's forty-two wheels and 200 men to each rope. The two should not be combined into one supposedly exact set of instructions. Their shared outline is impressive enough: an immense column was taken down, transported to the river, floated to the capital, and raised with elaborate mechanical help.

The *Sirat* dates the felling and transport to **Muharram 769 AH**, translated in the memoir as **September 1367 CE**. Some older references instead give **1356**; the Haryana State Gazetteer is one example. The explicitly dated contemporary account is why 1367 is used here for the Topra operation, while the differing date is kept visible. <sup>[2][5]</sup>

The sources also have a political purpose. A narrative that makes the ruler the source of every ingenious solution is praising kingship as well as describing engineering. Recognising that purpose helps us appreciate the work without treating every number or compliment as a modern technical report.

## Yes, Brahmins were asked to read the writing

This is the episode that turns a magnificent transport story into an encounter with lost knowledge.

After describing the raising and ornamentation of the Topra pillar, Afif turns to the writing on it. In the nineteenth-century English translation, he calls it writing in **“Hindí characters.”** He says that **many Brahmins and Hindu devotees were invited to read it, and that none was able**. <sup>[1]</sup>

The point is more precise than saying that Brahmins translated the edicts for Firuz. The recorded consultation failed to produce a reading. Afif does not preserve the names of the people consulted, a transcript of their discussions, or an account of how they tried to solve the script.

His phrase “Hindí characters” should not be mistaken for a modern identification of the inscription as Hindi written in Devanagari. The ancient edicts are in **Brahmi script**, in a form of **Prakrit**. The later Sanskrit records on the same shaft are another layer of its history. <sup>[3]</sup>

Script and language are different problems. A person can know Sanskrit, religious literature, or a contemporary writing system and still be unable to decipher much older letter forms. The failure recorded at this court does not tell us everything those scholars knew. It tells us that the oldest writing on this monument was beyond the reading that Afif reports.

This consultation is a separate episode from the [Brahmin's execution described elsewhere in Afif's chronicle](/article/firuz-shah-tughlaq-brahmin-persecution). His account of the pillar does not identify the consulted readers with that unnamed victim.

## The prophecy that made the sultan the hero

Immediately after reporting the failure to read the writing, Afif records another story. Some Hindus were said to have interpreted it as a prediction: nobody would move the pillar until a Muslim king called **Sultan Firuz** appeared. <sup>[1]</sup>

It is a remarkably flattering explanation. The stone had supposedly been waiting for the very king who had just moved it.

Afif introduces this with a report of what was said. He does not demonstrate how those words were obtained from the letters. The surviving Ashokan inscriptions, now readable, do not contain this prediction about Firuz. <sup>[1][3]</sup>

The story is evidence of the meanings gathered around the pillar in the sultan's time. It is not a translation of Ashoka's message. Whether it arose from flattery, a circulating legend, or another circumstance cannot be settled from this short passage.

The contrast is compelling. The court's story made the monument announce the destiny of a fourteenth-century ruler. The actual ancient text addressed the responsibilities of a very different monarchy.

The Topra pillar is exceptionally important because it preserves **all seven Major Pillar Edicts**, including the long Seventh Edict. Hultzsch notes that the seventh is unique among these pillar copies. Its writing extends around the shaft as well as appearing within the columnar arrangement of the earlier text. <sup>[3]</sup>

Read [Major Pillar Edict IV](/article/ashoka-major-pillar-edict-4), and the subject is the administration of justice. Ashoka discusses the responsibilities of officers known as *rajukas*, fairness in judicial proceedings, and a three-day respite for prisoners condemned to death. These are statements of royal policy, including the continued existence of capital punishment, rather than a picture of a modern state without coercion. <sup>[3]</sup>

Read [Major Pillar Edict V](/article/ashoka-major-pillar-edict-5), and the text turns to animals protected from killing and to restrictions on harmful practices. Read [Major Pillar Edict VII](/article/ashoka-major-pillar-edict-7), and the emperor reflects on the promotion of moral conduct, shade trees, wells, generosity, and the work of officials among different religious communities, including Brahmins and renunciants. <sup>[3]</sup>

The pillar's original programme reached far beyond the question of who could move a great stone. It asked how a ruler should influence conduct and provide for the beings within his realm.

That makes its survival unusually rich. A medieval court that could not read the text nevertheless preserved a major statement of ancient kingship in a new royal setting.

## The inscription became readable in 1837

The decisive breakthrough came in **1837**, when **James Prinsep** read and translated the Delhi–Topra inscription. Hultzsch recounts the route into the problem: comparison of inscriptions, repeated letter groups, and the recognition of short donor formulas from Sanchi helped unlock the alphabet. This was the work of assembling evidence across objects and places. <sup>[3]</sup>

Deciphering letters and identifying their royal author were connected but distinct achievements. Prinsep's early interpretation of the king's title was corrected through **George Turnour's** knowledge of Pali historical traditions from Sri Lanka, which helped identify the ruler as Ashoka. The literary tradition and the inscriptions illuminated each other. <sup>[7]</sup>

This was not the recovery of a king who had vanished from every form of memory. It was a new access to Ashoka's own public words, and a new basis for relating them to surviving traditions about him.

The shift transformed the pillar. A monument long explained through Bhima, royal ambition, and a story about Firuz could now be studied as a set of third-century BCE proclamations. Its medieval history remained; its ancient authorship became legible again.

The site's [guide to Ashoka's edicts](/article/ashoka-edicts) brings those texts together, including the individual pillar edicts preserved on this shaft.

## The other pillar had a rougher afterlife

The **Meerut pillar** deserves its own place in the story. Afif says that Firuz brought it from near Meerut and raised it at the hunting palace. It was not the column set above the structure at Kotla. <sup>[1]</sup>

A later account by **Carr Stephen**, published in 1876, describes its damage in a powder-magazine explosion during **Farrukhsiyar's reign, 1713–1719**. It also records the cutting out and sending of an inscribed portion to the Asiatic Society of Bengal, its return to Delhi in **1866**, and the re-erection of the restored pillar in **1867**. He reproduces the dated inscription on its restored base. <sup>[6]</sup>

That later history belongs to the Delhi–Meerut pillar. It should not be inserted into the Topra transport as a claim that Firuz broke the Kotla shaft into pieces to move it. Afif's detailed account of the Topra operation is about protecting and transporting the column, not deliberately cutting it up.

Two removals, two destinations, two later histories: keeping them distinct makes the engineering achievement and the surviving monuments easier to understand.

## Who owns the meaning of an ancient monument?

Ashoka used the stone to communicate royal ideas. A twelfth-century ruler added inscriptions. Local memory associated such columns with Bhima. Firuz uprooted this one, transported it, and gave it a dominant position in his capital. Scholars later recovered a reading of its older script.

Each layer changed what the pillar meant to the people standing around it.

Its removal can be understood as an act of royal appropriation: an extraordinary object was taken from one setting and made to serve the prestige of another ruler. At the same time, the care described in the transport accounts helped the shaft and its writing survive. Both observations follow from the recorded actions; neither requires us to pretend that the sultan understood the edicts. <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>

The workers who lowered and lifted it remain mostly anonymous. The people called to read it remain unnamed. Ashoka's message, the object of so much effort and speculation, outlasted them all.

**Firuz Shah brought the pillar to Delhi. He could make it stand above his capital; the old inscription would yield its meaning only centuries later. The sultan's monument still carries an emperor's words.**
