## A merchant’s son born in coastal Porbandar

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi was born on **2 October 1869** at **Porbandar** on the Kathiawar peninsula of Gujarat. His father Karamchand Gandhi served as the chief minister (*diwan*) of Porbandar and later Rajkot. Born into a Modh Bania merchant family, his childhood unfolded within a small princely coastal setting. [1, 2]

## Vaishnava devotion and Jain household ethics

His mother **Putlibai** was deeply devout, belonging to the Pranami tradition that integrated Vaishnava and Islamic teachings. She practiced strict vegetarianism, daily prayers, and rigorous religious fasting. The pervasive presence of Jain monks in Gujarat instilled in the young boy an abiding reverence for total non-injury (**ahimsa**) and truth. [1, 2]

## Early marriage to Kasturba Kapadia

In 1883, at thirteen years of age, Mohandas was married in a child marriage to fourteen-year-old **Kasturba Kapadia**. Later in his autobiography, Gandhi openly criticized the social custom of child marriage, recounting the early jealousy and domestic adjustments of their youth. Kasturba became his steadfast lifelong partner in public satyagraha. [1, 2]

## A father's tears and the lesson of Ahimsa

As an adolescent in Rajkot, Gandhi secretly experimented with smoking and stealing a bit of gold to clear a debt. Overcome with guilt, he wrote a full confession to his bedridden father, asking for punishment. His father read the note and wept silent tears of forgiveness, leaving a lifelong lesson in love-force. [1, 2]

## Sailing for legal studies in London

In September 1888, after vowing to his mother to abstain from meat, wine, and women, eighteen-year-old Gandhi sailed for England. He enrolled at the **Inner Temple** in London to study law. Living in Spartan rented rooms, he joined the London Vegetarian Society, befriending ethical reformers and Western intellectuals. [1, 2, 4]

## Discovering the Gita and the Sermon on the Mount

In London, Theosophist friends introduced Gandhi to Sir Edwin Arnold’s translation of the **Bhagavad Gita**. He simultaneously read the New Testament’s **Sermon on the Mount**, finding in both texts an urgent call to selfless action and returning good for evil. These readings laid the spiritual foundation for his future activism. [1, 4]

## Called to the bar and returning home

Called to the bar in June 1891, Gandhi returned to India, where he was shattered to learn that his mother had died during his absence. Attempting to establish a legal practice in Rajkot and Bombay, he suffered paralyzing stage fright in court, unable to cross-examine witnesses. Disheartened, he accepted a temporary contract in South Africa. [1, 2]

## The crucible of Pietermaritzburg station

In June 1893, traveling on legal business to Pretoria, a white passenger objected to Gandhi sitting in the first-class compartment. When Gandhi refused to vacate his seat, a constable threw him onto the freezing platform at **Pietermaritzburg station**. Shivering in the dark waiting room, he underwent a profound moral awakening. [1, 2, 4]

## Resolving to resist racial humiliation

Waiting for dawn at Pietermaritzburg, Gandhi contemplated fleeing back to India, but rejected retreat as cowardice. He resolved to stay and confront the root disease of racial prejudice. The personal insult transformed into a wider commitment to defend the dignity of thousands of Indian indentured laborers and traders in South Africa. [1, 2]

## Founding the Natal Indian Congress

In 1894, when the Natal government moved to disenfranchise Indian voters, Gandhi organized the **Natal Indian Congress**. He united diverse groups—indentured Tamil laborers, Parsi clerks, and Gujarati Muslim merchants—into a coherent political movement. He drafted petitions, wrote letters to the press, and educated the public on civic rights. [1, 2]

## The voluntary Indian Ambulance Corps

During the Anglo-Boer War (1899–1900), Gandhi organized a voluntary **Indian Ambulance Corps** of eleven hundred men to tend wounded soldiers. He wished to demonstrate that Indians were willing to share civic duties of citizenship alongside rights. Under heavy artillery fire at Spion Kop, his stretcher-bearers earned widespread admiration. [1, 2]

## The hated Black Act of Transvaal

In 1906, the Transvaal colonial administration passed the Asiatic Law Amendment Ordinance, known as the "Black Act." It required all Indians to register, carry fingerprint passes at all times, and submit to police inspections in their homes. The Indian community regarded the ordinance as a devastating assault on their personal honor. [1, 2, 4]

## The birth of Satyagraha in Johannesburg

On **11 September 1906**, at the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg, three thousand Indians assembled to protest the ordinance. The gathering took a solemn oath before God to refuse obedience to the law and endure imprisonment, fines, or death without violence. Gandhi named this conscious moral resistance **Satyagraha** ("holding firmly to truth"). [1, 2, 4]

## Communal simplicity at Phoenix Settlement

In 1904, deeply moved by John Ruskin’s book *Unto This Last*, Gandhi founded the **Phoenix Settlement** near Durban. Residents drew identical modest wages, grew their own vegetables, and printed the multilingual weekly journal *Indian Opinion*. It served as a practical laboratory for community self-reliance and voluntary poverty. [1, 2]

## Tolstoy Farm and moral preparation

In 1910, in partnership with architect Hermann Kallenbach, Gandhi established **Tolstoy Farm** near Johannesburg. Families of satyagrahis lived together, baking bread, tending orchards, and observing strict physical labor without servants. The community fostered mutual solidarity, preparing satyagrahis to withstand prison sentences and economic boycott without breaking. [1, 2]

## The Great South African March of 1913

In late 1913, protesting a discriminatory three-pound annual tax and a court ruling invalidating Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi marriages, thousands of Indian coal miners struck. Gandhi led over two thousand men, women, and children across the Transvaal border in peaceful defiance of immigration bans, enduring multiple arrests. [1, 2, 4]

## General Smuts and the Indian Relief Act

The spectacle of peaceful laborers enduring imprisonment and police brutality forced General **Jan Smuts** to negotiate. Under the **Indian Relief Act of 1914**, the three-pound tax was abolished, Indian marriages were legally validated, and indentured labor regulations were relaxed. Gandhi had proven the immense power of collective non-violent resistance. [1, 2, 4]

## Returning permanently to India in 1915

In January 1915, after twenty-one years in South Africa, Gandhi returned to India at forty-five years of age. His mentor **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** advised him to spend his first year traveling across India by third-class train, observing the lives of ordinary citizens. The tour exposed Gandhi directly to rural poverty and agrarian distress. [1, 2]

## Founding the Sabarmati Ashram

In May 1915, Gandhi established an ashram on the banks of the Sabarmati River near Ahmedabad. He admitted a family of "untouchables" (Dalits) into the community, prompting orthodox donors to cut off financial support until an anonymous patron intervened. The ashram became his national headquarters for training satyagraha volunteers. [1, 2]

## Defending indigo peasants at Champaran

In 1917, Gandhi visited **Champaran** in northern Bihar to investigate the **tinkathia** system, which forced tenant farmers to grow indigo on three-twentieths of their land for British landlords. Defying police orders to leave, Gandhi stated in court that he acted out of higher duty to conscience. The government conceded, ending the century-old exploitation. [1, 3]

## Fasting for textile workers in Ahmedabad

In early 1918, Gandhi intervened in a labor dispute between textile mill owners and workers in **Ahmedabad**. When striking laborers began to waver, Gandhi undertook his first public fast in India, declaring he would touch no food until arbitration was achieved. The mill owners relented, granting workers a thirty-five percent wage increase. [1, 3]

## Peasant tax refusal in drought-hit Kheda

Simultaneously in **Kheda**, Gujarat, peasant crops failed completely due to torrential rains, but colonial revenue collectors insisted on full tax payments. Gandhi organized a non-violent no-tax campaign, urging cultivators to let their lands be confiscated rather than yield. The administration eventually granted relief to poorer peasants, drawing Vallabhbhai Patel into national leadership. [1, 3]

## Nationwide hartal against the Rowlatt Act

In March 1919, the imperial government passed the repressive **Rowlatt Act**, allowing political suspects to be detained without warrant or trial. Gandhi called for a nationwide **hartal** (strike) on 6 April, shutting down businesses, schools, and transport across India. It marked the first nationwide mass mobilization in modern Indian history. [1, 3]

## The tragedy of Jallianwala Bagh

On **13 April 1919**, British troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer opened fire on thousands of unarmed men, women, and children gathered at **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar, killing hundreds. Horrified by the massacre, Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood, and Gandhi returned his Boer War medals, declaring British rule an unmitigated evil. [1, 3]

## Forging the alliance with Khilafat leaders

In 1920, Gandhi forged an alliance between the Indian National Congress and the **Khilafat Movement** led by the Ali brothers. He believed that supporting Indian Muslims in their grievances over the Ottoman Caliphate would cement enduring Hindu-Muslim brotherhood. The alliance provided the mass base for an unprecedented pan-Indian challenge to imperial rule. [1, 3]

## Launching the Non-Cooperation Movement

The Congress formally adopted the **Non-Cooperation Movement** in late 1920. Gandhi called upon citizens to surrender British titles, boycott foreign cloth, withdraw from government schools, and leave law courts. Millions took up hand-spinning on the **charkha**, wearing homespun **khadi** as a badge of national self-reliance and defiance. [1, 3]

## Suspending the movement after Chauri Chaura

On **4 February 1922**, a violent clash erupted at **Chauri Chaura** in Uttar Pradesh, where an enraged crowd torched a police station, killing twenty-two policemen. Gandhi immediately called off the entire national movement, insisting that satyagraha could never compromise with violence. Despite bitter criticism from colleagues, he prioritized moral discipline over political gain. [1, 2, 3]

## Pleading guilty to sedition in Ahmedabad

Arrested shortly after, Gandhi was tried for sedition before Judge C.N. Broomfield in Ahmedabad in March 1922. In a famous courtroom address, Gandhi openly pleaded guilty, declaring that sedition had become his moral duty toward an exploitative system. He was sentenced to six years in prison, spending his detention at Yerwada. [1, 2, 3]

## The constructive programme and the spinning wheel

Released early from prison in 1924 following surgery, Gandhi devoted himself to his **Constructive Programme**. He traveled across thousands of villages, campaigning against the evil of untouchability, promoting communal harmony, and organizing rural spinning cooperatives. He insisted that genuine freedom (*Swaraj*) required internal social reform and economic self-sufficiency. [1, 2]

## The pledge of complete independence

In December 1929, the Indian National Congress met in Lahore under Jawaharlal Nehru, declaring **Purna Swaraj** (Complete Independence) as its goal. On **26 January 1930**, millions across India raised the national flag and recited a collective pledge of independence. The country prepared for a renewed wave of civil disobedience. [1, 3]

## The 240-mile Salt March from Sabarmati

On **12 March 1930**, Gandhi set out from Sabarmati Ashram on the celebrated **Salt March** to the Arabian Sea at **Dandi**. Accompanied by seventy-eight trained volunteers, the sixty-year-old leader walked 240 miles through Gujarat villages. He chose the salt tax as his target because salt was an essential dietary requirement of the poorest laborers. [1, 2, 3]

## Scooping a pinch of salt from the sea

On the morning of **6 April 1930**, after prayers, Gandhi walked to the sea at Dandi, bathed, and picked up a lump of natural salt from the mud. By breaking the colonial salt monopoly, he signaled nationwide defiance. Across India, millions of citizens began illicitly manufacturing salt, courting mass arrest in hundreds of towns. [1, 2, 3]

## Stoic non-violence at Dharasana salt works

Following Gandhi’s arrest in May, Sarojini Naidu led twenty-five hundred satyagrahis in a march on the **Dharasana Salt Works**. Without raising a hand in defense, wave after wave of marchers advanced, stoically absorbing blows from police steel-tipped batons. American journalist Webb Miller’s international dispatches horrified readers and discredited British colonial authority worldwide. [1, 2, 4]

## The Gandhi-Irwin Pact in New Delhi

With over sixty thousand satyagrahis filling prisons, Viceroy **Lord Irwin** invited Gandhi for direct negotiations in New Delhi in March 1931. The resulting **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** released political prisoners and permitted coastal villagers to gather salt freely. The sight of the "half-naked fakir" negotiating on equal terms with the Viceroy marked a psychological victory. [1, 2, 4]

## Wearing a handspun shawl to Buckingham Palace

In autumn 1931, Gandhi traveled to London as the sole Congress delegate for the **Second Round Table Conference**. He stayed with the poor in London's East End and visited Lancashire textile workers who cheered him warmly. Invited to Buckingham Palace, he met King George V wearing his trademark khadi loincloth and shawl. [1, 2, 4]

## Fasting in Yerwada against separate electorates

Re-imprisoned in Yerwada Jail in 1932, Gandhi learned of the British "Communal Award," which created separate legislative electorates for the "Depressed Classes" (Dalits). Gandhi feared this would permanently divide the Hindu community and legally entrench untouchability. He launched a "fast unto death" from his prison cell to oppose the statutory division. [1, 2, 3]

## Concluding the Poona Pact with Dr. Ambedkar

Confronting immense national concern for Gandhi’s life, Dalit leader **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** negotiated with Gandhi at Yerwada Jail. On **24 September 1932**, they signed the **Poona Pact**, abandoning separate electorates in exchange for significantly increased reserved seats for Dalits in provincial legislatures. Gandhi then founded the Harijan Sevak Sangh to dismantle untouchability. [1, 2, 3]

## Rural life in the cottage at Sevagram

In 1936, Gandhi moved his headquarters to **Sevagram** ("village of service") near Wardha in Maharashtra, living in a simple mud-and-bamboo cottage. He dedicated himself to rural sanitation, cottage industries, and his educational model of *Nai Talim* (learning through crafts). Sevagram became the moral nerve center of the national movement. [1, 2]

## Giving the mantra of "Do or Die"

On **8 August 1942**, at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, the Congress launched the **Quit India Movement**, demanding an immediate end to British rule. Addressing the gathering, Gandhi gave the nation an unforgettable mantra: **"Do or Die"** (*Karo ya Maro*)—we shall either free India or die in the attempt. Predawn police raids arrested Gandhi and the entire Congress Working Committee. [1, 3]

## Grief and bereavement at Aga Khan Palace

Detained at the **Aga Khan Palace** in Pune, Gandhi suffered devastating personal losses. His brilliant personal secretary **Mahadev Desai** died suddenly of a heart attack in his arms within days of confinement. In February 1944, his devoted wife **Kasturba** passed away with her head resting on his lap after sixty-two years of marriage. [1, 2]

## Walking barefoot through the swamps of Noakhali

In late 1946, as partition loomed, horrific communal violence erupted in **Noakhali** in East Bengal. At seventy-seven years of age, Gandhi walked barefoot from village to village for four months through mud and swamps, comforting grieving families and sleeping in burnt homes. His solitary moral presence quelled communal riots when military forces were helpless. [1, 2]

## Fasting in Calcutta on Independence Day

On **15 August 1947**, when India achieved independence amidst the agony of Partition, Gandhi stayed away from official celebrations in New Delhi. He spent the day in a dilapidated Muslim home in **Calcutta**, fasting and praying to maintain peace. Viceroy Mountbatten famously hailed him as the "One-Man Boundary Force" who preserved Bengal from bloodshed. [1, 2, 4]

## A winter fast for refugee harmony in Delhi

In January 1948, anguished by the mistreatment of Muslim citizens in Delhi and Hindu refugees from Pakistan, Gandhi undertook his final fast unto death at **Birla House**. He demanded peace across Delhi and insisted that India pay Pakistan its post-partition asset share of fifty-five crore rupees. Leaders yielded, and Gandhi broke his fast on 18 January. [1, 2]

## Martyrdom on the way to evening prayer

On the evening of **30 January 1948**, while walking to his 5:00 PM prayer meeting at Birla House, Gandhi was shot at point-blank range by Hindu extremist **Nathuram Godse**. Murmuring **"Hey Ram"** ("O God"), Gandhi fell to the ground, dying instantly. Prime Minister Nehru addressed the weeping nation: "The light has gone out of our lives, and there is darkness everywhere." [1, 2]

## An enduring moral beacon for global freedom

Gandhi was cremated on the banks of the Yamuna River at Rajghat before more than a million mourners. His philosophy of **Satyagraha** transformed human struggles for justice, inspiring Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, and civil rights movements across the globe. He proved that moral courage and active non-violence remain the ultimate instruments of human liberation. [1, 2, 4]
