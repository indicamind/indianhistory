**A king being led away in ropes is the central image in al-Utbi's account of Mahmud's victory at Peshawar.** Jayapala, members of his family and leading men of his court had fallen into the conqueror's hands. The chronicler describes the removal of their jewellery and the public display of the defeated ruler. The military victory was being converted into wealth, hostages and a warning to other opponents. [1]

The battle took place on **8 Muharram 392 AH, conventionally converted as 27 November 1001**. It belongs among the best dated confrontations in the early sequence of [Mahmud of Ghazni's Indian campaigns](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). Its consequences were substantial, but it did not end Shahi resistance. Jayapala's son Anandapala and later generations continued the struggle. [1][2]

## The king who confronted Mahmud

Jayapala ruled the Hindu Shahi kingdom, whose territories and political connections straddled the northwestern approaches to the subcontinent. His power was associated with Waihind, also called Udabhandapura and generally identified with modern Hund near the Indus. He had already fought Mahmud's father, Sabuktigin. The encounter of 1001 therefore renewed a dynastic conflict rather than beginning relations between two previously isolated worlds. [1][2]

It is tempting to turn Jayapala into a representative of a single, united India. The sources instead reveal a particular king facing a particular expansionist power. Other rulers possessed their own armies, interests and territories. The fortunes of Jayapala's kingdom mattered widely, but he did not command every polity on the subcontinent. Precision about his position makes the scale of his defeat intelligible without granting him an imaginary universal sovereignty.

Mahmud, meanwhile, was consolidating a monarchy centred on Ghazni. His position depended on wars and alliances in Khurasan and Central Asia as well as on the eastern frontier. The Peshawar expedition followed the less clearly documented [attacks on frontier forts in 1000](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1000-frontier). It brought him against a stronger and more visible opponent. [2]

## An army outside Peshawar

Al-Utbi describes Mahmud assembling selected cavalry and advancing to Peshawar. Jayapala approached with cavalry, infantry and elephants. The chronicler presents a contrast between the smaller army favoured by God and a much larger enemy confident in numbers. That contrast is a familiar element in the work's celebration of Mahmud. It shapes both the military story and the figures attached to it. [1]

The translated account gives Mahmud **15,000 cavalry**, and Jayapala **12,000 horsemen, 30,000 infantry and 300 elephants**. These are statements by the chronicler, not audited returns. Other transmitted figures differ: Nazim's reconstruction, drawing on Gardizi as well as al-Utbi, exposes discrepancies in the descriptions of army strength, casualties and spoils. We can identify the types of troops more confidently than their exact numbers. [1][2]

War elephants were valuable military and political assets. Their presence signalled resources and could affect how an opponent arranged a battle. Their capture could strengthen the victorious army or supply prestigious gifts. It would be misleading, however, to reconstruct the exact placement of every elephant at Peshawar from a passage concerned chiefly with the glory of victory. The source does not provide a modern operational map.

## What al-Utbi says about the fighting

Al-Utbi describes an exchange of arrows and close fighting with swords and spears. He says that the Shahi army was defeated before noon. The passage uses elaborate comparisons: flashing swords, flowing blood and enemies laid across the ground. Its brutality is explicit, and its literary construction is equally apparent. [1]

The account credits divine assistance with the result. That is the explanation offered by a court author who intended to celebrate the ruler and his religion. A historical analysis can record that explanation without adopting it. It can also recognise that violent encounters occurred without treating every image of the narrative as a literal battlefield observation.

The sources do not let us reconstruct a dependable minute-by-minute sequence. They do establish the defeat of Jayapala's force and the capture of the king and important relatives. Those facts mattered more immediately than the particular flourish with which a writer described a sword stroke. An enemy sovereign in captivity offered Mahmud leverage that an ordinary battlefield success might not provide. [1][2]

## Death totals and a huge claim about captives

Elliot and Dowson's translation of al-Utbi claims **15,000 enemy dead** and **500,000 enslaved men and women** among the gains. The latter figure is especially difficult to accept as a reliable count. It appears in a passage that repeatedly stresses wealth beyond calculation and a victory of extraordinary dimensions. [1]

Gardizi's figure for the dead, as discussed by Nazim, is **5,000**. The disagreement should remain visible. Reporting a single number without its competing testimony would manufacture certainty. The alternatives cannot be resolved simply by choosing the higher figure because it sounds more dramatic, or the lower because it seems easier to imagine. [2]

The uncertainty about totals does not make enslavement or killing disappear. Al-Utbi openly celebrates both. The people listed as gains were human beings deprived of freedom, and the chronicler's pleasure in their capture belongs to the moral world of the conquering court. The responsible distinction is between a documented practice and an unverified numerical scale. We need not accept half a million as a census to understand that captivity was part of the campaign's violence.

## A captured king stripped of royal display

The treatment of Jayapala receives unusually direct attention. Al-Utbi says that the captives were bound, handled roughly and brought before Mahmud. He gives a large valuation for the jewels removed from Jayapala's necklace and from those of his relatives. The removal of ornaments had both material and symbolic force: signs of royal dignity became the conqueror's property. [1]

The translated narrative then describes Mahmud ordering Jayapala to be displayed in a condition of disgrace. The stated object was intimidation. A ruler who could be seen bound and humiliated embodied the danger facing those who resisted. This was political communication through a captive's body, rather than an incidental detail after the battle.

The account is entirely from the victorious side. Jayapala's own explanation, the reactions of the people who saw him and the individual experiences of the other prisoners do not survive in this passage. We should avoid inventing their speeches. Their absence from the record also warns us that the loudest surviving voice was not the only human perspective on the event.

## Elephants, ransom and hostages

Victory was followed by negotiation. Al-Utbi describes a demand for **fifty elephants** and the retention of a son and grandson as guarantees that the terms would be fulfilled. Jayapala appealed to Anandapala, whose resources lay across the Indus, and the elephants were delivered. The hostages were then released. [1]

Other transmitted material adds a substantial monetary ransom. Nazim discusses **250,000 dinars**, while the valuations of individual necklaces vary between accounts. These figures should be kept attached to their particular witnesses. They are not interchangeable entries in one surviving treasury ledger. [2]

The release of the hostages is a particularly concrete detail. It shows a bargain being completed rather than prisoners remaining an undifferentiated part of the spoils. The fifty elephants had to be obtained through surviving Shahi resources before the detained relatives could return. In this transaction, military captivity, a family relationship and access to valuable animals became connected instruments of negotiation. [1]

The hostage arrangement demonstrates that Jayapala and his family still had political value. Mahmud's victory did not produce an immediate, simple replacement of every Shahi institution. He extracted resources through the defeated dynasty. Anandapala remained able to obtain the elephants and act on his father's appeal. The kingdom had been gravely damaged while retaining elements of authority and organisation.

## Waihind after Peshawar

Al-Utbi's narrative proceeds to operations at Waihind and its neighbourhood. He describes people taking refuge in hills and forests, followed by the dispatch of a Ghaznavid force and further killings. The campaign extended beyond the first battlefield; pressure was applied to the surrounding political landscape. [1]

Later retellings have sometimes carried Mahmud from Peshawar to Bathinda. Elliot and Dowson objected to that identification in this campaign, reading the relevant place as Waihind. The distinction matters. Hund near the Indus and Bathinda far to the southeast produce very different routes. Similar-looking place names should not become an excuse to move an army across several additional rivers. [3]

Waihind also appears in the story of the major campaign of 1008–1009. Those are separate moments in a continuing frontier struggle. An article should not combine every battle bearing the name into a single encounter, nor assume that the earlier victory made the later one unnecessary. Repeated warfare is evidence that the struggle for effective authority remained unresolved.

## Jayapala's death

Al-Utbi reports that Jayapala subsequently burned himself to death. He connects the act with humiliation, old age and the loss of legitimacy after captivity. The narrative is frequently repeated as though it opened a direct window into the defeated king's thoughts. It does not: it is the interpretation of a hostile author. [1]

The distinction does not require suppressing the reported death. It requires presenting the report accurately. Jayapala's self-immolation is part of the historical tradition transmitted by early writers, while its emotional and ritual explanation reaches us through those writers' assumptions. There is no surviving private testimony in which the king explains his choice.

Nazim places the death after Jayapala's return, probably around 1002–1003, rather than treating it as an event on the afternoon of the battle. The broader sequence is more secure than a precise day: captivity, release under terms, death and the continuation of Shahi kingship under Anandapala. [2]

## What changed, and what survived

The victory removed wealth, elephants and people from the Shahi realm and enhanced Mahmud's standing. It demonstrated that he could defeat and capture a king who had opposed his father. In the language of his court, this was a vindication of his authority and religious mission. In practical terms, it altered the balance along the northwestern frontier. [1][2]

It did not establish permanent Ghaznavid control over the whole of northern India. Nor did it eliminate the Shahi dynasty immediately. Anandapala continued to negotiate, resist and draw upon surviving resources. Later campaigns at Waihind and Nandana show that political defeat could be followed by renewed resistance from a changed territorial base. The history is a prolonged dismantling of power, not a single instant in which every opponent vanished.

Peshawar therefore deserves its place near the beginning of the series for more than its famous date. It exposes the mechanisms of conquest: a military victory, the capture of an elite family, organised humiliation, ransom demands, hostage guarantees and further attacks beyond the battlefield. Al-Utbi's triumphal language records those mechanisms even where his numbers cannot be trusted as precise totals.
