**The conqueror who returned from Bhatia had won the city and lost much of his baggage.** Al-Utbi celebrates Mahmud's victory over its ruler, describes killing and pillage, and lists elephants among the gains. He then admits that swollen rivers and hostile country made the journey home dangerous. The campaign's ending sits awkwardly within a narrative devoted to the ruler's triumph, which makes it especially revealing. [1]

This expedition, conventionally dated **1004–1005**, is the third grouping in [Mahmud of Ghazni's Indian campaigns](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). The name in the early account is Bhatia, with variant spellings. Its identification has generated a serious disagreement: Bhera and Bathinda have both been proposed. The article retains the historical name rather than disguising a disputed location as a settled fact. [2][3]

## Which Bhatia?

The geographical problem is fundamental. A historian cannot confidently describe the campaign's strategic destination while remaining unsure where that destination was. Al-Utbi says that Mahmud crossed the Indus in the neighbourhood of Multan and advanced towards Bhatia. He describes a fortified, wealthy city with a substantial ditch and a ruler able to field elephants. Those clues narrow the discussion without providing a modern street address. [1]

Elliot and Dowson argued for **Bhera** in the Punjab. Muhammad Nazim later made a case for **Bathinda**, challenging earlier identifications and discussing the question separately in an appendix. Their disagreement affects the route, the surrounding landscape and the relationship between the campaign and the roads into the Gangetic plain. [2][3]

Neither an attractive modern photograph nor a familiar place name resolves the textual problem. A photograph of a fort at one proposed location should be labelled as a proposed identification, not presented as the securely documented wall Mahmud attacked. The safest narrative follows the sequence the early source supplies and distinguishes that sequence from later attempts to plot it on a map.

## The interval after Peshawar

The campaign followed [Jayapala's defeat at Peshawar in 1001](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1001-peshawar). That earlier victory had demonstrated Mahmud's ability to capture a major ruler and extract elephants and ransom. It had not established uncontested authority across the territories farther east. Bhatia represents another encounter with a local power whose resources were still outside his direct control. [1][2]

Al-Utbi introduces the expedition after Mahmud's affairs in Sijistan, or Sistan. This placement matters because the eastern campaigns were interspersed with warfare elsewhere. Ghazni's forces were not proceeding mechanically from one Indian objective to the next. Mahmud was building and defending a larger monarchy, with demands in several directions. [1]

Nazim places the departure in **October 1004**, within 395 AH, and the return around **May–June 1005**. Those dates constitute a reconstruction from the sequence of early accounts. The broad span is preferable to treating the whole event as a single day in 1004 or assuming that the printed Hijri year supplies an exact Gregorian date for every stage. [2]

## Biji Rai meets the invasion

The ruler appears in the English translations under forms including **Biji Rai** and **Baji Rai**. Al-Utbi says that he left the protection of his fortifications and offered battle. His army included elephants, which the chronicler describes as a major support of the force. The opening confrontation was therefore outside the city's walls, rather than a siege from the first moment. [1]

The account describes fighting across three days and a decisive attack on the fourth. Its repeated references to swords, arrows and elephants establish the kinds of combat the author wanted readers to imagine. They do not allow a precise reconstruction of every formation or a numerical estimate of the defenders. No reliable full army return is preserved in the passage.

Al-Utbi gives Mahmud a heroic personal role in the decisive charge. In his presentation, the sultan strikes opponents on both sides and breaks the enemy line. A court author had strong reasons to place the ruler at the centre of victory. The episode should therefore be reported as the chronicler's account, without converting his stylised description into an independently verified display of individual swordsmanship. [1]

## From open battle to the ditch

The defenders withdrew behind the city's walls. Al-Utbi then turns to the work required to reach them: the attackers dealt with the ditch, damaged its defensive edges, opened approaches and widened passages. These are practical details within a highly rhetorical narrative. They show that victory in the field did not immediately remove the obstacles presented by a fortified settlement. [1]

The ditch deserves attention because it was part of the defensive system, not a decorative feature. An attacker seeking to bring men close to the walls had to overcome the gap and the ground around it. Al-Utbi's account places labour alongside fighting: movement, filling and clearing were necessary parts of the assault. The source does not specify the dimensions well enough to calculate how long that work took.

The sequence also complicates a simple story of effortless conquest. Biji Rai first fought outside, then used the fortifications, while the attackers worked to overcome them. The chronicler ultimately attributes success to divine support, but his own account retains evidence of duration, resistance and physical obstacles.

## The ruler's escape and death

Al-Utbi says that Biji Rai left the city secretly with a few companions and sought refuge away from the walls. A selected body of Mahmud's troops pursued him and surrounded his party. The chronicler reports that the raja, seeing no escape, killed himself with a dagger. [1]

The source interprets the death through its hostile religious vocabulary. That interpretation belongs to al-Utbi. What can be extracted more cautiously is the reported sequence of flight, pursuit, encirclement and self-inflicted death. We do not possess Biji Rai's own explanation or the words exchanged among his companions.

The geographical language around his refuge adds to the difficulties of locating Bhatia. Forests and elevated ground appear in the translated narrative, but identifying their exact modern counterparts remains part of the debate. It would be misleading to name a particular surviving hill as the scene without additional evidence. The dramatic clarity of the raja's reported end exceeds the geographical clarity of the text.

## Pillage and the people who vanish into booty

The narrative explicitly describes the army continuing to kill and plunder. It does not merely say that a flag changed or that the raja lost a battle. Violence continued through the acquisition of territory and resources. The distinction matters because an account limited to kings and elephants would conceal the treatment of the wider population. [1]

At the same time, the passage does not preserve a dependable death total or a register of enslaved inhabitants. It leaves individuals unnamed. We should resist inventing representative victims or attaching a later campaign's casualty figure to Bhatia. The record's asymmetry is itself significant: it records valuable animals and arms more clearly than the identities of the people who suffered.

Al-Utbi assigns **120 elephants** to Mahmud's share. Gardizi's account, as compared by Nazim, gives **280 elephants** in the gains. Those figures should not be combined into an apparent exact total. They illustrate both the importance of elephants to the conquerors and the instability of numerical claims transmitted by the chronicles. [1][2]

The object of the attack is also worth identifying accurately. Al-Utbi describes a wealthy fortified city with arms, troops and elephants, rather than opening with a famous temple treasury. His narrative emphasises the ruler, defensive works and resistance before turning to booty and religious instruction. A general description of Mahmud as a temple raider does not explain all the targets in his career; Bhatia appears in this source as a defended political centre. [1]

## Conversion and the language of cleansing

Al-Utbi says that Mahmud remained at Bhatia and appointed someone to instruct those who had adopted Islam. He frames the operation as removing religious pollution. The language is evidence of the conquering court's confessional priorities; it is not a neutral description of the previous inhabitants or their beliefs. [1]

Nazim's reconstruction gives a more explicit account of coercion and associates survival with conversion. That stronger formulation should remain attached to the study and the sources it compares. The translated passage used here securely records violence, a stay after the victory and organised religious instruction. It does not provide individual testimonies showing the mixture of force, calculation or conviction behind each person's change of religion. [1][2]

The combination nevertheless matters. Warfare, territorial acquisition and the establishment of religious institutions were connected in the court's account. Conversion cannot be discussed as an entirely separate event occurring in a peaceful vacuum. Nor can a collective statement by a victorious writer tell us how every local household responded.

## A return through dangerous rivers

Al-Utbi's final paragraph changes the emphasis. Mahmud was returning during a period when the rivers were full, and the terrain and hostile forces compounded the difficulty. The chronicler admits that much of the baggage was lost and that warriors were dispersed. His conclusion credits divine protection with the ruler's survival. [1]

These losses are not incidental to the campaign. An army travelling with goods and animals had to move its gains through the same geography that had constrained its approach. A captured treasury was only useful if enough of it could reach the victor's centre of power. A swollen river could threaten men and wealth regardless of the outcome of the preceding battle.

The passage also limits what can be claimed. It gives no reliable ledger of the losses and no precise count of men who drowned. Nazim connects the difficult return with the hostility of Multan's ruler, but that is a reconstruction from the wider sequence. The text does not support an invented scene of a named convoy sinking at a securely identified crossing. [1][2]

## Why Bhatia led towards another conflict

The route near Multan and the troubles of the return connect Bhatia with the next major eastern campaign. In 1006 Mahmud attacked Multan, whose ruler was an Ismaili Muslim. The relationship between passage, local political resistance and the Ghaznavid court's religious hostility would become more explicit there. [1][2]

Bhatia should not be reduced to a mere prelude. Its own story records sustained resistance, the attack on a fortified city, the death of a ruler, plunder and an attempt to give the conquest a religious permanence. Its conclusion exposes the cost of carrying a victory home. Those are substantial outcomes even though the exact location remains contested.

The campaign also shows why clear distinctions are useful. A victorious army could take a city without enjoying a safe journey back. It could appoint religious instructors without leaving us evidence of durable control over every neighbouring district. It could acquire a great quantity of wealth while losing an unknown share in transit. “Victory” describes the result of the confrontation, not every consequence that followed.
