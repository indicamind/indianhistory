**In 1006, Mahmud marched against a Muslim ruler whose religion his court historian denounced as corrupt.** Multan's Abu al-Fath Daud was associated with Ismaili Islam. Al-Utbi's account treats his religious activity as an offence warranting military intervention. The episode makes clear that Mahmud's eastern wars cannot all be described as encounters between a Muslim army and Hindu kings. [1][2]

The campaign also drew in Anandapala, the Hindu Shahi ruler, and unfolded across routes whose rivers and political boundaries mattered to the invaders. It is the fourth dossier in [Mahmud of Ghazni's Indian campaigns](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions), following the attack on Bhatia and preceding the rebellion of a governor entrusted with Ghaznavid interests in the region.

## Multan before the invasion

Multan was an established political and religious centre in the Indus world. Its history included a famous temple, early Muslim government and later Ismaili authority. This was a layered urban past, rather than a town that first became significant when Mahmud arrived. The relationship between its rulers and surrounding powers had developed through trade, diplomacy and warfare. [2][3]

The sources often call the community “Qarmatian” or use hostile terms for alleged heretics. Such labels require care. Medieval Sunni authors could apply broad polemical categories to opponents whose beliefs they intended to discredit. “Ismaili” is useful here for identifying the religious and political association without adopting the conqueror's denunciation as a factual description of moral impurity. [2][3]

Daud's religious difference was not an incidental detail in al-Utbi. The chronicler alleges that he promoted his doctrines among the inhabitants and depicts Mahmud as responsible for stopping him. That account presents the conquest as an intervention in the ordering of Islam itself. Its victims and opponents therefore included people who understood themselves to be Muslims. [1]

## After the Bhatia campaign

The previous expedition had brought Mahmud through the region near Multan on the way to Bhatia. Its return was troubled by rivers and hostile territory. Later reconstruction connects the deterioration in relations with Multan to those movements. The connection is plausible within the sequence, but it does not replace the explicit religious justification supplied by al-Utbi. [1][2]

The two explanations address different aspects of the conflict. A ruler's control over roads and crossings affected military reach. A rival religious establishment affected the legitimacy the Ghaznavid court wished to claim. Territorial ambition, security and confessional hostility could reinforce one another. We do not need to select a single motive and pretend that the others were absent.

The campaign is conventionally placed in **396 AH, or 1006**. Al-Utbi gives it a distinct narrative after Bhatia. Elliot and Dowson argue that it should not be collapsed into the earlier expedition merely because some later histories compress the chronology. The separate preparations and the change of immediate objective matter. [1][4]

## A demand for passage

Al-Utbi describes swollen rivers obstructing the army and says that Mahmud requested passage through Anandapala's territory. Anandapala refused and offered resistance. The chronicler then presents Mahmud as turning first against the Shahi ruler before continuing towards Multan. [1]

This sequence establishes a triangle of conflict, rather than a simple attack along an empty corridor. Daud held Multan. Anandapala retained power in parts of the northwestern region after the defeat of his father Jayapala. Mahmud sought to move a substantial army through a landscape in which both water and rival rulers could impede it.

The exact route is less secure than that outline. Elliot and Dowson criticise circuitous routes produced by later identifications of place names. A modern narrative should not draw a confident line across the map while quietly relying on one disputed reading. The important point is that access itself became a matter of armed contention. [4]

## Violence in Anandapala's country

The early account is explicit about the attack that followed refusal. Al-Utbi describes slaughter, imprisonment, plunder, burning and depopulation. He celebrates the pursuit of Anandapala through difficult country and says that the defeated ruler withdrew towards the neighbourhood of Kashmir. [1]

Those words should not be softened into a bloodless exchange of military positions. The source presents the destruction of resources and the intimidation of a population as part of the pressure applied to a ruler. At the same time, the account is a triumphal one. It does not provide independently checked numbers for the killed, the imprisoned or the settlements damaged.

The distinction is particularly important here because the action against Anandapala was not the stated final objective. People in his territories suffered during a campaign directed at Multan. The route to a target could itself become a zone of conquest and devastation. A history organised only around the destination risks overlooking that wider field of violence.

## What happened to Daud?

Al-Utbi says that Daud, learning of Anandapala's defeat, judged his own position hopeless. In the translated account, he prepared to remove his possessions on elephants to Sarandip, the historical name generally associated with Sri Lanka, leaving Multan exposed. This is the narrative of the victorious side, and its description of his intentions is not a surviving statement by Daud himself. [1]

Other accounts describe arrangements that left Daud alive and politically relevant. His reappearance in the events leading to the [second conquest of Multan in 1010](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1010-multan) is one reason not to write as though the 1006 expedition ended his career permanently. Flight, submission, a financial agreement and the establishment of Ghaznavid oversight are not interchangeable descriptions of an identical event. [2][4]

The evidence supports an important victory for Mahmud and the imposition of demands on Multan. It supports a less tidy political aftermath than a sentence announcing that the old regime vanished overnight. The years that followed included a rebellion by Sukhpal, renewed military intervention and the eventual imprisonment of Daud. [2]

## A city assessed for an enormous payment

In Elliot and Dowson's translation, al-Utbi says that Mahmud besieged Multan, took it by assault, treated the inhabitants severely and imposed a payment of **twenty million dirhams**. These claims connect military success with collective punishment and the extraction of wealth. The chronicler's language treats the financial burden as an expiation of the inhabitants' religious offences. [1]

Much later, Firishta describes **20,000 gold dinars or gold dirhams as an annual payment**, coupled with promises of obedience and the abandonment of the condemned doctrine. The difference involves the sum, the metal or denomination, the timing and the nature of the agreement. These should be presented as alternative traditions, not combined into a fabricated balance sheet. [4]

No municipal tax record survives in these passages to show how a levy was divided among households or collected. The financial claim is therefore better evidence of the court's presentation of subjugation than of an exact amount entering a treasury. Its coercive purpose remains clear even while its numerical precision remains uncertain.

The contrast with the later expedition is essential. Accounts of the return in 1010 describe Daud's imprisonment and severe persecution of the Ismaili establishment. Those consequences should not all be moved into 1006. The first campaign imposed a settlement while leaving room for further disturbance; the second sought to close that unfinished contest. Keeping them separate explains why Multan reappears in a campaign list instead of making the repetition look like an error. [2]

Later historians also give Daud a family history. Elliot and Dowson identify him as a grandson of Hamid Lodi, whose dealings with Sabuktigin belong to the earlier frontier struggle. The details rest on later tradition, but they reveal how those historians understood Multan: as a continuing local principality with inherited relationships, rather than a religious faction that suddenly appeared when Mahmud needed an opponent. The invasion confronted a political establishment as well as a doctrinal rival. [4]

## A conflict inside Islamic history

Mahmud's court presented him as a defender of Sunni order and a ruler recognised by the Abbasid caliph. Multan's Ismaili establishment represented a different religious and political alignment. That setting gives substance to al-Utbi's accusations, although it does not make those accusations impartial. [1][2]

The episode challenges a habit of treating medieval religious identity as a guarantee of political alliance. Muslim rulers fought one another, while religiously different powers could negotiate or cooperate against a shared danger. The refusal of passage by Anandapala and the campaign against Daud need to be understood through their particular interests, rather than sorted into two permanently unified religious camps.

It would be equally misleading to remove religion altogether. Al-Utbi makes doctrinal opposition central to his explanation. The campaign's rhetoric, the later treatment of Multan's religious institutions and the persecution described in subsequent accounts all require it to remain in the story. The evidence points to political expansion conducted through a strongly confessional language of legitimacy. [1][3]

## Mosques and the memory of earlier rule

Al-Biruni describes changes to Multan's religious buildings. In the passage quoted by Elliot and Dowson, he says that an earlier Ismaili or Qarmatian ruler destroyed the famous idol and killed its attendants. In Sachau’s fuller translation, Jalam established the replacement mosque in his own elevated brick mansion or castle, while closing the older mosque. Mahmud subsequently reopened an older mosque associated with earlier Muslim government, while the competing building ceased to function in the same way. [3]

This account belongs to a longer history of the city, and not every action in it occurred during the assault of 1006. Its value is to show that conquest involved the control of religious memory and public space within Islam as well as between religions. Which mosque remained open could express which earlier authority was considered legitimate.

Al-Biruni's account also cautions against a common chronological shortcut. Multan's principal idol had already been attacked before Mahmud's arrival. Attributing every destruction associated with a city to its most famous later invader produces a simpler narrative by discarding the sequence supplied by the sources. [3]

## A victory interrupted by events elsewhere

Mahmud could not remain indefinitely in Multan. The invasion of his western dominions by the Karakhanid ruler known as Ilak Khan demanded his attention. Nazim's reconstruction places the appointment of Sukhpal, also called Nawasa Shah, within the arrangements made as Mahmud turned back towards that danger. [2]

The episode reveals a recurring limitation of conquest. Mahmud's power was extensive, but his presence could not be everywhere. Authority in an eastern province depended on governors and local arrangements while the main army dealt with another frontier. The next crisis arose within precisely that system of delegated rule.

The 1006 expedition therefore had several outcomes: Anandapala's territories were attacked, Multan was brought under severe pressure, religious difference became an explicit justification for conquest, and the need to return west constrained the settlement. The persistence of resistance in later years does not cancel the violence of this victory. It shows that a successful invasion and durable control were different achievements.
