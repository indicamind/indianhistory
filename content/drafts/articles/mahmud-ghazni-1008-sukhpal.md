**The man Mahmud trusted to guard part of his eastern conquests became the reason for his next campaign.** Sukhpal, called Nawasa Shah in the chronicles, had adopted Islam and entered the conqueror's political service. During Mahmud's struggle against the Karakhanids, he rejected that religious affiliation and challenged the authority under which he governed. [1][2]

The rebellion began in **late 1007**, while the campaign that removed him belongs to **early 1008**. This distinction is easily lost when the episode is given a single date. It is the fifth dossier in [Mahmud of Ghazni's Indian campaigns](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions), following [the conquest of Multan in 1006](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1006-multan). [2]

## Who was Sukhpal?

The sources identify him as an Indian prince who had become Muslim and received authority over territories conquered by Mahmud. His name appears in several forms, including Sukhpal and Sewakpal. “Nawasa Shah” is a title or designation associated with the word for a daughter's son or grandson; it has encouraged attempts to place him precisely within the Shahi family. [1][3]

The exact relationship is less secure than many retellings suggest. Later writers connect him with Jayapala, but their formulations vary. The fact that Anandapala and Sukhpal belonged to the wider political setting of the Shahi frontier does not establish that one was certainly the other's son. A confident family tree assembled from inconsistent labels would obscure the problem rather than resolve it. [2][3]

The important early point is his position between two worlds of authority. He came from a local royal background and subsequently held office under Mahmud. The Ghaznavid ruler was therefore governing conquered territory partly through someone whose status predated, and was not created entirely by, the conquest. That arrangement could bring useful connections, but it could also preserve the resources for a challenge.

## Captivity and conversion before governorship

Gardizi, as discussed by Nazim, links Sukhpal's conversion with earlier captivity and the intervention of Abu Ali Simjuri. That account does not place the conversion straightforwardly after Jayapala's defeat in 1001. It warns against assuming that every notable captive in the story must have been seized in Mahmud's best-known early battle. [2]

The earlier circumstances remain incompletely reconstructed. Sukhpal's path into Ghaznavid service involved the military and political networks of the previous generation, rather than a single clear sequence of capture at Peshawar, immediate conversion and appointment. Nazim considers possible connections with earlier Shahi hostages, but identifies these as reconstruction. [2]

What follows more securely is that conversion and political employment became connected. A prince who accepted Islam could be regarded as a suitable intermediary for a conquering Muslim ruler. His existing standing could make him useful among the people he governed. The arrangement also depended on continued loyalty, which religious affiliation alone could not guarantee.

## What territory did he govern?

Al-Utbi says that Nawasa Shah had been appointed over some of Mahmud's conquests in Hind to protect their borders. The description is broader than a surviving appointment document naming one precisely bounded province. Nazim places his governorship at Multan in the arrangements made when Mahmud had to turn back towards the western crisis. Other discussions have associated him more closely with the Shahi frontier. [1][2][3]

The distinction matters because “governor of Multan” can imply a clarity the early narrative does not possess. The city, its surrounding territory and the adjoining frontier were not necessarily administered as a single unchanging unit throughout these years. Mahmud's delegation of authority is more certain than the exact perimeter of Sukhpal's jurisdiction.

Nor was the appointment proof that every conquered district had become obedient. A governor tasked with protecting a frontier was operating in a region where local powers retained influence. The invasion of 1006 had weakened opponents and extracted resources, but it left political arrangements that required supervision and could break down when the main army was elsewhere.

## The opportunity created by war in Central Asia

Mahmud's conflict with the Karakhanid power commonly represented by Ilak Khan provides the immediate setting. Forces from the north had threatened his western dominions while he was engaged in India. The sultan had to protect Khurasan and defeat those rivals before he could return his full attention to the east. [1][2]

Al-Utbi's sequence explicitly places the action against Nawasa Shah after victory over Ilak Khan. Nazim, using Gardizi's chronology, dates the news of Sukhpal's rebellion to **Rabi II 398 AH, January 1008**. The revolt itself had begun during the preceding winter, around December 1007. A label such as “the invasion of 1007” therefore compresses two different stages. [1][2]

The opportunity was structural as well as personal. Mahmud had a large realm but could not keep its principal army in every province. The east depended on delegated command while he confronted a northern enemy. Sukhpal's rebellion exposed the vulnerability inherent in that arrangement, particularly where a new regime relied on a governor with independent local standing.

## Apostasy in the court historian's account

Al-Utbi presents the change in religious allegiance as the defining offence. In his language, Nawasa Shah abandoned Islam and returned to the worship the author condemned. He also describes contact with other local chiefs in connection with rejection of Mahmud's authority. Religion and political disobedience appear together. [1]

This is more than a neutral personnel dispute. The chronicler treats a governor's religious defection as a challenge to the order Mahmud claimed to uphold. The narrative vindicates the ruler for reversing it. At the same time, it supplies no first-person statement from Sukhpal explaining whether his decision arose from conviction, political calculation, local pressure or a combination of reasons.

It would be unwarranted to decide those motives for him. His previous conversion should not automatically be dismissed as fraudulent, nor should the subsequent change be romanticised as an entirely private act of conscience. What survives is a rebellion described by its opponent, in which a religious change and a transfer of political allegiance were closely connected.

## Mahmud's rapid intervention

The early account emphasises the speed of Mahmud's movement. Al-Utbi says that he advanced swiftly, removed Nawasa Shah from office, seized his accumulated treasure and reasserted control. The purpose of the passage is to show that a breach in authority was rapidly closed. [1]

Nazim's reconstruction adds that Sukhpal offered resistance and then fled towards a hill refuge before being captured. The identification of that refuge is disputed. Readings of the place name have generated different geographical proposals, including the Salt Range. There is insufficient certainty to stage the pursuit at a confidently named modern pass. [2]

The source does not preserve a detailed siege or a large, separately described battle. A campaign could be significant because it removed a governor without matching the scale of Peshawar. This episode concerns the enforcement of rule after conquest. Counting it among the invasions should not turn it into an invented clash of enormous armies.

## Confiscation, a fine and imprisonment

Al-Utbi explicitly records the seizure of Nawasa Shah's treasure and the end of his governorship. Gardizi's account, discussed by Nazim, provides the further tradition that he was captured, fined **400,000 dirhams** and imprisoned. Later accounts expand the custodial detail, including the treasurer to whom he was entrusted. [1][2][3]

These stages should be kept distinct. Removal from office and confiscation have an early narrative basis. The exact sum and the description of imprisonment come through a different witness. They should not be silently blended into a single eyewitness account whose author supposedly saw every transaction.

The punishment also shows why confiscation belongs in military history. Sukhpal's accumulated wealth was a resource that could support independence and a prize for the returning sovereign. Taking it both rewarded the victor and reduced the defeated governor's capacity to recover power. The financial dimension was part of the suppression, rather than a decorative note after it.

The fine also differs in kind from the payments described at Multan. Here it is attached to a named defeated governor and the suppression of his rebellion. The earlier levy concerned a city or its ruler under terms of conquest. Combining the amounts would blur the distinction between individual confiscation, collective assessment and continuing tribute. [1][2]

## What violence can be established?

Al-Utbi employs the language of bloodshed and the destruction of idolatry in describing Mahmud's intervention. It makes the coercive character of the operation explicit. It does not give us a reliable casualty total, a list of settlements destroyed or a clear distinction between the losses of fighting men and inhabitants. [1]

That limitation should be preserved. There is no evidential basis here for importing a massacre figure from Peshawar or Mathura. Equally, the absence of a count should not turn the episode into a peaceful dismissal. It was an armed return against a rebel governor, narrated through imagery of the sword and followed by confiscation and captivity.

The people governed by Sukhpal remain largely outside the chronicler's field of vision. We do not know how widely the rebellion was supported, whether communities were divided or which local interests favoured restoration of Ghaznavid rule. A later historian's desire for a clear choice between ruler and rebel cannot supply those missing voices.

## Religious identity did not remove political complexity

The armies serving Mahmud included people of different regional and religious backgrounds. Al-Utbi himself lists Indians among the forces employed against Ilak Khan. Service in the Ghaznavid army and opposition to it did not map neatly onto a permanent boundary separating all Indians from all outsiders, or all Hindus from all Muslims. [1]

Sukhpal's career illustrates that complexity particularly sharply. A local prince could become a Muslim, serve the conqueror, govern conquered territory and subsequently reject both the religion and the political arrangement through which he held office. The later rebellion did not erase his earlier service, just as that service did not make his loyalty irreversible.

Multan adds a further complication: its previous ruler Daud was an Ismaili Muslim opposed by Mahmud's Sunni court. The sequence therefore includes conquest of one Muslim establishment, appointment of a converted prince and suppression of that prince's later rebellion. Treating the whole story as one undifferentiated religious confrontation would miss its actual structure. [1][2]

## An authority restored, not a frontier settled

Mahmud removed Sukhpal, but restoring a governor's territory did not settle the wider contest with the Shahis. A much larger confrontation followed at Waihind, leading to the seizure of the treasury at Nagarkot. The pace of events makes Sukhpal's rebellion a hinge between the settlement after Multan and another major assertion of Ghaznavid power. [1][2]

Its significance lies in exposing the limits of a victory that depends on a ruler's continued absence being manageable. Mahmud could delegate authority; he also had to return when that delegation failed. The brief passage on Nawasa Shah opens a view of how conquest had to be maintained through appointments, surveillance, punishment and the repeated movement of armies.
