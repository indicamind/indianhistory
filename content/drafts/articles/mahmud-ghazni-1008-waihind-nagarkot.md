**Even Mahmud's own court historian acknowledges that the battle might have gone against him.** Near Waihind, al-Utbi says, the opposing army came close to victory before the sultan's household troops attacked its rear and reversed the result. The passage preserves a moment of danger inside a work devoted to royal success. [1]

Victory opened the way to something else: the pursuit to **Bhimnagar, or Nagarkot, associated with Kangra**, where Mahmud seized an exceptionally wealthy treasury. This dossier treats the battle and the fortress as successive stages of the same campaign, following the continuity in al-Utbi's narrative. It is the sixth grouping in [Mahmud of Ghazni's Indian campaigns](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). [1][3]

## Why the campaign spans two years

The expedition is often called the invasion of 1008, but its operations extended into **1009**. The chronology reconstructed from al-Utbi and Gardizi places Mahmud's departure near the end of December 1008, in **399 AH**. The battle and the capture of Nagarkot followed. The familiar calendar label should not move every event backwards into the year of departure. [2]

Nazim gives the departure as **29 Rabi II 399 AH, converted as 31 December 1008**, and places the return to Ghazni around the middle of 1009. The exact Gregorian rendering of medieval Islamic dates can require caution, but the sequence across the winter and following months is clear enough. A date range makes that sequence visible. [2]

This was not the first battle associated with Waihind. Operations there had followed Jayapala's defeat in 1001. The recurrence of the name records a continuing struggle over the Indus frontier, not a duplicate entry that can simply be removed. Shahi power survived earlier defeats and could still present Mahmud with a serious military problem.

## Anandapala's surviving power

After Jayapala's death, Anandapala continued the Shahi dynasty's resistance. He had already opposed Mahmud's passage during the [Multan campaign of 1006](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1006-multan). The [suppression of Sukhpal's rebellion in early 1008](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1008-sukhpal) did not eliminate the wider network of local rulers and military resources on which resistance depended. [1][2]

Al-Utbi identifies the commander confronting Mahmud near Waihind as **Brahmanapala, son of Anandapala**. That is an important detail, because later accounts often give Anandapala the central battlefield role. The distinction between the king under whose authority an army assembled and the prince described as commanding it should remain visible. [1]

The early narrative does not provide a reliable political census of every contingent. It establishes a formidable opponent, fighting with elephants and other forces, and capable of putting the Ghaznavid army in danger. More elaborate descriptions of a northern Indian alliance come from later writers and require their own attribution.

## The great confederacy in Firishta

Firishta, writing in the early seventeenth century, describes Anandapala appealing to rulers across northern and central India. His list includes Ujjain, Gwalior, Kalinjar, Kannauj, Delhi and Ajmer. He adds stories of women sending wealth to support the struggle, including contributions obtained by selling jewellery or working at spinning. [3]

These details have powerfully shaped popular memory. They present resistance as an unusually broad collective effort. They are also separated from the campaign by roughly six centuries. Al-Utbi's much earlier description does not independently establish every ruler and contribution in Firishta's account. A list repeated frequently by modern writers does not acquire an earlier date through repetition.

It is reasonable to say that later tradition remembered a great coalition. It is less secure to narrate every named contingent as though a contemporary muster roll survived. The resistance was substantial without our needing to accept every detail of its later literary expansion. The distinction preserves both the possibility of cooperation and the limits of the evidence.

## A battle near the Indus

Al-Utbi places the encounter by the river of Waihind, associated with the Indus. He describes combat lasting from morning until evening and acknowledges that the opponents nearly prevailed. The turning movement by the household troops, striking at the enemy's rear, is his explanation of how the situation changed. [1]

The court author's religious explanation accompanies the military one. Divine assistance makes the attack effective in his presentation. A modern account can identify that interpretation while retaining the practical information: the struggle was prolonged, Mahmud's position was dangerous, and an attack from another direction helped break the opposing force.

The result was a rout and pursuit. Al-Utbi gives **thirty elephants** among the gains and describes the defeated being killed as they fled through difficult country. The source's own language makes clear that violence did not stop when the organised line broke. The pursuit extended the consequences of the battlefield defeat. [1]

## The elephant story and a different ending to the battle

Firishta provides a different, more elaborate account. He describes a long confrontation before battle, fierce attacks by Gakkhar fighters and heavy losses among Mahmud's forces. He then makes the crucial turning point an elephant carrying Anandapala: frightened or injured under attack, it turned away, and the army mistook the movement for the ruler's flight. [3]

The story is memorable because it gives an enormous reversal a single visible cause. It must nevertheless remain Firishta's story. Al-Utbi's account centres on an attack by the household troops and names Brahmanapala at the head of the opposition. Presenting the rear attack and the elephant panic as independently confirmed parts of one exact sequence would hide the disagreement between witnesses.

The casualty figures in the later passage require the same caution. Thousands of deaths are claimed on each side and in the pursuit, but no independent count survives. The serious resistance and the eventual defeat are more securely established than the exact numbers attached to stages of the fighting.

## Pursuit to a mountain treasury

Al-Utbi connects Mahmud's march to Bhimnagar directly with pursuit of the defeated. That connection supports treating Waihind and Nagarkot as one campaign grouping. Elliot and Dowson reject a reconstruction that separates them into different expeditions simply to fit another chronology. [1][3]

The destination was a strongly situated fortress associated with the Kangra region. The early description stresses its elevation and the surrounding waters. Its defensive position mattered, but so did the wealth stored there. Al-Utbi says that rulers, wealthy devotees and religious patrons had accumulated precious goods as offerings to a revered sacred institution. [1]

That treasury had a meaning beyond its exchange value. Gifts made in pursuit of religious merit became the conqueror's spoils. The same objects could carry devotional significance for donors and political significance when displayed by Mahmud. Seizing them converted accumulated patronage into a demonstration of another ruler's power.

## The fortress opens

Al-Utbi describes Mahmud surrounding the stronghold and preparing an attack. In his account, the defenders were frightened by the force confronting them, appealed for mercy and opened the gates. Gardizi's account, accessed through Nazim's comparison, gives a siege lasting three days. [1][2]

The difference of emphasis matters. One version stresses terror and rapid submission; the other supplies a duration. Neither provides enough information for a detailed diagram of siege engines, assault ladders and breaches. A historian should not furnish the walls with machinery the sources do not mention merely because a fortified setting invites it.

The narrative at this point is principally about possession of the treasury. It should not be enlarged automatically into a claim that every sacred building in the surrounding region was completely demolished. The early passage explicitly supports the stripping and appropriation of wealth. Additional assertions about the physical destruction of particular structures require separate evidence.

## Who took charge of the wealth?

Al-Utbi says that Mahmud entered with close attendants and Abu Nasr of Juzjan. Two trusted officers, Altuntash and another chamberlain whose name varies in transliteration, were assigned responsibility for gold, silver and other valuables. The sultan personally attended to the jewels. [1]

This is a strikingly administrative moment. The army did not merely encounter a vague mountain of riches. The narrative describes allocation of responsibility, the separation of categories and arrangements for transport. Whatever exaggeration surrounds the total, the passage presents confiscation as an organised royal operation.

Camels and people carried the seized goods. That detail connects treasure to the labour required to move it. Objects accumulated in a hill fortress had to travel back through the region of the campaign before they could become a spectacle at Ghazni. Their removal was a physical operation as well as a religious and political claim.

## Numbers too grand to accept uncritically

The translated chronicle claims **seventy million dirhams** in coined money and an enormous weight of gold and silver ingots. It describes fine fabrics, precious stones and a prefabricated or folding structure of silver, with dimensions that convey extraordinary luxury. [1]

Such figures should not be treated as an audited inventory. The text repeatedly insists that the quantity exceeded calculation, while translations and scholarly retellings differ over weights and units. Medieval measures also varied. Converting the entire reported treasure into a confidently calculated modern cash value would add another layer of unsupported precision.

The safer conclusion is substantial and still consequential: an exceptionally rich treasury was seized and transported, and the event became central to the court's celebration of Mahmud. The stories of particular luxury objects show what writers considered persuasive evidence of magnificence, even where exact totals cannot be recovered.

## The display at Ghazni

After returning, Mahmud displayed the spoils in his palace court. Al-Utbi describes ambassadors and dignitaries seeing jewels and objects whose splendour supposedly surpassed anything they knew from earlier rulers. This was an audience arranged around conquered wealth. [1]

The display served more than personal enjoyment. It offered visible evidence that Mahmud's campaigns produced resources and success. Foreign envoys encountered the results of an eastern victory in the setting of his capital. Wealth taken from a sacred treasury became an instrument in the representation of an expanding monarchy.

The spectacle also exposes the imbalance of the surviving account. We hear about the admiration expected from visitors at Ghazni, but not from the donors whose offerings had been removed or the people whose defeat made the seizure possible. The court historian's attention follows the treasure towards its new owner.

The list of seized objects also distinguishes this campaign from the preceding attack on Sukhpal. There, confiscation removed a rebel governor's accumulated resources. At Nagarkot, the source describes wealth gathered through a much wider history of sacred patronage. The different origins of the spoils mattered: the victory appropriated not merely one ruler's working funds, but goods carrying the prestige of many donors and generations. [1]

## What the victory did not end

Mahmud appointed a trusted officer to the fort and returned west. Yet the defeat did not immediately extinguish every Shahi resource. Anandapala and his successors retained or recovered authority in other places; Nandana would become the focus of another major campaign in 1014. [1][2]

Waihind and Nagarkot therefore mark a serious change in the balance of power without constituting the conquest of all northern India. A dangerous opponent had been defeated, a prestigious treasury seized and Ghaznavid authority extended. Resistance could still reorganise beyond the ground already lost.
