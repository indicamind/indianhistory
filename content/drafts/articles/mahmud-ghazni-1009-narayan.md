After the riches seized at Nagarkot had been displayed at Ghazna, Mahmud led another expedition into northern India. Its destination appears in al-Utbi’s account as Narain or Narayan. The narrative is brief, but its consequences are explicit: chiefs defeated, sacred images overthrown, people killed, property taken, and a subsequent agreement promising elephants and soldiers to the conqueror. The same account ends with caravans travelling safely between Hind and Khurasan. Violence and commerce appear within a few paragraphs of each other.[1]

This campaign forms part of the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). It follows the [Waihind and Nagarkot expedition of 1008–1009](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1008-waihind-nagarkot). It must remain distinct from the [Nandana campaign of 1013–1014](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1013-nandana): similar place names and confusing translated headings have repeatedly brought these separate episodes together.

## A campaign after Nagarkot

The preceding expedition had combined victory over Shahi resistance with the seizure of devotional wealth at a hill fortress associated with Nagarkot. Al-Utbi describes the treasures being shown to foreign envoys at Ghazna. His transition to Narayan therefore places the new attack within an already established pattern: military success abroad became royal display at home. Wealth was transported, presented, distributed, and made to demonstrate the ruler’s exceptional standing.[1]

Narayan was not another name for the same fortress. The court chronicler introduces a fresh expedition and then a separate embassy. He does not supply a full itinerary, departure date, or list of the towns crossed. Muhammad Nazim’s twentieth-century reconstruction dates the attack to the beginning of 400 AH, approximately October 1009, using the order in al-Utbi and the dating supplied by Ibn al-Athir. The latter wrote substantially later. The date is a reasoned reconstruction rather than an exact day recorded by a campaign eyewitness.[2]

There is also a contemporary poetic reference. Nazim cites a verse attributed to Ghadāʾiri celebrating a reward received for the victory of Narayan. It supports the place of the victory in courtly patronage, although it cannot supply the missing military itinerary. A poet’s reward and a chronicler’s account illuminate the royal publicity surrounding success; neither gives the defending population’s own record of the attack.[2]

## Where was Narayan?

Modern reconstructions commonly identify the destination with Narayanpur in the Alwar region of Rajasthan. Nazim follows Alexander Cunningham’s identification and connects the expedition with access toward the Ganges plain. That location is useful for understanding the usual historical reconstruction, but it should be labelled as an identification, not treated as a coordinate printed in al-Utbi’s text.[2]

The nineteenth-century discussion in Elliot and Dowson is less settled. Its editors considered a connection with Anhilwara and acknowledged geographical difficulties. Their appendix is valuable because it preserves the uncertainty, even when its proposed solutions have not stood equally well. An old translation’s footnote is not another medieval witness to the location.[3]

Al-Utbi says Mahmud reached the middle of Hind. That expression cannot fix the site on a modern map. The chronicler’s geographical language centres the conqueror’s advance and the scope of his success; it is not a measured survey of the subcontinent. A precise route passing through a sequence of present-day districts would need evidence beyond this phrase.

The distinction from Nandana is firmer than the exact location of Narayan. In the Arabic-derived extracts translated by Elliot and Dowson, Narain appears on printed page 36 and Nardin on pages 37–39. They are separated by an embassy and belong to different points in the narrative. Reynolds’s translation of a Persian adaptation uses headings that can obscure that distinction. The later Salt Range fighting should not be moved to Rajasthan simply because names look alike in English transliteration.[1][2][4]

## What the original narrative actually records

Al-Utbi describes Mahmud marching over hard and soft ground until he reaches the country of chiefs who had previously acknowledged no master. The description is politically revealing. Independence itself becomes a condition the invading ruler is entitled to end. The narrative measures success through the reduction of these chiefs and the destruction of the religious objects under their protection.[1]

The source reports a battle, plunder in property, horses, and elephants, and killing across hills and valleys. It does not give a dependable overall death toll. Nor does it provide a sequence of named defenders, a siege duration, or a detailed account of the main town’s fall. Those omissions limit what can be reconstructed. The supported narrative is already severe without adding invented street fighting, civilian speeches, or a specific number of dead.[1]

The overthrow of images was part of the action the chronicler celebrated. Describing the campaign only as a commercial opening would erase that statement. Describing it only as image-breaking would erase the simultaneous seizure of wealth, military animals, and political authority. The source presents these outcomes together because each could reinforce the prestige and resources of the ruler.

Its language also divides people into the conqueror’s religious community and a despised opposition. Such labels tell us how a Ghaznavid court writer justified the violence. They are not neutral descriptions of the inhabitants. We can report the killing directly without adopting the author’s judgment that the victims’ beliefs made their deaths meritorious.

## The embassy that followed

After the raid, al-Utbi introduces an embassy from a ruler of Hind who had witnessed the devastation caused by conflict with Mahmud. Relatives and chiefs were sent to ask that further invasions cease. Their offer included an annual delivery of fifty elephants, products of the country, and two thousand men for service at Mahmud’s court.[1]

The identification of this ruler needs care. Al-Utbi does not provide a personal name in the passage. Nazim understands him as the ruler of Narayanpur, while nineteenth-century editors made other connections. The natural sequence makes a connection with the preceding raid plausible, but the text does not permit a confident biography of the negotiator. An account should preserve the distinction between the named destination and the unnamed ruler of the embassy.[1][2][3]

This was an agreement negotiated under the threat of renewed invasion. The ruler’s offer followed reported destruction in his country and concern for his subjects. Calling it simply a friendly exchange would conceal the pressure that made the bargain possible. Equally, the text does not say Mahmud abolished the negotiating court or replaced every local institution with direct Ghaznavid administration. Tribute could leave an existing ruler in place while imposing a costly subordinate relationship.

Al-Utbi says Mahmud accepted the offer and sent an envoy to ensure that its conditions were carried out. A vassal of the Indian ruler accompanied the elephants to Ghazna. These details show that the agreement involved verification and delivery, not merely a declaration in a victory speech. Nevertheless, the account does not supply a surviving treaty, a year-by-year register of payments, or evidence that the arrangement remained unchanged for decades.[1]

## Why fifty elephants mattered

Elephants occur repeatedly in the accounts of Mahmud’s campaigns. They appear as defensive weapons, captured prizes, demanded tribute, and displays of royal wealth. At Narayan, their prominence ties the aftermath of the battle to the future military resources of the victor. An animal removed from one ruler’s service might become a resource in another ruler’s army.[1]

The chronicler describes the promised elephants as exceptionally large and strong. That qualification belongs to the rhetoric of a valuable gift. The stated fifty animals and two thousand men should remain attributed to him: they are reported terms, not figures checked against transport accounts. We also do not know from this passage the age, training, or eventual employment of each elephant.

Keeping an elephant required more than possession of the animal. Its practical value depended on handlers, feeding, movement, and continued care. The source does not itemise these arrangements. Its silence should prevent a fictional reconstruction of the caravan’s daily routine, but the omission also helps explain why an elephant tribute was a substantial demand. It transferred an expensive living resource rather than a symbolic token.

The promised soldiers had a different significance. Service at Mahmud’s court would draw manpower away from the tributary ruler and make some of it available to the conqueror. Al-Utbi does not explain whether the two thousand were to be replaced annually, how long they served, or what obligations their families retained at home. These questions remain open. The recorded demand is sufficient to demonstrate that submission reached beyond a payment of precious metal.

## Peace for caravans

The account’s final sentence connects the settlement with secure caravans travelling between Khurasan and Hind. That is important evidence for the court’s presentation of the result. Mahmud’s war was made to lead to order; submission supposedly turned an unsafe passage into a protected commercial connection.[1]

This does not mean the merchants’ security compensated the people whose property had been taken or whose relatives had been killed. Different groups could experience the same political settlement very differently. A trader crossing a road after the agreement might benefit from reduced fighting, while a household in the attacked territory had already suffered the cost of obtaining that peace.

Nor does the brief statement establish the volume of trade, the products carried, or the exact road followed. Nazim interprets the expedition partly in terms of access toward the Ganges Doab, but that is his reconstruction. Al-Utbi’s direct claim is narrower: tribute was paid and caravans travelled securely. A modern account should not inflate it into a documented customs system or a complete commercial integration of northern India.[1][2]

The juxtaposition remains revealing. Military violence, religious prestige, material extraction, and caravan protection were not separate worlds. A ruler could destroy sacred objects during a raid, demand animals and men afterward, and then present the resulting security as a further achievement. That combination explains more of the source than a single motive assigned to every participant.

## What changed, and what cannot be proved

The immediate outcome reported by al-Utbi is a successful raid followed by submission and deliveries. No sustained siege narrative survives in his passage. No local royal name is securely supplied. There is no evidence here that the whole of Rajasthan was conquered, that Delhi was the intended target, or that a battle at a precisely identified modern location determined the entire campaign.[1][2]

Narayan’s importance lies in a more specific development. Mahmud could turn the demonstrated threat of another attack into recurring obligations: elephants, manpower, gifts, and acknowledgement of superiority. The return to Ghazna did not necessarily end the relationship established through force. At the same time, the fragmentary evidence does not tell us how durable that relationship was after the first reported fulfilment.
