Mahmud’s campaign against Multan in 1010 was directed against a Muslim ruler and a Muslim religious community. Abu’l-Fath Daud, associated with the Ismaili principality of Multan, was taken prisoner and removed to a fortress outside India. The accompanying repression included killings and the distribution of prisoners among forts, according to the near-contemporary tradition cited by Muhammad Nazim. It is an episode in which political conquest and sectarian persecution were closely connected.[1]

The campaign belongs to the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). It followed the [Narayan expedition of 1009](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1009-narayan), but it also resumed an older struggle: Mahmud had already attacked [Multan in 1006](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1006-multan). The return demonstrates that submission achieved during one expedition did not automatically produce lasting control.

## Multan before the second attack

Multan occupied a strategic position in the river country of the northwest. Its political and commercial connections mattered to anyone moving between Ghazna, the Punjab, and the approaches to the Ganges plain. It was also a religious centre with a history older than the Ghaznavid campaigns. Al-Biruni’s account remembers a celebrated sun image, pilgrimage wealth, changes of rulers, and competing mosques within the same city.[2]

The rulers attacked by Mahmud were Ismaili Muslims. Sunni writers often described them with hostile terms, including the broad designation “Carmathians” or “Qarmatians.” Such vocabulary records the categories used by opponents. It should not be treated as a precise description of every resident’s beliefs or as a neutral verdict on the legitimacy of the community.

Mahmud’s own authority drew support from Sunni religious claims and recognition from the Abbasid caliphate. A rival Muslim principality could therefore be represented as a doctrinal enemy as well as a political obstacle. The first Multan campaign’s account explicitly joins accusations of religious error to disobedience. The two charges reinforced one another in the Ghaznavid presentation of intervention.[3]

This matters for the larger history of the invasions. A map coloured only by a Hindu–Muslim opposition would misdescribe the actors at Multan. It would also conceal the experience of Ismaili victims. Recognising their identity does not diminish violence against Hindu populations elsewhere; it makes the account of Mahmud’s wars more accurate.

## Why Mahmud returned

The attack of 1006 had been followed by demands and arrangements for submission, but Mahmud was pulled back toward conflicts in his western dominions. Nazim explains the renewed expedition as the completion of a subjugation left unfinished when the threat of Ilak Khan required his departure. He dates the return to the beginning of 401 AH, approximately October 1010.[1]

That reconstruction cites Gardizi, a historian writing in the middle of the eleventh century. The Gardizi passages used here are accessed through Nazim’s citations and discussion, not through an independently checked translation of the entire Persian work. This distinction is important because the older Elliot and Dowson appendix gives the impression that the episode depends chiefly on much later chronicles. Gardizi’s evidence, as cited by Nazim, places a near-contemporary witness behind the reconstruction.[1][4]

Nazim suggests that Daud was probably creating renewed disturbance. The word “probably” deserves to survive. There is no surviving declaration from Daud in the sources inspected here explaining his intentions. A decision to resist imposed demands could appear as rebellion from Ghazna and as the preservation of local rule from Multan. The court’s political vocabulary does not settle that disagreement for us.

The history between the two expeditions also involved the appointment and revolt of Nawasa Shah, commonly identified with Sukhpal. His precise jurisdiction and relationship with neighbouring rulers are not entirely clear in the sources. That episode shows the difficulty of maintaining authority through appointed or subordinate rulers. It should not be used to invent a continuous, fully documented Ghaznavid provincial administration throughout the intervening years.[1][3]

## What can be reconstructed of the campaign

The surviving evidence used here supports the return to Multan, the defeat or removal of its ruler, and harsh repression of his religious supporters. It does not supply a detailed contemporary siege diary. No secure day-by-day account survives in the inspected passages identifying each gate attacked, each defensive work, or the location of every confrontation.[1]

This limitation affects how the campaign should be pictured. Multan’s later fortifications, tombs, and gates are valuable records of the city’s long history, but they are not automatically the structures Daud defended. A modern photograph of the fort area cannot be used as architectural proof of the battlefield in 1010. The prominent tomb of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, for example, belongs to a much later period.

The absence of tactical detail does not make the consequences obscure. A ruler was captured, followers were reportedly killed or incarcerated, and religious authority within the city was reorganised. Those acts could transform the political life of Multan without leaving us a reliable account of the attack’s precise formation or duration.

The campaign should therefore be described as a successful coercive intervention, with the extent of permanent administrative control left open. “Conquest” can name the overthrow of a ruler, but it does not by itself prove that every outlying settlement was occupied continuously or that resistance had become impossible.

## Daud’s capture and removal

Nazim’s account, citing Gardizi, says Abu’l-Fath Daud was taken prisoner and confined at Ghurak, also rendered Ghorak. Nazim locates the fortress roughly fifty miles northwest of Kandahar. The essential point is removal: the ruler of Multan was separated from the city and from the political resources on which his authority depended.[1]

The account says he remained there until his death. Nazim’s phrase that he ended his days in peace should not soften the condition into voluntary retirement. He was a prisoner. Even if the surviving source does not describe torture or execution, the loss of freedom and enforced residence were instruments of conquest.

Nor does the evidence permit a dramatic final speech, a detailed description of his cell, or a confidently dated death scene. Daud’s surviving historical role is largely filtered through the writers of the power that defeated him. Acknowledging that imbalance is more responsible than filling the gap with imagined dialogue.

His removal also changed the meaning of the earlier settlement. The first expedition had apparently allowed some accommodation with an existing ruler. The second brought an end to Daud’s ability to govern from Multan. Renewed force could thus replace a tributary arrangement with detention when the conqueror considered the arrangement inadequate or insecure.

## Killings and imprisonment

Nazim reports, on Gardizi’s authority, that thousands of people whom the sources call Carmathians were killed or sent as prisoners to different forts. That is an explicit report of sectarian repression. It should be neither omitted behind a word such as “pacification” nor converted into a precisely verified population count.[1]

The categories in the report are broad. We do not have an individual register establishing how many victims were combatants, religious officials, townspeople, or dependants. The sources inspected here do not give a complete demographic profile. “Thousands,” where retained, must remain a chronicled claim rather than an audited total.

Distribution among forts would have separated prisoners from their local networks. That is a reasonable consequence of the reported action, although the text does not reveal each prisoner’s destination or subsequent life. It would be wrong to assert that every captive was executed, sold, or eventually released. The source supports detention and dispersal; it does not settle every individual fate.

This was violence within the history of Muslim societies as well as part of Mahmud’s invasions of India. The people targeted were not made less vulnerable by sharing Islam with their conqueror. Sectarian boundaries, political allegiance, and rival authority could override that common affiliation. Multan is therefore essential to an account of the campaigns that seeks to identify victims and motives accurately.

## Al-Biruni and the two mosques

Al-Biruni supplies another kind of evidence. Writing around 1030, he describes changes in Multan’s religious buildings across several regimes. He says Jalam ibn Shaiban destroyed the old sun image and killed its priests when the Ismaili rulers established themselves. Those acts belong to an earlier phase in his narrative, not to Mahmud’s second expedition.[2]

This distinction prevents a serious error: the destruction of Multan’s famous sun image should not simply be assigned to Mahmud in 1010. Al-Biruni identifies a different agent. His description of the image as wooden and covered with red leather, with rubies for eyes, also differs from assumptions that every sacred object in these stories was a stone linga or an identical kind of statue.

The scholar then explains that Jalam closed the older mosque, which he associated with an earlier ruling tradition, and established worship in another building. When Mahmud overthrew the Ismaili rulers, the older mosque was restored to Friday worship and the second was allowed to decay. Al-Biruni says the latter site was being used for preparing bundles of henna in his own time.[2]

The passage ties religious authority to the use of urban space. Restoring one congregation’s mosque and abandoning another was a public alteration of legitimacy. It signalled which past was to be revived and which rival establishment was to lose status. This was more than a change of ruler’s name in a chronicle.

Al-Biruni does not date every architectural alteration to a particular day in the 1010 campaign. His evidence should therefore be used for the broader overthrow of the Ismaili regime and its institutional consequences. It corroborates a transformation of public worship without pretending to supply a complete account of the military operation.[2]

## Did persecution end Ismaili power?

The campaign succeeded against Daud, but the later survival of opposition complicates any claim that Mahmud permanently extinguished the community. Nazim notes subsequent resistance associated with Daud’s son after the death of Mahmud’s successor Masud. The observation belongs to a later period, yet it is relevant to the limits of the 1010 outcome.[1]

Killing, captivity, and the replacement of rulers could suppress a political establishment without erasing religious identity. The reappearance of resistance does not reverse the suffering of those attacked in 1010. It demonstrates that violent success and lasting consent were different things.

The same distinction applies to the city itself. Multan continued to have a history after the invasion. Later monuments and institutions should not be presented as though the campaign reduced its entire future to a single catastrophe. A long-lived city could endure severe violence and remain a centre of settlement, commerce, and devotion under later powers.

## The place of Multan in Mahmud’s wars

The second campaign reveals three connected uses of force: eliminating an unreliable political ruler, imposing a preferred religious establishment, and punishing a community represented as doctrinally hostile. Material advantage and the security of routes remained relevant, but the evidence does not permit religious repression to be dissolved into an exclusively economic explanation.

What can be stated firmly is that Daud was removed, captivity and killing formed part of the remembered suppression, and al-Biruni records a public reordering of the city’s mosques. The exact scale of the casualties and the full mechanics of provincial government remain less clear. Keeping these levels of certainty distinct allows the account to be both explicit and responsible.
