The expedition against Nandana was not an effortless advance through a defeated country. Mahmud’s first movement was checked by severe winter weather. The renewed attack encountered an entrenched defender, elephants blocking a narrow approach, and fighting that the court chronicler says lasted several days. The eventual Ghaznavid success brought the loss of a Shahi stronghold and a return march accompanied by people who had been enslaved.[1][2]

This article belongs to the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). It follows the [second Multan campaign of 1010](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1010-multan) and precedes the [Thanesar expedition](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1014-thanesar). Its destination, Nandana in the Salt Range, must not be confused with [Narayan, attacked in 1009](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1009-narayan).

## A Shahi position that still mattered

Earlier defeats had badly damaged Shahi power, but they had not ended all organised resistance. Muhammad Nazim reconstructs a surviving Shahi centre in the Salt Range after the Waihind and Nagarkot campaign. Anandapala was succeeded by his son Trilochanapala, whose family and allies continued to oppose Ghaznavid expansion.[2]

Nandana stood among the approaches through the Salt Range, in present-day Pakistan. Its importance came from the relationship between a defensible hill position and routes across the region. A stronghold could protect a ruler’s remaining authority, provide a refuge, and obstruct an opponent’s movement. Its military significance did not depend on controlling the entire subcontinent.

The sources use several forms of the name. Al-Utbi’s Arabic-derived account appears under Nardin in Elliot and Dowson; Gardizi and Baihaqi use forms closer to Nandana, according to Nazim. The names help explain why older campaign lists can look inconsistent. They do not justify merging this episode with the similarly named Narayan raid several years earlier.[1][2]

The Shahi side is also represented by varying names and titles. Al-Utbi’s Nidar Bhim is identified in modern reconstruction with Bhimapala, Trilochanapala’s son; the epithet is understood as “fearless.” Trilochanapala’s efforts to obtain assistance from Kashmir belong to the wider campaign. Father and son should not be compressed into a single person merely because the court account does not explain their dynasty for a modern reader.[2]

## The winter movement of 1013

Al-Utbi dates Mahmud’s departure to late autumn in 404 AH. He describes snowfall so severe that the roads disappeared and passes were closed. Horses and camels suffered from the cold, and movement became uncertain. He then presents a pause in which supplies were assembled and commanders summoned before campaigning resumed in spring.[1]

Nazim reconstructs the first attempt as a departure around November 1013 followed by a return, then a renewed expedition around March 1014. His account uses the sequence in al-Utbi alongside other evidence. The distinction between a winter halt in the narrative and a reconstructed return to Ghazna is worth retaining: the inspected court text does not provide the same geographical precision at every stage.[1][2]

The weather is not incidental to the military history. An army needed passable roads, fodder, and transport before it could bring its strength to bear. Mahmud’s ability to win battles did not exempt his animals or supplies from mountain conditions. The pause also allowed time for preparation and reinforcement rather than simply suspending an unchanged column on the road.

Al-Utbi’s description is highly literary, including comparisons that turn the reassembled host into an almost immeasurable force. Those comparisons should not be converted into troop totals. The historical substance is that winter obstructed the movement and that the renewed expedition required organised resources.

## Bhimapala closes the approach

In the reconstruction based on several sources, Trilochanapala entrusted the fort’s defence to Bhimapala while seeking support from Kashmir. Al-Utbi describes the defending commander occupying a narrow and difficult pass. Stones reinforced the position, and elephants blocked the approach.[1][2]

This arrangement used terrain to reduce the advantage of an attacker with substantial cavalry. A constricted approach limited how many mounted men could fight at once. Elephants added a formidable obstacle, while hills and prepared works offered protection to the men behind them. The account presents a deliberate defensive choice, not a population waiting passively to be attacked.

Mahmud distributed commands among several officers. Al-Utbi names Amir Nasr, Arslan al-Jazib, Abu Abdallah al-Tai, and Altuntash in the wings, advance, and centre. His descriptions praise their strength and courage, but the named arrangement also suggests differentiated bodies under recognisable leadership rather than one undirected mass.[1]

The composition of the attacking force was varied. The chronicler mentions Daylamite troops and Afghan spearmen in the attack on the difficult position. Such details matter because a simple picture of an exclusively mounted Turkish army obscures the infantry and regional forces needed for fighting in confined ground.[1]

## Several days of fighting

Al-Utbi says the struggle continued for several days. Some defenders were drawn out into the plain, where Ghaznavid cavalry attacked them. After reinforcements arrived, the defending commander brought out a larger force, with the hills behind it and elephants on the wings. The narrative thus records a change from a protected position to a more open engagement.[1]

It would be easy to turn that movement into a confident claim that Bhimapala made an obvious blunder. The sources do not preserve his own assessment. Reinforcements, pressure on supplies, the position of neighbouring forces, or an opportunity perceived on the ground could all affect such a decision, but none should be invented as his documented reason. What survives is the opponent’s account of the movement and its result.

When the elephants advanced, al-Utbi says the attackers directed arrows against their eyes and trunks. The description makes their vulnerability explicit without proving that every elephant reacted identically. The source’s concluding celebration of captured animals confirms their value to the victor, but it provides no independently checked tally for this campaign.[1]

The same passage records serious injury to Abu Abdallah al-Tai. Mahmud sent guards to bring the wounded commander out of the fighting and ordered him placed on an elephant. The episode serves the chronicler’s praise of courage and royal concern, yet it also acknowledges that victory did not mean an absence of danger or wounds among the attacking leadership.[1]

The defenders were defeated. Al-Utbi describes killing in hills, ravines, and watercourses, as well as the seizure of elephants and other property. There is no reliable overall death toll. The violence can be stated plainly without adding a number the source does not provide.

## The fortress and the Kashmir connection

Al-Utbi’s account concentrates on the battle, booty, and religious significance of victory. Nazim supplements it with Gardizi’s account of the siege of Nandana: mining beneath the walls, archery against the defenders, and surrender by a garrison that could not maintain resistance. These details are Gardizi’s as transmitted through Nazim’s reconstruction, not additional sentences supplied by al-Utbi.[2]

The fall of the fort did not end the broader operation. Trilochanapala had sought assistance from Sangramaraja of Kashmir. A force under Tunga came to his aid. Kalhana’s *Rajatarangini*, written in the twelfth century, preserves a Kashmir-side account that is valuable because it comes from a different historical tradition, although it too was composed after the events.[2][3]

Kalhana describes initial success by the Kashmir commander followed by defeat when the invading leader attacked more effectively. The passage criticises Tunga’s judgment and gives Trilochanapala a separate role in continued resistance. It should not be reduced to the Ghaznavid court’s claim that divine favour made victory automatic. The accounts share an outcome while arranging blame and courage differently.[2][3]

The precise relationship between each engagement and each valley needs caution. Nazim places the subsequent confrontation north of the Jhelum and combines the Persian and Kashmir narratives. That synthesis helps explain the campaign, but a modern map should not imply that every battlefield coordinate is secure.

## The captives on the return march

The most disturbing passage in al-Utbi comes after the victory. He says captives became so numerous that their price fell, and that people who had held respected positions at home were reduced to slavery under ordinary shopkeepers. He welcomes this humiliation as an expression of religious triumph.[1]

This is evidence of enslavement, not merely a euphemistic transfer of “booty.” The people taken were deprived of freedom and made available for purchase. The chronicler’s interest in the collapse of status tells us something about his values: degradation that a reader may recognise as suffering becomes a reason for celebration in the victor’s narrative.

The passage does not provide a reliable total or name individual victims. It does not identify the occupation, age, or destination of every captive. Those limits should prevent an invented demographic reconstruction, but they do not weaken the direct evidence that the taking and cheap sale of human beings formed part of the reported outcome.

It also shows how warfare reached beyond the battlefield. Captivity could follow the army into markets and households far from the attacked region. A victory’s consequences were therefore not exhausted when the last formation withdrew from the pass. The forced movement of people was part of the material return from campaigning.

## The temple inscription and a translation error

Al-Utbi includes a story about an inscription found at a temple. In the Elliot and Dowson translation, it claimed an antiquity of fifty thousand years. The chronicler contrasts this with the much shorter sacred chronology accepted by Mahmud’s advisers, who reject the stone’s testimony.[1]

The story is evidence for a dispute over religious history as represented at the Ghaznavid court. It is not proof that the temple was fifty thousand years old. Nor can the inscription now be treated as an independently verified archaeological document simply because a chronicler reports it. Nazim’s discussion even gives a different enormous figure when paraphrasing the episode, another reason not to use the number as established dating.[2]

There is a further problem in the old English wording. Elliot and Dowson render the temple’s sacred object as the “great Budda.” Nazim points out that the underlying word *budd*, related to Persian *but*, means an idol in this context. That wording does not establish that the temple was Buddhist.[2]

The correction has real consequences. A mistaken translation can create an entire religious identification that then travels through later summaries. The responsible description is a temple and sacred image reported in al-Utbi’s narrative, with no confident Buddhist attribution based on this phrase alone.

## A stronghold lost, resistance displaced

Gardizi’s evidence, as cited by Nazim, supplies the appointment of a Ghaznavid officer at Nandana and a return in the summer of 1014. This distinguishes the outcome from a raid that simply removed portable treasure and departed. A garrison or appointed custodian could make the site part of a continuing structure of military control.[2]

Trilochanapala nevertheless survived and continued to appear in the politics of northern India. Nazim reconstructs his movement toward the eastern Punjab and the foothills. A lost fortress and a defeated army did not mean that every Shahi alliance or family member vanished. Subsequent campaigns encountered rulers and relationships reshaped by these earlier defeats.

Nandana’s conquest therefore combined a military breakthrough with a human catastrophe: defensive positions were overcome, a fortress was lost, and captives were sold. At the same time, winter resistance, the duration of the fighting, and the appeal to Kashmir show the limits and challenges faced by the invading force. This is a more substantial history than a simple entry declaring another effortless victory.
