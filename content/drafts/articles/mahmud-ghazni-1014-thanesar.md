The most tangible consequence of Mahmud’s attack on Thanesar appears in al-Biruni’s description of a displaced sacred image. The bronze Cakrasvamin, nearly the size of a person, had been removed from the city that venerated it and lay in the hippodrome at Ghazna. It was there alongside the sacred object later taken from Somnath. The scholar’s account gives the campaign a material aftermath beyond a list of victories: a religious object had become a public sign of its worshippers’ defeat.[1]

Al-Utbi supplies a different part of the story: an army crossing difficult country, a defended river, hard fighting, captured elephants, and plunder. Read together, the accounts reveal military extraction and deliberate religious humiliation. They also require care over the date and the relationship between the battle on the approach and the seizure of the town.[1][2]

## Thanesar as a sacred destination

Thanesar, in present-day Haryana, had a religious importance that the near-contemporary sources explicitly acknowledge. Al-Biruni says Hindus held the city in high veneration and identifies its famous image as Cakrasvamin, explaining the name through the *cakra*, or discus. His description preserves information about the object’s material, size, and religious meaning.[1]

This was not simply a treasury that happened to contain metal. A sacred image could connect worshippers to a place, a ritual tradition, and remembered history. Taking it away therefore damaged an institution’s devotional life as well as transferring a valuable object. The public destination at Ghazna further changed its meaning: an object of honour at home became a trophy in the conqueror’s capital.

Al-Biruni also reports a traditional association with Bharata and ancient wars. That is evidence of the origin story attached to the image, not an independently established date for casting the bronze. A modern account can preserve the tradition without treating sacred chronology as a metallurgical examination.

No photograph of Thanesar’s present religious buildings should be described as an image of Cakrasvamin in its original eleventh-century setting. The text identifies the removed bronze; it does not identify a surviving modern shrine as the same building Mahmud encountered. Modern landscapes help locate a historical city, but they cannot replace the missing object and its original context.

## A date with competing traditions

This article uses 1014–1015, the sequence reconstructed by Muhammad Nazim: departure in autumn 405 AH, approximately October 1014, and return in the following spring. The position after Nandana agrees with al-Utbi’s order, while Nazim cites Ibn al-Athir for the year.[3]

The sources do not all agree. Nazim’s note explicitly says Gardizi places the expedition in 402 AH, corresponding to 1011–1012. Other traditional lists use an earlier date. The disagreement should remain visible rather than disappearing behind an apparently exact calendar entry. Gardizi’s earlier date is especially important because he is near-contemporary; the later dating cannot simply be described as his own statement.[3]

Al-Utbi’s surviving extract provides a narrative but no explicit year for this operation. It is consequently stronger evidence for the reported fighting than for a modern departure day. The use of a two-year title also reflects a campaigning season extending across autumn and spring, rather than the assumption that an Islamic lunar year and a Common Era year share the same boundaries.

These uncertainties do not make the attack imaginary. The displacement of Cakrasvamin is independently reported by al-Biruni. They affect the calendar and the arrangement of the episode within a reconstructed series, not the existence of every action described in it.

## Elephants and religious purpose

Al-Utbi begins with Mahmud learning that the country possessed elephants especially valued for warfare. The chronicler connects the ruler’s possession of them with resistance and then declares the invasion a campaign against idolatry. The passage gives material acquisition and religious purpose together.[2]

The elephants were military resources. Their seizure could weaken one ruler and strengthen another, and their capture was repeatedly counted among Mahmud’s achievements. The source’s claim about a particular breed should remain attributed to it; it does not give a surviving veterinary or breeding record that would establish the animals’ precise origin.

The religious purpose is equally explicit in the narrative. It should not be omitted on the assumption that every declaration of faith concealed an exclusively financial motive. Conversely, a writer’s devotional explanation does not erase the tangible value of animals, wealth, and political submission. These purposes could operate together without being identical.

Nazim also describes an offer attributed to Trilochanapala to deliver fifty elephants if Thanesar were spared. The proposed bargain belongs to the wider source tradition he reconstructs. It illustrates a possible attempt to exchange material resources for the safety of a venerated place; it is not a surviving treaty whose terms can be independently examined.[3]

## The approach and the defended river

Al-Utbi says the army crossed barren country with little or no water before reaching a river passing through difficult ground. The claim that no person had previously traversed the desert belongs to the chronicler’s celebration of an unprecedented achievement. It should not be turned into a literal assertion that the landscape had never supported travellers.[2]

At the river, defenders occupied a protected position behind elephants, with infantry and cavalry. The banks were steep and the bottom stony. The important military point is the combination of water, terrain, and a prepared opposing force. A crossing army would have to negotiate the obstacle before it could deploy all its strength effectively.

The river is not named in the passage. Nazim identifies it with the Sutlej near its emergence from the hills and associates the defending ruler with a figure called Ram in Gardizi. He also discusses possible identifications of the ruler’s territory. These are reconstruction steps; the Arabic-derived extract does not itself label the site with a modern bridge or town.[2][3]

It is therefore safer to call this the river battle on the approach to Thanesar than to picture it automatically at the edge of the city. The expedition could include a major engagement some distance from its eventual sacred destination. Separating those stages prevents an attractive but unsupported single-scene narrative.

## How the crossing was forced

According to al-Utbi, Mahmud sent troops across at two different fords to attack both sides of the defending position. Another force moved along the riverbank against men concentrated in the ravines. The tactic sought to make the defenders confront pressure from several directions rather than relying on a single frontal crossing.[2]

The battle continued into the evening. The chronicler reports that the defenders eventually fled, abandoning elephants that were driven into Mahmud’s camp. One escaped capture, while the largest were reserved for the ruler. This is a specific account of spoils divided after a hard engagement, even though the text does not supply a dependable complete tally.

Al-Utbi describes the water as discoloured by blood and no longer drinkable. Such imagery makes the killing explicit, but it is also a common literary way to magnify victory. It is not an independent environmental measurement from which casualties can be calculated. The account supports severe fighting and deaths, not a scientifically recoverable volume of blood.

The source says darkness limited the pursuit. That detail places a practical boundary on the victor’s opportunity to kill. The author regrets an interruption that a modern reader may recognise as permitting some people to escape. His emotional alignment with the invading army must remain apparent when the incident is retold.

Nazim’s reconstruction also says the attackers’ losses were heavier than those of the defenders, citing al-Utbi and Gardizi. That assessment should remain attributed to the reconstruction, because the inspected extract gives no complete figures on either side. It nevertheless cautions against equating eventual possession of the field with an inexpensive victory. An army could capture the opponent’s elephants and still pay heavily in casualties; the surviving celebration of booty should not make its own losses disappear.[3]

## Plunder and removal from Thanesar

Nazim’s reconstruction distinguishes the river victory from the entry into Thanesar. It says the city’s ruler fled and that Mahmud entered without another battle, plundered the place, and removed Cakrasvamin. For this stage Nazim uses Gardizi and al-Biruni rather than pretending that al-Utbi supplies every detail.[3]

That distinction changes the account of violence. A hard-fought approach does not establish that the city itself underwent an equally prolonged siege. Nor does entry without opposition imply a harmless occupation. Property could be seized and a sacred institution attacked even where an organised garrison did not contest the gate.

Al-Biruni’s independent description is particularly valuable here. He identifies the bronze by name and material and places it at Ghazna. His work was completed around 1030, close enough to the campaigns to provide a stronger chronological anchor than the colourful stories written several centuries later.[1]

The scholar does not claim to have stood beside Mahmud during the assault. His evidence concerns the object and its remembered origin, not a complete eyewitness itinerary. Its strength should be used precisely: it confirms a displaced sacred bronze and the connection to Thanesar, while leaving other battlefield details to other accounts.

## What a public trophy accomplished

Transporting a revered object to a distant capital served more than the acquisition of metal. The image could be displayed where the victor’s subjects, soldiers, visitors, and rivals encountered it. Its presence made an otherwise distant campaign visible within Ghazna’s public space.[1]

Al-Biruni later places the Somnath object beside it. The juxtaposition suggests how separate campaigns could be assembled into a cumulative royal statement. Religious objects from different regions were removed from their communities and brought into a setting controlled by the same ruler. Their shared destination linked victories that had occurred years apart.

This should not be confused with museum preservation. An object displayed as the evidence of another community’s humiliation has a different purpose from an object retained for historical understanding. The point of the removal in these accounts was bound to conquest and the degradation of a rival religious authority.

The bronze’s later fate is not securely supplied in the inspected passages. Its location around al-Biruni’s time does not establish an unbroken chain of custody to a present collection. An image of a different bronze cannot responsibly be labelled as this captured Cakrasvamin merely because its general iconography resembles the description.

## Consequences and limits

The reported outcomes were military defeat on the approach, captured elephants, plunder, and the removal of a major sacred object. The evidence is explicit enough to resist euphemism, but it does not supply a reliable overall death toll, a complete list of destroyed buildings, or a detailed record of permanent Ghaznavid administration in Thanesar.[1][2][3]

Nor did this expedition mean every remaining objective could be reached. The subsequent Kashmir campaign encountered a different relationship of fortifications, mountain routes, and winter conditions. An army successful against one river position could still fail before another kind of obstacle.

Thanesar’s history in this series rests on that combination: a forceful military narrative and an unusually specific record of cultural dispossession.
