Mahmud did not conquer Kashmir. In the expedition conventionally dated 1015–1016, his advance was stopped before a mountain fortress called Lohkot. The siege failed, winter disrupted his communications, and the army withdrew. Later accounts add a dangerous return through water-covered ground in which men died. This episode is essential to the history of his invasions because it records a defended limit to his power rather than another successful raid.[1][2]

It belongs to the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions) and follows the [Thanesar expedition](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1014-thanesar) in the chronology adopted here. Kashmir had already entered the story through assistance offered to Shahi resistance during the [Nandana campaign](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1013-nandana). The failed attempt should remain separate from the [second Kashmir and Lahore sequence of 1021–1022](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1021-kashmir-lahore).

## The political connection with the Shahis

Kashmir was not an isolated landscape outside the politics of the Punjab. Its ruler, Sangramaraja, had supported Trilochanapala against Mahmud. A Kashmir force under Tunga appears in the accounts of the fighting associated with the loss of Nandana. The connection placed the Kashmir court within the network of powers resisting Ghaznavid expansion.[1]

Muhammad Nazim suggests that Mahmud’s subsequent advance was probably intended to punish Sangramaraja for that assistance. The explanation is plausible, but its status matters: it is a historian’s reconstruction, not a surviving declaration by Mahmud specifying the purpose of the expedition. Strategic access, the subordination of a neighbouring power, and the continuation of earlier conflict could all have mattered without leaving us a ranked list of motives.[1]

The previous conflict also helps explain why a mountain approach mattered. Defeating a Shahi force in one region did not eliminate the possibility of refuge, assistance, or renewed alliances elsewhere. Control could remain incomplete as long as a neighbouring court retained the ability to offer support beyond the reach of a particular victory.

None of this establishes a unified command joining every opponent of Mahmud. Kashmir had its own ruler, interests, and military decisions. The Shahis had theirs. Their cooperation should be described as an alliance or intervention supported by the sources, without turning several courts into a single modern nation-state.

## Lohkot and the mountain approach

The fortress’s name appears as Lohkot, Loharkotta, and related forms. Nazim, drawing on Gardizi and Aurel Stein’s interpretation of Kashmir geography, identifies it with Loharin. He reconstructs an advance through the Jhelum region and the valley of the Tohi toward the approaches to the Tosa Maidan pass.[1]

This geography should be presented as a reconstruction. The nineteenth-century Elliot and Dowson appendix proposed a different identification and openly called the location difficult to fix. Their uncertainty cannot be erased by adopting whichever fort in a modern photograph looks most dramatic. A precise label needs a defensible relationship to the textual and topographical evidence.[2]

What is more secure than every coordinate is the military relationship: a fortified position obstructed passage toward Kashmir. Mahmud could not obtain his objective merely by winning a battle on an open plain. He had to get past a place whose garrison and terrain restricted his movement.

That is the practical significance of a mountain fortress. An army passing through a narrow corridor might leave a hostile stronghold behind it only at substantial risk to movement and supply. The sources do not preserve Mahmud’s full operational assessment, but the decision to besiege Lohkot indicates that he could not treat it as irrelevant.

## What survives of the siege

Nazim dates the expedition to 406 AH, beginning in 1015 and ending with a return in spring 1016. For the siege he cites Gardizi, a near-contemporary historian. The Gardizi material here is consulted through Nazim’s references and discussion; an independently inspected complete English translation has not been used.[1]

The reconstruction says the fort had a reputation for being exceptionally difficult to take. Mahmud invested it, but about a month of effort did not produce a successful capture. Heavy snow then cut his communications, and he raised the siege. This is the central sequence supported by the evidence: attempt, resistance, deteriorating conditions, withdrawal.[1]

The later Firishta narrative, quoted in Elliot and Dowson, similarly emphasises the fort’s height and strength, increasing cold, snowfall, and reinforcements reaching the defenders from Kashmir. Firishta wrote in the early seventeenth century, around six centuries after the campaign. His version may preserve older material, but it should not be given the authority of a correspondent watching the siege.[2]

No inspected source supplies a complete engineering account. We do not know the dimensions of the assault works, the daily expenditure of arrows, or the number of defenders on each wall. Statements that a particular tower was mined or that a named gate was stormed would therefore exceed the evidence. The failure itself is sufficiently consequential without imaginary siege choreography.

## Why snow could change the outcome

Winter affected the campaign through movement and communication. A mountain army depended on routes for supplies, messages, reinforcement, and eventual withdrawal. Snow that obstructed those routes could make continued presence more dangerous even if the besieging force remained formidable in a direct encounter.[1]

The issue was not simply whether individual soldiers could endure cold. Horses and transport animals needed food and passable ground. An army carrying weapons, baggage, and provisions had different requirements from a small group travelling on foot. The sources do not provide a full supply inventory, so those requirements should be explained as general constraints rather than reconstructed as a fictional daily ration schedule.

The defenders also had an advantage that cannot be reduced to weather. They held the position Mahmud needed to overcome. In Firishta’s later narrative, reinforcements came from Kashmir while the attackers’ situation worsened. Terrain, a functioning garrison, and support from the interior combined to make time work against the besieger.[2]

It would be misleading to say snow alone “defeated” an otherwise uncontested army. The army remained outside a fort that had resisted its efforts. Weather made that unresolved military problem more costly and eventually untenable. The outcome belonged to both the effectiveness of the defended position and the conditions surrounding it.

## Retreat and the water-covered plain

Nazim’s reconstruction adds a dangerous return: Mahmud lost his way in the hills and reached ground covered with water. Troops died, and the ruler escaped with difficulty. Nazim cites later authors, including Ibn al-Athir, for this portion; he uses Gardizi for the subsequent wintering and return to Ghazna.[1]

Firishta’s account in Elliot and Dowson offers a similarly alarming picture of water in every direction and an army unable to extricate itself for several days. Its dramatic language should remain attributed. The inspected evidence does not permit an exact reconstruction of the flooded area, the number drowned, or the day on which each body of men escaped.[2]

Older editors tried to identify the water with specific rivers and plains. Nazim proposes another topographical possibility in a note. Those suggestions are useful research leads, not a licence to draw one certain retreat line on a modern map. The source tradition supports serious difficulty involving water; the exact location remains less secure.

This episode also requires a distinction between battlefield killing and deaths during withdrawal. The sources do not establish that every man lost was slain by Kashmir troops. Some may have died through conditions on the route as the narratives describe. Equally, describing the losses as environmental does not turn the failed invasion into an event without military responsibility or strategic consequences.

## Winter in the Punjab

According to Gardizi as used by Nazim, Mahmud spent the remaining winter months in the Punjab before returning to Ghazna in spring, approximately March 1016. The campaign therefore did not end in an immediate uninterrupted ride from the failed siege to the capital.[1]

Wintering away from Ghazna placed demands on the army and on the territory supporting it. The inspected passage does not itemise requisitions or name households affected by its presence. It would be wrong to invent a specific plunder operation for every place where the force paused. The narrower conclusion is that withdrawal from Lohkot still required a prolonged management of troops beyond the principal objective.

The distinction also helps explain the campaign’s two-year date. An expedition launched in 1015 could continue into 1016 through retreat and winter quarters. A list that assigns each campaign a single year may obscure this duration and make separate stages appear to be separate invasions.

## A gap in the victory chronicle

Al-Utbi does not supply a continuous account of this failed siege. His history celebrates Mahmud’s successes and gives only indirect material relevant to Kashmir in this part of the sequence. Elliot and Dowson noticed the omission but relied heavily on much later authors when discussing the expedition.[2]

The near-contemporary Gardizi evidence cited by Nazim improves that older picture. It means the first Kashmir attempt should not be dismissed as a story recorded only six centuries later. At the same time, Gardizi’s testimony should not be silently merged with every detail supplied by Firishta. Their different dates and distances from the event remain important to the strength of individual claims.[1][2]

The sources also disagree over the year: the *Tabaqat-i Akbari* places the attempt in 407 AH, while the reconstruction used here follows 406 AH. This is one reason an apparently tidy seventeen-campaign calendar cannot be treated as an enumeration delivered intact by a single contemporary author. The campaign’s failed outcome is clearer than every detail of its modern numbering.

## Kashmir beyond Mahmud’s reach

Al-Biruni’s broader account of the effects of Mahmud’s wars includes an important observation. He says learning had retreated from conquered regions to places beyond the conquerors’ reach, including Kashmir and Benares. His description is a retrospective judgment about displacement and intellectual life, not a census of every scholar who moved.[3]

The statement nevertheless fits the central limit illustrated here: Kashmir remained outside Mahmud’s successful conquest. It could shelter institutions and connections that were not easily absorbed by victory elsewhere. That survival did not mean the region was untouched by surrounding conflict; the alliance with the Shahis and the siege at its approaches show otherwise.

Al-Biruni’s wording should not be expanded into a claim that all learning disappeared from every conquered place or that every scholar migrated to Kashmir. He names destinations and describes a disruptive process; he does not enumerate institutions or people. The important relationship is between the security of a region beyond the army’s reach and the possibility of maintaining intellectual life there. Its scale cannot be calculated from this passage alone.[3]

The failure also did not permanently end Mahmud’s ambitions. He attempted the Kashmir approach again in the sequence dated 1021–1022, with another unsuccessful siege. The later attempt should be studied on its own evidence rather than used to erase the earlier one as an accidental duplication.[1]

The first expedition therefore changes the shape of the series. Mahmud’s forces could inflict severe destruction and carry away wealth and people, but their power had geographical, logistical, and military limits. At Lohkot, those limits were defended successfully.
