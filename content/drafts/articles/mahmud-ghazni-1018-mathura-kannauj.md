In the winter of 1018–1019, Mahmud’s army crossed into the Ganges–Yamuna region and attacked a succession of political and religious centres. The campaign was not one battle. It included submission under threat at Baran, a deadly engagement with Kulchand, the plunder and burning of temples at Mathura, the seizure of Kannauj’s fortifications, attacks on other strongholds, and a pursuit ending in killing and captivity at Sharwa. The return brought prisoners into markets extending far beyond northern India.[1]

Al-Utbi’s near-contemporary account is unusually explicit. It describes admiration for Mathura’s architecture followed by an order to burn and level the temples. It also describes captives sold cheaply and dispersed to Khurasan, Iraq, and Transoxiana. These are the court chronicler’s own claims, made in celebration rather than condemnation. They provide evidence of violence and enslavement while requiring caution over enormous numbers and triumphant religious language.[1]

This account forms part of the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). It follows the [failed first Kashmir attempt of 1015–1016](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1015-kashmir) and leads to the [renewed eastern and Chandela campaigning](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1019-chandela). The series keeps these episodes separate for reading without claiming that every boundary in the traditional invasion lists is equally secure.

## Preparing an eastern expedition

Al-Utbi begins with preparation after Mahmud’s return from Khwarazm. Spies were sent toward the Indian frontier, horses and troops were allowed time to recover, and a further campaign was planned. This opening matters: the expedition was not simply an improvised pursuit from the previous battlefield. Intelligence, rest, and a decision about the next objective preceded movement.[1]

The chronicler says volunteers from Transoxiana and neighbouring regions wanted service in a religious war. His reported twenty thousand is not an audited muster total. What the passage establishes more securely is the way a distant campaign could be represented as an opportunity for religious merit as well as military reward. The army’s participants were recruited into a framework that celebrated conquest and the defeat of rival worship.

Al-Utbi also makes extravagant claims about the extent of earlier conquest. They should not be adopted as a literal map of Ghaznavid government. Mahmud had not brought the entire subcontinent under continuous administration; the failure in Kashmir alone demonstrates a limit. Military reach, tributary agreements, garrisons, and direct rule were different forms of power.

The approaching campaign would extend that reach toward major river cities. Mathura and Kannauj were not interchangeable targets. Their political and religious histories differed, as did the circumstances of the army’s arrival. The narrative joins them through the itinerary of the invading force, not because their inhabitants formed a single army or polity.

## Crossing the rivers

The route required repeated crossings of the Punjab rivers before the army reached the Yamuna. Al-Utbi names the Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej in forms transmitted through Arabic and Persian. He emphasises their depth and the danger posed to horses and baggage animals.[1]

The chronicler dates the Yamuna crossing to 20 Rajab 409 AH, rendered in the edition as 2 December 1018. The date is one of the campaign’s stronger chronological anchors. It is more useful than an attempt to derive a precise departure day from a later writer’s general reference to spring or the campaigning season.[1][2]

Local rulers and guides appear in the account. One figure associated with routes toward Kashmir offers submission and guidance. That detail shows how an invading army could use regional knowledge rather than advance wholly independently of local political choices. It does not prove universal voluntary cooperation by the people of every territory crossed.

The source’s marching descriptions also emphasise distance, early movement, and difficult terrain. We should preserve the logistical importance without turning the prose into an invented travel diary. The inspected evidence does not establish a modern road for every stage or the exact place where each river was crossed.

## Baran: submission under the sword

At Baran, al-Utbi names a ruler called Hardat. The chronicle says he feared for his life as Mahmud approached and concluded that conversion to Islam offered the safest course. It then reports his appearance with ten thousand men declaring acceptance of the religion.[1]

The numerical claim requires attribution. We do not have a register of ten thousand individuals or evidence allowing us to reconstruct each person’s belief. But the source’s explanation of the ruler’s fear makes coercion integral to the episode. A profession of faith made when military punishment threatens is not evidence of an entirely unpressured religious decision.

The account celebrates this outcome as divine assistance to Mahmud. A modern narrative need not share that evaluation to report the event. It can say plainly that the court writer depicts threatened violence producing submission and conversion, while leaving the long-term religious lives of the people concerned unresolved.

Baran also demonstrates an alternative to immediate battle. A local ruler could try to avoid attack through compliance. That choice might preserve lives in the short term while demanding political and religious concessions. The source leaves no basis for assuming that every ruler faced identical conditions or that submission guaranteed the same treatment everywhere.

## Kulchand’s resistance and death

The next major engagement in al-Utbi’s narrative concerns Kulchand. The source presents him as a powerful ruler with wealth, elephants, strong fortifications, and troops. His position was associated with forest and a nearby river. The name and site have been identified in different ways by later historians; an exact modern battlefield should not be asserted without further evidence.[1][2]

Mahmud’s advance guard forced its way through the wooded approach. Fighting followed with close use of swords and spears. When the defenders could no longer hold, some attempted escape across the river. Al-Utbi reports people killed, captured, or drowned during this flight.[1]

He claims nearly fifty thousand killed or drowned. That is a court chronicler’s total, not an independently checked casualty count. The enormity of the figure should not become the foundation for a precise demographic calculation. The reported forms of harm—battle deaths, killing during flight, drowning, and captivity—are substantial even when the total remains uncertain.

The same source says Kulchand killed his wife and then himself with a dagger. The account should not romanticise her death as a chosen sacrifice: the text does not preserve her consent or perspective. Nor can a single reported act be made evidence for a universal practice among every community attacked by Mahmud. It is a particular violent ending recorded by an enemy chronicler.[1]

Al-Utbi claims 185 elephants were taken. As elsewhere, these were material prizes as well as signs of victory. A defending ruler’s loss of them could affect his remaining military capacity, while their transfer advertised the invader’s success. The number belongs to the source and should remain attached to it.

## Mathura’s architecture in the conqueror’s account

Mathura receives a striking description. Al-Utbi presents a river city with strong stone walls, gates built on substantial foundations, numerous masonry religious buildings, iron fastenings, and wooden structural supports. At its centre stood a temple he regarded as beyond adequate description.[1]

He then gives the substance of a letter attributed to Mahmud praising the extraordinary cost and time that would be required to reproduce the central building. Its enormous valuation and two-hundred-year construction estimate belong to admiration expressed through hyperbole. They cannot be treated as a surviving contractor’s budget or an exact archaeological chronology.

The admiration is nevertheless significant. The narrative does not portray the buildings as worthless or artistically negligible. Their destruction followed an acknowledgement of exceptional workmanship. Cultural achievement and vulnerability to attack were thus visible in the same account.

The description also links architectural splendour to sacred objects and precious materials. Al-Utbi lists gold and silver images, rubies, a sapphire, and impressive metal weights. Those lists show the categories in which a court writer represented religious wealth. They are not independently audited inventories, and the passage’s apparently impossible details should not be silently converted into architectural fact.[1]

No surviving modern photograph of Mathura’s ghats can show this central temple as it stood in 1018. Later paintings and present-day riverfront views can illustrate the city’s enduring setting, provided their captions state their dates. They should not be used to make an unsupported identification of the attacked building.

## The order to burn and level the temples

Al-Utbi states that Mahmud ordered the temples burned with naphtha and fire and levelled to the ground. This is a direct report of deliberate destruction of religious buildings, made by a writer who approved of the campaign. It should not be softened into an unspecified “visit” or reduced to the taking of movable wealth alone.[1]

At the same time, the statement is a textual claim whose exact extent cannot be measured simply by repeating the word “all.” The inspected passage does not provide a plan matching each destroyed building to an excavated site. We can report the order and the chronicler’s representation of its execution without claiming a complete archaeological reconstruction of the city afterward.

The attack joined appropriation and destruction. Precious objects were described as convertible into weighed metal, while the buildings that gave them a sacred setting were burned. For worshippers, the loss was therefore not just financial: it damaged the places and objects through which religious life was organised.

Religious hostility is explicit in the court narrative, but it operated with practical incentives. The seizure of bullion, gems, elephants, and captives rewarded the army and reinforced royal power. An interpretation that excludes either declared iconoclasm or material extraction would leave out evidence the source itself supplies.

## Kannauj: fortifications, flight, and plunder

After Mathura, the army proceeded toward Kannauj. The narrative gives 8 Shaban 409 AH for arrival, reconstructed as 20 December 1018. Al-Utbi says the ruler had fled across the Ganges, leaving the invaders to approach a complex of seven forts associated with the river.[1][2]

The ruler’s name is a problem in the old translation. Al-Utbi appears as naming a Rai Jaipal, while the historical ruler of Kannauj in this context is usually identified as Rajyapala. The translated form must not be used to merge him with the Shahi rulers of the northwest. The source’s naming difficulty and the identification made by modern historians are separate matters.[1][2]

Al-Utbi says the seven forts were taken in one day and that Mahmud allowed his soldiers to plunder and take prisoners. He describes inhabitants fleeing, being scattered, or killed if they did not escape. Again, this is the victorious chronicler’s account; it does not supply a complete population register or distinguish every civilian from every combatant.[1]

The text also claims nearly ten thousand temples at Kannauj. That number should not become a verified count of separate monumental buildings. It expresses the chronicler’s picture of a great religious centre and may include categories not recoverable from the English wording. Its rhetorical scale does not need to be accepted literally to recognise the significance of the reported assault on the city.

Flight by the ruler did not protect the inhabitants from plunder. The passage therefore resists an easy division between places that fought and places that submitted or were abandoned. Royal survival, military resistance, and the treatment of residents were related but different questions.

## Munj and Asi: violence beyond the two famous cities

The expedition continued through other fortified places. Al-Utbi calls Munj a Brahman stronghold and describes opposition followed by desperate flight from high openings and battlements, with many deaths. The brief account should not be expanded into a detailed reconstruction of ritual suicide or a precise catalogue of victims that the source does not provide.[1]

At Asi, the chronicler describes a ruler called Chandal Bhor, a forested defensive setting, and extensive ditches. The ruler fled. Mahmud then ordered the destruction of five forts, and the narrative explicitly connects demolition with inhabitants buried in the ruins, while members of the garrison were killed, plundered, or imprisoned.[1]

These are severe claims, not merely an announcement that territory changed hands. They also remain claims transmitted in a celebratory account. There is no inspected excavation report here demonstrating the fate of each fort or allowing a casualty total to be assigned to the ruins.

The lesser-known places matter because the campaign’s effects extended beyond Mathura and Kannauj. A title centred on famous cities can conceal the sequence of attacks, pursuits, and local collapses along the way. The army’s return wealth accumulated through several episodes rather than a single isolated seizure.

## Sharwa and the politics of flight

Al-Utbi’s account of Chand Rai at Sharwa introduces relationships among regional rulers. It describes earlier warfare, an attempted marriage alliance involving a son called Bhimpal, and detention after the proposed alliance. Names and identifications are difficult, but the narrative clearly portrays a region of rival courts rather than one coordinated defensive command.[1]

Advice to flee from Mahmud appears in a letter attributed to Bhimpal. Like other letters embedded in medieval chronicles, it should be read as a text transmitted by the author, not assumed to be an independently surviving original document. It shows how the chronicler represented Mahmud’s reputation as a force shaping local decisions.

Chand Rai withdrew with treasure and elephants into difficult country. Mahmud captured the fort but remained dissatisfied because the ruler had escaped. He pursued through forest and stony ground. The account dates the attack on the fleeing party to shortly before midnight on 25 Shaban, rendered as 6 January 1019.[1]

Al-Utbi describes killing and captivity in the surprise attack, followed by days spent searching bodies for valuables. This is not a harmless recovery of abandoned property. The source explicitly joins the pursuit of a ruler and his wealth to lethal violence against the group caught with him.

Its story of elephants approaching Mahmud almost voluntarily turns captured animals into signs of divine favour. That literary flourish should remain recognisable as the chronicler’s interpretation. It cannot establish that the animals understood or chose a religious allegiance.

## Captives became merchandise

The concluding account of prisoners is among the most important passages in the campaign narrative. Al-Utbi says captives were sold for between two and ten dirhams, taken to Ghazna, and purchased by merchants arriving from distant cities. He describes their dispersal into Transoxiana, Iraq, and Khurasan.[1]

These are reported prices, not a complete market series. They cannot be converted responsibly into a precise present-day currency value without assumptions the passage does not support. Their significance is the chronicler’s claim that people had become so plentiful as saleable captives that the price was low.

The source explicitly presents rich and poor, and people of different appearance, as reduced to a common condition of slavery. That is a social consequence beyond the temporary capture of soldiers on a battlefield. Personal status, family connection, and local standing could be broken when people were removed and sold.[1]

The text does not name those individuals or tell us each eventual destination. It does not permit a confident account of every occupation, household, or later generation. But these limits should not become an excuse to hide slavery behind “booty.” The involuntary transfer and sale of people are precisely what the account describes.

The markets also show the campaign’s geographic reach. Violence in the Ganges–Yamuna region could supply people to purchasers across a much wider political and commercial world. An army’s return to Ghazna was therefore not the end of the captives’ displacement; it could be a stage in further forced movement.

## What the campaign achieved—and what it did not establish

The expedition brought wealth, animals, prisoners, and prestige to Mahmud. It also left destroyed religious buildings, disrupted courts, deaths, flight, and enslavement in the places attacked. Al-Utbi’s celebratory account provides evidence of both sides of that outcome, even though it values them very differently from the communities that suffered.[1]

It does not prove continuous Ghaznavid administration over every city reached. A raid could penetrate far, devastate a place, and withdraw without creating a stable province. The later politics of Kannauj and the Chandelas make that distinction especially important. Military devastation did not end every regional contest or establish a single durable settlement for the entire plain.[2]

The campaign also demonstrates why original sources should be read explicitly. Their evidence supports the burning of temples, killings, captivity, and slave sales. Their enormous totals, confused names, religious insults, and literary miracles require scrutiny. Accuracy depends on preserving both the disturbing actions and the limits of what can be established about them.
