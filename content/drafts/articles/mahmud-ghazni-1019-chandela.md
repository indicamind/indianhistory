The destruction of temples at Mathura and the plundering of Kannauj did not finish Mahmud of Ghazni’s wars in northern India. The next sequence of events involved surviving Shahi rulers, the politics of the middle Ganges plain, and a powerful Chandela kingdom farther south. Yet this is also where an apparently simple list of invasions becomes difficult to defend. One near-contemporary narrative describes a dangerous river crossing. Gardizi, consulted through Nazim, and much later histories preserve another strand concerning Chandela power. The later version describes an expedition against a ruler called Nanda after the king of Kannauj was killed for submitting to Mahmud. These accounts overlap, but they are not identical.[1][2][3]

This article belongs to the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). It follows the [Mathura and Kannauj campaign of 1018–1019](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1018-mathura-kannauj). The dates in the title deliberately express a range: a campaign placed in 410 AH by one later historian is placed in 412 AH by another, while al-Utbi’s surviving narrative supplies no year for its river action. The safe task is to explain what each account actually says before deciding how much of a single campaign can be reconstructed.[1]

## The landscape after the raids

Mahmud had returned from his eastern expedition with wealth, elephants, and captives. Al-Utbi, a writer closely associated with the Ghaznavid court, describes the sale of prisoners and their dispersal into Khurasan, Iraq, and Transoxiana. He also presents the destruction of sacred buildings as a triumph of Islam. The consequences of the expedition were therefore not confined to the removal of treasure. People lost freedom, religious institutions lost buildings and possessions, and rulers had to make decisions under the demonstrated threat of another invasion.[2]

There was no single Indian government to negotiate for all these territories. The Shahi dynasty had fought the Ghaznavids in the northwest. Kannauj belonged to a different political setting. The Chandelas ruled from strongholds in the region now associated with Bundelkhand. A ruler’s submission, flight, or resistance could affect neighbours without giving those neighbours the same interests. A king who avoided immediate destruction by accepting Mahmud’s demands might appear dangerously accommodating to another power. Conversely, the claim that an enemy had killed a submissive ruler offered Mahmud a useful explanation for renewed intervention.[1]

The later story of punishment for Kannauj’s submission must be read against this fragmentation. It does not establish a united national command or a campaign fought on behalf of every religious community. It describes, through the language of its chroniclers, several competing courts and an invading ruler exploiting their relationships. The distinction matters because a retrospective map can make these powers look much more coordinated than the evidence allows.

## A near-contemporary account: the river called Rahib

Al-Utbi’s account, translated in the nineteenth century by Elliot and Dowson, begins after another expedition against the Afghans. Mahmud turns toward Hind, granting protection to those who submit and killing those who oppose him. The historian says he obtains booty before reaching a river called the Rahib. Its depth and muddy bottom make crossing difficult; men remove their armour before entering the water.[2]

On the opposite bank stands a ruler named Puru Jaipal, with soldiers and elephants. This spelling belongs to the translated source. It should not be silently converted into whichever familiar ruler a modern narrative requires. Names and titles were transmitted through Arabic and Persian manuscripts, and the different versions of these campaigns repeatedly confuse or alter them. The nineteenth-century editors themselves struggled to reconcile the Jaipals who appear in the Shahi and Kannauj stories.[1]

The river separates two armies and gives the defending force a practical advantage. Mahmud’s soldiers cannot simply use their usual mounted charge while struggling through deep water. Al-Utbi says that inflated skins were prepared and that a first group of eight men crossed. The opposing ruler sent troops and five elephants to prevent their landing, but the swimmers’ archery defeated that attempt. More Ghaznavid soldiers then crossed, followed by the army.[2]

These details are valuable evidence of how the chronicler imagined and celebrated a difficult military operation. They identify an obstacle, a crossing method, an advance party, and support from the remaining force. But the numerical neatness of eight heroes and the claim that the whole army arrived without even a horse’s hair being harmed also belong to a victory narrative. The account is not a surviving operations report that can be checked against a defending commander’s version.

Once across, Mahmud orders a mounted attack. Al-Utbi says some opponents are killed, others captured, others wounded and asking for protection, and the rest dispersed. He reports the seizure of 270 elephants. That number should remain attached to the chronicler: it shows the place of elephants in the representation of success, but it is not an independently audited tally.[2]

## What al-Utbi does not say

The absences are important. This account does not name a Chandela ruler called Nanda. It does not explain the campaign as revenge for the killing of Kannauj’s king. It does not say Mahmud stormed Kalinjar. It does not place this action securely in either 1019 or 1021. Gardizi and later material may illuminate these omissions, but it cannot be inserted into al-Utbi’s text and then cited as though he supplied the combined story.[1][2]

Even the river is uncertain. In their appendix, Elliot and Dowson discuss whether the Rahib should be identified with the Ramganga or the Sai. Other histories set a similar crossing on the Yamuna. A mistaken river identification changes the inferred route substantially. It can move an army from one side of the Ganges plain to another and alter which political centre seems to be threatened.[1]

Readers can therefore accept that the source reports a contested river crossing without pretending that every modern place on an illustrated route has been proved. A route line drawn with precision can conceal uncertainty rather than resolve it. Where the text is uncertain, a map should display a broad zone or alternative possibilities.

## The later Chandela account

An earlier strand also survives. Gardizi, as discussed by Nazim from *Zayn al-akhbar*, pp. 76–78, names Ganda, records the demand for submission and conversion, and describes his night withdrawal and the taking of elephants. Thus the confrontation is not known only from late-sixteenth-century writing. Its reported numbers also vary: Gardizi’s version, through Nazim, gives 145,000 infantry where Nizam al-Din gives 105,000. Neither total is an independently verified muster. The following paragraphs examine the directly accessible later version separately; its overlap with the early strand does not remove the problem of aligning either account with al-Utbi’s undated Rahib action.[3]

Nizam al-Din Ahmad’s *Tabaqat-i Akbari*, written in the late sixteenth century, provides a different framework. In its account, Mahmud hears that a ruler called Nanda has killed the king of Kannauj because that king acknowledged and submitted to him. Mahmud consequently advances against Nanda’s territory. This author places the expedition in 410 AH.[1]

The story then includes another encounter with Puru Jaipal at the Yamuna. Eight Ghaznavid guards cross the river, throw the opposing army into confusion, and proceed against a town called Bari. Finding it undefended, they plunder it and pull down temples. The eight-man episode is conspicuously similar to the crossing in al-Utbi, but similarities do not settle whether the later writer has preserved additional information, relocated an older episode, or combined more than one event.[1]

Afterward Mahmud approaches Nanda. The later history gives Nanda an immense army: 36,000 horsemen, 105,000 infantry, and 640 elephants. It says Mahmud first sends an envoy demanding submission and acceptance of Islam. Nanda refuses. Mahmud views the army from an eminence, becomes troubled by its size, and prays for success.[1]

These troop figures and the description of Mahmud’s feelings should be attributed explicitly. They are not numbers from a contemporary muster roll or observations recorded by someone standing beside him. The scene serves a literary purpose: a threatening host makes the eventual outcome demonstrate divine favour. That does not require readers to dismiss every event, but it does require them to separate a possible campaign from the dramatic explanation attached to it centuries later.

## Withdrawal rather than a documented decisive battle

The *Tabaqat-i Akbari* says Nanda leaves during the night with a small group, abandoning baggage and equipment. Mahmud, suspicious of an ambush, examines the ground before ordering plunder. The text reports that 580 elephants are taken in neighbouring woods and that the invaders return to Ghazna with booty.[1]

This is not a detailed account of a great pitched battle in which Mahmud annihilates the Chandela army. The central action is the opposing ruler’s withdrawal and the seizure of what remains. A story in which both rulers hesitate or avoid a full engagement should not be rewritten into an uncomplicated battlefield conquest. Equally, the withdrawal in the later account does not prove that no fighting occurred anywhere on the march: the same text describes violence at the river and the destruction of temples at Bari.

The reported elephants also require care. The source claims 640 in the army and 580 captured; the near-contemporary river narrative claims 270. Combining those totals would manufacture a larger haul that no single source reports. Choosing the most impressive number while suppressing the other account would be equally misleading. The differences are part of the evidence readers should see.

## Nanda, Ganda, and Vidyadhara

Modern discussions connect this campaign with the Chandela rulers Ganda and Vidyadhara. The translated chronicles’ name Nanda cannot, by itself, determine which ruler held effective command or how authority passed between generations. Nor can a spelling resemblance substitute for inscriptions and dynastic chronology. The terms “Chandela campaign” and “Nanda in the later account” are consequently more secure here than an unqualified assertion that every source names the same individual.[1]

The king of Kannauj is another difficulty. The historical episode is often discussed in relation to Rajyapala, but al-Utbi’s rendered names in the preceding expedition are confusing. Elliot and Dowson even proposed a connection between the Shahi Jaipal tradition and Kannauj that should not be adopted merely because it appears in an old reference work. A nineteenth-century editor’s solution is a hypothesis, not an additional medieval witness.[1]

The story that the Kannauj ruler was punished for submitting also invites an attractive but hazardous simplification: that Mahmud immediately marched to avenge a loyal vassal. The later chronicle supplies that explanation. It does not provide a surviving letter of appeal, a contemporary treaty defining the relationship, or testimony from the Chandela court about its reason for war. The political motive can be reported as the tradition’s account, while its documentary limits remain visible.

## Why the date remains disputed

Elliot and Dowson place this material under a thirteenth expedition dated 412 AH, while acknowledging that al-Utbi gives no year and Nizam al-Din gives 410 AH. They prefer the later dating associated with Firishta. They also discuss whether the river episode and a campaign toward Kalinjar were actually one expedition.[1]

Muhammad Nazim subsequently revisited this chronology using the near-contemporary Gardizi. His reconstruction places Mahmud’s departure in autumn 410 AH, or October 1019, and follows the expedition through the river encounter and confrontation with the Chandela force. This provides a stronger chronological basis than simply accepting the much later Firishta’s date. It makes 1019–1020 the preferable working date, while the title’s wider range alerts readers to the older disagreement and the unresolved alignment of all the narratives.[3]

Islamic lunar years do not correspond neatly to a single Common Era year. Differences of one AH year, alternative identifications of an episode, and winter campaigns spanning parts of two solar years can produce apparently contradictory modern lists. A chronology that prints “1019” beside a polished battle narrative may therefore look more settled than the underlying evidence.

The separate [Gwalior and Kalinjar article](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1022-gwalior-kalinjar) covers the later expedition dated 1022–1023 in Nazim’s reconstruction. Keeping the episodes separate for reading does not mean the surviving evidence proves every boundary used in a traditional seventeen-invasion list.

## Violence, submission, and the purposes of war

Both versions connect military movement with extraction. Al-Utbi describes booty, prisoners, and elephants; the later Chandela account describes plundered equipment and animals after a withdrawal. Both also frame the conflict religiously. The later account’s demand for conversion, and its description of temple destruction at Bari, cannot be removed without altering what the text says.[1][2]

These purposes were capable of operating together. Taking elephants could strengthen subsequent campaigns. Seizing wealth rewarded an army and magnified royal power. Demanding submission weakened an opponent. Presenting destruction as a victory of faith legitimised the conqueror and gave his followers a language in which violence became merit. Readers do not have to choose a single motive that excludes all the others.

The human result is also clearer when administrative terms are made concrete. “Booty” could include a person. A prisoner might be sold away from home; an elephant represented the forced transfer of a major resource; a burned temple meant lost possessions and damaged worship as well as a celebrated royal exploit. Al-Utbi’s approving tone does not erase these consequences. It makes the historian’s own position especially important to disclose.

At the same time, the evidence here is insufficient for a precise casualty total. The account of the river lists categories of killed, captured, and fleeing people without a reliable overall count. The later Chandela narrative’s huge army should not be transformed into an equally huge death toll. Explicit history names violence where it is reported and stops where the surviving evidence stops.

## An unresolved episode in a continuing war

The most defensible reconstruction is limited but substantial: Ghaznavid pressure continued after Kannauj; a near-contemporary source describes a successful and violent river operation against a ruler rendered as Puru Jaipal; Gardizi, consulted through Nazim, and later histories connect renewed campaigning with Chandela power and the fate of Kannauj’s king. They disagree over dates and add details that cannot all be independently confirmed.[1][2]

The uncertainty does not make the campaign unimportant. It reveals how a court chronicle, later compilations, and modern numbered lists produce different kinds of historical knowledge. A reader who knows where a story comes from can recognise the difference between evidence of a reported action and evidence of how later generations wanted that action remembered.
