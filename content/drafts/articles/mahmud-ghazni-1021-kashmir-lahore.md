Mahmud of Ghazni’s campaigns were devastating, but they were not an uninterrupted succession of conquered kingdoms. Kashmir presents a particularly clear limit. In the campaign conventionally dated 1021–1022, he again approached the mountain fortress called Lohkot. He failed to take it, abandoned the siege, and returned toward the Punjab. The valley beyond did not become his conquest.[1][2]

This was his second attempt, following the [first Kashmir campaign of 1015–1016](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1015-kashmir). It comes after the disputed [Chandela and river campaign](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1019-chandela) in the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions). Its importance lies partly in what the result forces us to acknowledge: a powerful army could inflict immense damage in the plains and still fail against a defended approach into the mountains.

## Two attempts, not one victory

The chronology is easily blurred. A first attempt is placed in 406 AH, corresponding to the campaign of late 1015 and early 1016. A second belongs to 412 AH, beginning in the autumn of 1021 and ending with a return to Ghazna in spring 1022. Muhammad Nazim’s scholarly reconstruction distinguishes them using Gardizi, the near-contemporary author of the *Zayn al-akhbar*.[2]

Gardizi is especially important because al-Utbi does not supply a full narrative of either unsuccessful expedition. That silence should not become an excuse to omit the failures. Court history has uneven coverage: a source exceptionally useful for descriptions of victory can be much less informative about an unsuccessful siege. Other evidence must carry the story, with the route by which it reaches us made clear.

The distinction also prevents an opposite mistake: placing the same defeat twice merely because later authors repeat a familiar motif. In this case Nazim specifically reads Gardizi as recording two attempts separated by several years. The second revisited a barrier that had already stopped Mahmud. That is a more precise proposition than saying he repeatedly invaded “Kashmir” without explaining whether he entered its central valley.[2]

## What Lohkot guarded

In the reconstructed route, Lohkot was a fortified obstacle on an approach into Kashmir, associated with the modern Loharin area and the Toshamaidan route. The identification draws on historical geography, not on the assumption that any present-day fort sharing a similar name must be the same place. Nazim cites Gardizi and Kalhana’s form Loharakotta; he also relies on Aurel Stein’s study of Kashmir’s routes.[2]

The military problem was greater than a wall. An attacking force needed a usable approach, food for soldiers, forage for animals, room to deploy, and a way to preserve communication with its base. A position commanding a mountain entrance could interrupt those requirements without ever meeting the invader on an open plain. The fact that an army could approach a fortress did not mean it could move its full force past it.

Al-Biruni’s near-contemporary description helps recover that geographical setting. In *India*, he describes Kashmir as a plateau enclosed by high and difficult mountains. He emphasises the inhabitants’ care over entrances and roads. He also discusses the limited access given to outsiders and the difficulty of commerce. These passages are not a narrative of Mahmud’s siege, but they independently show why the approaches mattered so much.[3]

His travel geography should not be mistaken for a modern military map. Some names and distances require interpretation, and his general statements cannot establish the position of every camp. Their value is more fundamental: Kashmir’s protection depended on controlling a small number of difficult passages, a condition an invader could not remove simply by having won previous battles elsewhere.

## The first failure remained relevant

The earlier siege had already revealed the problem. As reconstructed from Gardizi, Mahmud invested Lohkot, but heavy snow interfered with communication. After about a month the attempt was abandoned. Accounts of the difficult withdrawal include loss of direction and danger from water; the relationship among the later versions is not completely simple. The basic result, however, is clear: the fortress was not reduced and the invasion failed.[2]

That earlier experience gives the second attempt its significance. Mahmud was not encountering an entirely unknown barrier in 1021. He was returning to an obstacle that had resisted him. It is reasonable to infer that the route remained strategically important, but there is no need to invent a private vow of revenge, a council speech, or a detailed siege plan that the surviving texts do not preserve.

The intervening years had also shown the reach of his power in other directions. The Mathura and Kannauj expedition carried his army deep into the northern plains. That success did not automatically solve the mountain problem. Operational experience in one landscape did not remove the defensive advantage of another.

## Autumn 1021: the second approach

Nazim dates the renewed expedition to September–October 1021, in 412 AH, citing Gardizi. The second siege again lasts approximately a month in that account. The fortress’s natural defences cannot be overcome. Severe winter conditions then worsen the attackers’ position, and Mahmud raises the siege.[2]

The seasonal setting is meaningful, but it does not license picturesque invention. We do not possess a day-by-day diary describing every snowfall or a reliable list of men who died outside the walls. A historical account can state that winter and the defended site obstructed the expedition without adding imagined frozen bodies, speeches by starving soldiers, or precise casualty totals.

The army’s withdrawal should also be described as a failed siege, not softened into a successful reconnaissance. Whatever other operations followed in the Punjab, the immediate objective at Lohkot was not achieved. Conversely, the failure does not prove that Mahmud’s army was annihilated or that its entire military system collapsed. He returned and campaigned again. The evidence supports a significant local defeat of purpose within an empire that remained powerful.

## Lahore and the winter in the Punjab

Following the failed attempt, the army returned toward Lahore and the sub-Himalayan Punjab. Nazim notes Gardizi’s reference to Lahore and a region rendered Takeshar, associating the latter with the hill country of the western Punjab. His reconstruction puts Mahmud’s return to Ghazna at the beginning of spring, March–April 1022.[2]

The distinction between retreating to a wintering region and capturing a city is essential. A phrase saying that the ruler returned to Lahore does not by itself prove that Lahore was first conquered during that exact movement. The Shahi position in the Punjab had been eroded through earlier fighting, displacement, tribute, and Ghaznavid intervention. A single dramatic “fall of Lahore” can conceal a more extended process.

The much later *Tabaqat-i Akbari*, quoted by Elliot and Dowson, describes a month before Lohkot, withdrawal toward Lahore and Bagar, and orders to plunder the hill country. It says immense booty was collected. This passage supplies an explicit tradition of predation after the failed siege. It does not convert the failure into the conquest of Kashmir.[1]

Plunder should not be treated as a neutral consolation prize. It meant that the ability to resist a fortress assault did not protect every neighbouring settlement or household from an army returning through the region. At the same time, the quoted passage does not give a reliable list of affected villages, a captive total, or a casualty count. Those details cannot be supplied merely to make the account more dramatic.

## A composite campaign in the later histories

Some older lists place Kirat, Nur, Lohkot, and Lahore together as a single expedition. Elliot and Dowson’s appendix does so, under a heading dated 413 AH, while discussing place names and extracting a narrative from Nizam al-Din Ahmad. The sequence includes the submission of one mountain chief, an expedition against another district, construction of a fort, conversion, and then the Kashmir attempt.[1]

That arrangement needs qualification. Nazim treats the Nur and Kirat operations separately and argues that the second Kashmir expedition belongs to 412 AH, not the appendix’s 413. He specifically warns that linking the Kashmir siege as a sequel to those valley operations lacks authority in the evidence he uses.[2]

The resulting uncertainty affects more than a date. If several operations were compressed into one heading by a later compiler, an article that gives Mahmud a seamless march through all four places may create a route that no near-contemporary source records. Keeping the reader’s article focused on the second Kashmir attempt and Punjab return preserves what can be supported while acknowledging the older composite list.

This is also why traditional numbered campaigns should function as a reading guide rather than a claim that medieval officials maintained the same seventeen-item catalogue. Sources group events differently; modern authors make additional decisions about what constitutes an invasion. The numbering can be useful without making those editorial choices invisible.

## Religion and coercion in the surrounding accounts

The later narrative of Nur and Kirat explicitly describes idol worship, acceptance of Islam, appointment of a Muslim governor, and the spread of Islam by both consent and force. These are claims in a late-sixteenth-century history. They should be presented as such, rather than imported into the Lohkot siege as if they were a contemporary report of conversions inside Kashmir.[1]

Nor does the failure to enter the valley imply that the wider conflict lacked a religious language. Mahmud’s court historians repeatedly praised his campaigns as struggles against unbelief, and the destruction or removal of sacred images was part of his public reputation. The task is to identify where a specific account actually documents those acts. For this siege there is no basis for claiming that he destroyed all Kashmir’s temples or imposed a new religious order throughout the valley.

The distinction protects the history from both evasion and exaggeration. Violence elsewhere in the campaign sequence is amply documented. Here, the central fact is the survival of a defended entrance against him. One outcome should not be overwritten to make the whole career fit an unbroken story of either triumph or failure.

## What the defenders achieved

For the defenders, holding Lohkot denied Mahmud the route he sought. Fortification and terrain forced him into a time-consuming operation and exposed the besiegers to a worsening season. The surviving narrative gives less attention to the defenders’ organisation than to Mahmud’s movement, but their success is visible in the result.[1][2]

It would be tempting to fill that imbalance with a heroic commander’s invented speech or a precise tactical plan. The better response is to acknowledge the missing perspective. We know a defended position remained untaken; we do not know every order issued within it. Recognising that gap does not diminish the achievement. It prevents the modern account from replacing a source’s silence with fiction.

Kashmir’s political independence also cannot be reduced to geography alone. Mountain entrances were useful because people maintained and defended them. Al-Biruni’s remarks on guarded roads underline that relationship between terrain and organised control. A mountain range is not automatically a functioning defensive system; the sources describe a society attentive to the points where outsiders could enter.[3]

## The limits of a conqueror’s reach

The campaign ended with Mahmud outside Kashmir’s central valley and returning through a Punjab increasingly exposed to Ghaznavid power. It demonstrates a mixed outcome: failure at the fortified entrance, continued capacity for movement and plunder elsewhere, and no permanent conquest of the valley.[1][2]

The distinction is visible even within the comparatively short later account. Its sequence moves from an untaken fortress to plunder collected elsewhere and finally a return home. Those stages could all be narrated as part of one royal expedition, yet they describe different military results. The valley’s successful defence did not guarantee security for the Punjab; the army’s plunder in the Punjab did not undo the valley’s successful defence. Reading the sequence in that form restores the uneven geography of power that a single coloured area on a map can obscure.[1]

Such a result matters to the interpretation of his reign. Royal titles and chronicles could describe sweeping dominion, but effective control varied sharply from place to place. Raiding, tributary arrangements, garrisons, and the ability to pass an army through a district were different relationships. None should be treated as interchangeable with ruling every region that appeared in a victory narrative.
