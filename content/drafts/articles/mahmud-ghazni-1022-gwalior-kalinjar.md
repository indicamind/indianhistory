The expedition against Gwalior and Kalinjar presents a different outcome from the burning of Mathura or the attack on Somnath. Here, the surviving accounts describe strong fortresses, siege pressure, elephants offered as the price of peace, and the withdrawal of the invading army. Mahmud of Ghazni could claim submission and valuable resources, but the evidence does not justify saying that he stormed and permanently occupied both great forts.[1][2]

This article continues the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions), following the [second Kashmir attempt and Punjab return of 1021–1022](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1021-kashmir-lahore). The campaign is commonly dated to 1022–1023. Its relationship with the earlier [Chandela confrontation of 1019–1020](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1019-chandela) is important: the first encounter had not eliminated the power of the rulers south of the Ganges plain.[2]

## Why return to Chandela territory?

The earlier expedition left a powerful opponent in place. In the later Persian narrative this ruler is called Nanda; modern historical reconstruction connects the episode with the Chandela dynasty and the names Ganda and Vidyadhara. The transmission of names is difficult, and the translated chronicles do not warrant treating every spelling as a precise identification of the same individual.[1][2]

What matters for the campaign is that the political centre had survived. A raid could obtain booty and elephants without removing the ruler or occupying his principal fortresses. An army returning to Ghazna with trophies did not necessarily leave an obedient government behind. Continued resistance or refusal to meet demands could therefore draw another expedition.

Nazim’s reconstruction explicitly says that the earlier campaign had not broken the Chandela ruler’s power. Using Gardizi, he places the renewed advance in 413 AH, corresponding to 1022, and the return in spring 1023. This reading is more coherent than the inconsistent dates printed in the nineteenth-century compilation, whose heading gives 414 AH while its quoted passage gives 413.[1][2]

A date difference should not distract from the more consequential question: what kind of victory was achieved? A conquered fortress, an intimidated ruler, an annual tributary promise, and an immediate gift of elephants are related but distinct outcomes. The sources’ own descriptions make that distinction possible.

## Gwalior: an obstacle on the approach

The account places Gwalior before Kalinjar. In Nazim’s reconstruction, its ruler is a subordinate of the Chandela power and holds a fortress on a steep rock. Mahmud attacks but cannot take it. After four days, the local ruler seeks peace and offers 35 elephants.[2]

The *Tabaqat-i Akbari* passage quoted by Elliot and Dowson similarly describes a siege and a settlement after four days, with 35 elephants promised in return for protection. The relatively brief text does not give a detailed account of breaches, ladders, mining, or an assault through a gate. Those familiar siege details should not be supplied simply because a modern article needs a dramatic battle scene.[1]

A ruler could resist successfully at the walls and still judge a negotiated payment preferable to a longer siege. Fortification protected a position, but it did not make the surrounding countryside, supply routes, and dependants invulnerable. The sources do not preserve the full calculations of Gwalior’s ruler. They do, however, describe an agreement instead of the fortress’s destruction.

The number of elephants remains a source report. It is less extravagant than some of the enormous totals in the campaign traditions, but modest appearance is not independent verification. It should be written as “the account reports 35 elephants,” rather than converted into a precise modern valuation or treated as a surviving treasury receipt.

## What an elephant payment meant

Elephants appear so frequently in these narratives that they can become decorative symbols of medieval India. In these transactions they were material resources. An animal suitable for military use represented training, handlers, provisioning, and the ability to sustain a costly force. Transferring elephants could benefit the recipient’s army while reducing the donor’s resources.

Their appearance also served political storytelling. A chronicler could measure a ruler’s submission through visible animals delivered to the victorious camp. A herd made a royal success tangible in a way that a vague promise of friendship did not. This is why the reported numbers deserve attention even when their exact accuracy cannot be proved.

The distinction between payment and conquest remains essential. Receiving animals did not necessarily give Mahmud the power to appoint every officer, collect every local tax, or keep a permanent garrison inside the fort. If a history claims all of those consequences, it needs additional evidence. The elephant transaction alone cannot supply them.

## Kalinjar: siege pressure without a documented storming

Mahmud next proceeds to Kalinjar. The fortress’s strength dominates the accounts. Nizam al-Din Ahmad calls it exceptional among the strongholds of Hindustan. Nazim, drawing on Gardizi and other material, describes a siege intended to close approaches and compel the defenders through shortage.[1][2]

A blockade was an attempt to make a defensive advantage costly over time. A wall might stop an assault while the garrison still depended on stored food, water arrangements, and continued political support. Yet the surviving narrative here does not establish how completely Mahmud closed the approaches or how near the defenders came to exhaustion. The historical statement should therefore remain that he laid siege and sought submission, not that starvation inside the fort has been documented household by household.

The settlement is the key event. The ruler offers 300 elephants and seeks peace. Mahmud accepts and raises the siege. In Nazim’s account the arrangement also includes a promise of annual tribute. Whatever degree of compulsion produced it, this was a negotiation between an invading army and a ruling power that remained capable of bargaining.[1][2]

Calling the outcome merely a friendly exchange would conceal the armed pressure. Calling it the complete destruction of Kalinjar would contradict the account’s form. The sources describe a threatened stronghold purchasing or negotiating relief, while Mahmud converts the siege into a claim of successful submission.

## The riderless elephants

One memorable detail says that the elephants were sent out without riders. The Ghaznavid soldiers seized and mounted them. In the *Tabaqat-i Akbari* narrative, the defenders are surprised by this display. Gardizi, as transmitted through Nazim’s discussion, also provides the riderless-elephant story.[1][2]

The scene presents military nerve as a form of mastery. Elephants that might have been dangerous become another opportunity for the invader’s men to demonstrate superiority. It is also the kind of vivid episode that later authors could embroider. Nazim specifically objects to a claim that the elephants were intoxicated: the earlier authority he cites does not support it.[2]

The difference is small in wording and large in effect. Riderless elephants are the reported diplomatic delivery; deliberately maddened elephants would imply a concealed trap and an additional heroic ordeal. Adding the latter makes the story more exciting by changing the evidence. It should not appear in a factual reconstruction unless its later source and uncertain status are explained.

We also lack an independent record from the defenders explaining their purpose. Sending the animals without handlers may have had practical or ceremonial meanings that a victorious court’s account does not preserve. It is safest to state the reported action and the chronicler’s interpretation separately.

## A Hindi poem in a Ghaznavid victory narrative

The account then turns from elephants to verse. Nanda sends a Hindi poem praising Mahmud. The sultan has it examined by learned men and poets associated with his court, receives their favourable judgment, and rewards the author. The translated chronicle reports a grant or confirmation involving 15 forts and further presents.[1]

This scene matters because it records diplomacy conducted through people able to interpret an Indian language within the Ghaznavid court’s world. The relation between the two sides was not maintained solely by a battle cry or a blow. Envoys, interpreters, poems, gifts, and the conferral of honours also gave shape to power.

The poem itself is not preserved in the cited passage. We cannot quote lines from it, name its original metre, or reconstruct the author’s feelings. Praise offered under military pressure does not prove personal admiration; it can be a diplomatic act. Nor does a reported reward settle what authority Mahmud actually possessed over each of the 15 forts.

The statement is best treated as part of the settlement tradition. It suggests a ruler acknowledging the invader sufficiently to end the immediate threat, while the victorious narrative depicts Mahmud as able to confer honours. Both sides could have had reasons to accept an arrangement that could be represented differently at their own courts.

## The question of lasting control

The distinction between a claim of overlordship and sustained administration is central to this expedition. A promise of tribute could mark subordination, but the durability of such promises depended on enforcement, local politics, and the ruler’s ability to return. The survival of the Chandela centre after the campaign cautions against colouring an entire region as a permanently administered Ghaznavid province on the basis of this one settlement.[2]

The sources used here describe Mahmud’s return to Ghazna. They do not provide a detailed occupation regime for Kalinjar, a complete replacement of its governing elite, or the destruction of its entire defensive system. A map caption saying “area raided or subjected to demands” can therefore be more accurate than one saying “territory annexed.”

This does not make the expedition harmless. The demand was backed by an army capable of violence, and the transfer of valuable resources reduced the defender’s freedom of action. Submission negotiated under siege was a coercive political relationship. Its coercion should be visible without attributing to it every consequence of a permanent conquest.

## What can be said about destruction and captivity?

In other campaigns, al-Utbi explicitly celebrates killing, temple burning, and the sale of captives. The passages central to Gwalior and Kalinjar emphasise siege and settlement instead. They do not supply a documented massacre inside either surrendered fortress or a reliable total of people enslaved there.[1]

That difference should be respected. It would be wrong to infer that Mahmud had abandoned the religious and fiscal purposes evident elsewhere. It would also be wrong to copy the atrocities of Mathura or Somnath into Kalinjar simply because the same invader was involved. Each episode requires its own evidence.

The narrative still contains the threat of destructive force, and negotiations happened in its presence. The fact that a siege ended in payment rather than a storm could itself have spared people from the forms of killing and capture that the court histories praise in other places. The record does not let us count those spared, and it should not be made to do so.

## Reading the sources at different distances

The most easily accessible full passage is in the late-sixteenth-century *Tabaqat-i Akbari*, quoted by Elliot and Dowson. Its distance from the campaign is substantial. Nearer evidence matters, which is why Nazim’s references to Gardizi are useful. This article distinguishes directly inspected translated passages from early material encountered through a modern scholar’s analysis.[1][2]

Al-Utbi’s absence is also relevant. His surviving Indian narrative does not give this campaign the coverage found in the earlier Mathura expedition. A modern writer cannot fill the gap by transferring one of his descriptions of temple destruction to a later year. The uneven survival and selection of texts set real limits on detail.

The old compilation’s inconsistent AH heading offers a warning about dates as well. Quoting a printed source accurately does not mean repeating every editorial error it contains. A historical account should explain which chronology it adopts and why, while retaining the original wording where the disagreement matters.

## A settlement before Somnath

The expedition traditionally ends with Mahmud returning in the spring of 1023. He had obtained elephant payments and a submission that could be celebrated at Ghazna. Gwalior and Kalinjar had faced armed coercion, but their fortresses were not shown by this evidence to have been annihilated or permanently occupied.[2]

The contrast with the next major expedition is striking because it is supported by the sources. At Kalinjar, the account centres on a ruler and an agreement. At [Somnath in 1025–1026](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1025-somnath), near-contemporary evidence explicitly records destruction of the sacred image and its removal to Ghazna, while a later narrative describes the assault and heavy slaughter. These outcomes should be compared with their differences intact.
