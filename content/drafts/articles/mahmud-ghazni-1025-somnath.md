In January 1026, Mahmud of Ghazni’s army reached Somnath on the coast of western India. The sacred image was broken, its valuable coverings taken, and portions carried to Ghazna. There they were placed in public settings designed to advertise the conqueror’s triumph: the hippodrome and the entrance of a mosque, where people wiped their feet. This destruction and deliberate humiliation of a sacred object are recorded by al-Biruni, a scholar writing close to the events.[1]

The fighting surrounding that destruction is described most fully in a later narrative. Ibn al-Athir, writing roughly two centuries afterward, tells of assaults on the walls, killing at the temple gate, and fugitives overtaken at sea. His reported death toll of more than 50,000 is famous. It is a medieval historian’s claim, however, not a contemporary count of the dead. Keeping these different kinds of evidence separate allows the violence to be stated plainly without turning every later embellishment into fact.[1][2]

Somnath forms the sixteenth dossier in the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions), following the [Gwalior and Kalinjar expedition of 1022–1023](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1022-gwalior-kalinjar). It was followed by a punishing journey through western India and Sind, and later by the [campaign against the Jats, conventionally dated 1027](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1027-jats).

## A shrine beside an ocean route

Somnath was a place of worship, but its setting also belonged to the maritime world of the Indian Ocean. Al-Biruni explains its fame partly through its harbour and its role as a station for people travelling between Sufala in the country of the Zanj and China. His description places the shrine within a network of movement and exchange extending far beyond a single kingdom.[1]

The name, in his explanation, means “lord of the moon.” He identifies the sacred object as the linga of Mahadeva and recounts the religious story connecting its worship with the moon. He also describes offerings brought from distant places: water from the Ganges and flowers from Kashmir. These details convey the shrine’s reach through devotion as well as geography.[1]

Ibn al-Athir’s later account presents the institution on a grand scale. He reports endowments from more than 10,000 villages, extensive treasure, a thousand Brahmans involved in worship, attendants serving pilgrims, and musicians and dancers. The exact figures cannot be independently verified from his account. They nevertheless show the kind of wealthy religious establishment that later historians believed Mahmud had attacked.[2]

There is a crucial difference between describing that institution and reducing it to a storehouse waiting to be seized. Its wealth was attached to worship, offerings, employment, journeys, and the prestige of a sacred place. To attack it was to damage those relationships as well as to obtain material resources. The removal of a linga to a mosque threshold made the religious meaning of the act particularly explicit.

## Why Mahmud went so far

Mahmud already had a record of attacking sacred institutions and bringing wealth and images back to Ghazna. The bronze Cakrasvamin taken from Thanesar later lay alongside a portion of the Somnath image, according to al-Biruni. The two objects became parts of the same public language of victory, even though their original religious identities differed.[1]

Ibn al-Athir supplies a particular explanation for the new expedition. He says Hindus attributed Mahmud’s destruction of other images to Somnath’s displeasure with those deities. Mahmud therefore resolved to destroy Somnath too, expecting the failure of its protection to encourage conversion. This is the later historian’s account of the motive, not a surviving transcript of a decision made in the sultan’s presence.[2]

The religious purpose described by the chronicles can coexist with the pursuit of wealth and prestige. A rich shrine offered portable valuables; a distant expedition offered an exceptional feat to celebrate; destruction of a famous sacred object could demonstrate the king’s proclaimed defence of Islam. The sources give no reason to erase any of these purposes in favour of a single exclusive explanation.

The distance also made the undertaking risky. An army crossing dry country needed water and food not only for its soldiers but for a large train of animals. A campaign that succeeded at its destination could still be damaged on the way home. The history of Somnath includes both the conquest and that exposed return.

## From Ghazna to Multan

The accepted chronology places Mahmud’s departure from Ghazna in the autumn of 1025 and the attack in January 1026. Al-Biruni independently dates the destruction to 416 AH. Muhammad Nazim’s reconstruction, using several medieval accounts, places the departure in October and the halt at Multan in November.[1][3]

The old English translation of Ibn al-Athir quoted by Elliot and Dowson prints 414 AH in the narrative, despite the appendix’s own heading placing the expedition in 416–417. That printed date should not be used to move the assault two years earlier. The near-contemporary chronological anchor is 416 AH; the later detailed story must be read with its transmission problems acknowledged.[1][2][3]

Multan was a base for preparing the desert crossing. The later account gives 30,000 mounted men in addition to volunteers and describes large numbers of camels carrying water and grain. These reported totals are not certain statistics, but their central practical point is credible: the invaders could not depend on finding adequate supplies along an unprepared desert route.[2]

Water points consequently had military importance. In Ibn al-Athir’s narrative, a fort with wells is taken after the crossing begins. He says the inhabitants are killed and their images broken, and that the army carries water away before continuing. The well and the sacred image appear in the same episode: a logistical need and religiously celebrated destruction formed parts of one advance.[2]

## Through Gujarat toward the coast

The army moved toward Anahilwara, the Chaulukya capital associated with Bhima. The later account says Bhima abandoned the city and sought safety in a fortress. Nazim reconstructs the refuge as Kanthkot in Kutch, while acknowledging and rejecting other proposed identifications in his discussion. The forms of the name vary in the translated texts.[2][3]

Bhima’s flight left Mahmud free to continue, but it did not mean every population on the route surrendered peacefully. Ibn al-Athir describes intermediate forts where people were killed, fortifications destroyed, and images broken. He also reports a force of 20,000 local fighting men defeated and dispersed, followed by the taking and plundering of Dabalwara near Somnath.[2]

These actions should not be hidden beneath the phrase “he marched to Somnath.” The advance itself, in this account, involved the destruction of places and the killing of people before the principal shrine came into view. At the same time, the text’s uncertain place names and rounded numbers limit the precision with which those episodes can be mapped.

The march brought a force from inland Afghanistan to a coastal settlement sustained by a very different geography. The final objective stood beside the sea, not behind an endless protective desert. What had impeded the invaders also made their progress a feat for their chroniclers to celebrate: Mahmud had carried an army across a difficult route to strike a distant centre of worship.

## The assault on Somnath

Al-Biruni supplies an important distinction between the age of the sacred place and the age of its fortification. He says the fortress containing the image and its treasures had been built about a hundred years before his time. That is his historical information, not a modern archaeological dating, but it shows that his account did not treat every feature of the site as equally ancient. An established place of worship could acquire a comparatively recent defensive enclosure.[1]

The relationship between shrine and fortress also shaped what followed. Walls could protect the institution and its accumulated possessions; once the fighting passed them, defenders and worshippers could be compressed toward the sacred centre. The later narrative’s movement from ramparts to temple gate is therefore more than a change of scenery. It shows a defensive perimeter giving way and violence concentrating at the place the enclosure was meant to protect.[2]

The coast complicated escape in turn. The sea offered boats and the possibility of getting beyond the attacker’s immediate reach, but it could also expose fugitives to pursuit and drowning. The account describes both the walls and the water as elements of the struggle. It does not give a complete plan of the fortress or the position of every vessel, so the precise relationship of the buildings to the shore remains beyond this reconstruction.

Ibn al-Athir describes a strong fortress built beside the water, its walls washed by the sea. He says Mahmud arrived on a Thursday in the middle of Dhu'l Qa'da. The following day the assailants advanced, brought ladders to the walls, and gained a position above them. Fighting continued, and night interrupted the action.[2]

The source also supplies taunts from the defenders, who supposedly expected their deity to destroy the invaders. Such dialogue belongs to a later literary account of the siege. It can illuminate the religious framing of the story, but it should not be treated as words securely recorded from the ramparts.

On the next morning, the narrative says, the assault resumed. The defenders were driven toward the temple. Ibn al-Athir describes successive groups entering the shrine, praying, and returning to fight until they were killed. The gate became the setting for intense slaughter. The account presents the defenders’ religious attachment as central to their resistance, just as it presents the attackers’ action through the language of Islam.[2]

The chronology reconstructed by Nazim places these principal days on 7 and 8 January 1026. His analysis also uses Gardizi and other medieval material, rather than relying only on Ibn al-Athir. The precise progress of the fighting remains less securely recoverable than the fact that the shrine was attacked and its image destroyed.[3]

## The dead and those who tried to escape

The later account does not end the violence when the walls are taken. It describes people fleeing in boats, pursued by the victors, with some killed and others drowned. Flight by water, which might otherwise have offered an exit from a coastal fortress, becomes another scene of death.[2]

Ibn al-Athir concludes with a reported total exceeding 50,000 slain. That figure has often migrated into modern histories without its source label. It should remain “Ibn al-Athir reports more than 50,000,” accompanied by the fact that he wrote much later and gives no independently verifiable counting method. Al-Biruni’s near-contemporary account of the destruction does not supply this casualty total.[1][2]

The uncertainty of a number does not make the reported slaughter unimportant. It changes what can honestly be claimed about its scale. Nor does the narrative allow everyone killed to be classified neatly as a professional soldier. Its defenders include people gathered around a sacred place, and its fugitives are not described through the categories a modern military census would require.

The cited siege narrative provides no reliable overall count of captives. Captivity was a recurring feature of Mahmud’s campaigns, but figures from Mathura or another expedition cannot be transferred to Somnath. The explicit evidence here concerns killing, drowning, plunder, and destruction of the sacred image.

## Breaking the linga

Al-Biruni’s evidence is especially important at this point. He calls the object a linga of Mahadeva. He says Mahmud ordered its upper portion broken and the remainder transported to Ghazna, along with coverings and ornaments of gold, jewels, and embroidered garments.[1]

This description differs from the familiar image of a human-shaped idol smashed open to reveal a hidden chamber of jewels. A solid stone linga did not require a concealed interior for its valuable adornment to be seized. Al-Biruni’s account identifies both the sacred form and the external wealth associated with it.

Ibn al-Athir’s later version says part of the object was burned and another portion carried to Ghazna. He also describes pillars, lamps, a golden chain with bells, and treasuries containing images of precious metal. These details place the sacred object within an elaborate institution; their architectural and numerical precision comes from that later source.[2]

The sum of treasure is another reported figure. The Elliot and Dowson translation of Ibn al-Athir says its value exceeded two million dinars. Other accounts and modern reconstructions print different sums. These cannot responsibly be converted into a single certain present-day cash value. The clear historical proposition is the seizure of substantial wealth, not an exact modern price tag.

## Sacred fragments at Ghazna

The destruction was followed by transport and display. Al-Biruni says one portion lay in Ghazna’s hippodrome with the bronze Cakrasvamin from Thanesar. Another lay before the mosque door, where feet were cleaned on it. The placement converted a revered object into something to be trodden on.[1]

That detail makes humiliation a documented part of the outcome. The act was not simply the accidental damage of a building during a battle or the melting of metal to pay troops. A fragment retained its identity sufficiently for the conqueror’s public to understand what had been degraded. Material survival could be made to serve the message of religious defeat.

Al-Biruni’s tone is itself complex. He writes with extraordinary interest in Indian learning and records the destructive effect of Mahmud’s wars, yet also uses conventional prayers for the ruler. Readers should not flatten him into either a modern detached observer or an uncomplicated court propagandist. His testimony is valuable because it is specific and close to the period; it still requires interpretation.[1][4]

In his general discussion of the invasions, he says Mahmud ruined prosperity and scattered people, explaining that learning withdrew to regions such as Kashmir and Benares. This is a broad assessment of the damage visible to the scholar, not a claim that the whole subcontinent had been occupied or reduced to one condition.[4]

## The return did not retrace a simple victory road

Taking Somnath did not remove Mahmud’s enemies or solve his supply problem. Later accounts describe a threatened interception by a ruler called Parama Dev. In the *Tabaqat-i Akbari* passage, Mahmud avoids that confrontation and returns through Sind toward Multan, suffering shortages of water and forage. The identity of Parama Dev has been debated; assigning him confidently to a particular famous king goes beyond this passage.[5]

Ibn al-Athir describes an operation against Bhima’s refuge, rendered Kandahat, involving a dangerous tidal crossing. He then takes Mahmud toward Mansura. Its ruler is called a Muslim apostate; he flees into date-palm country, where his followers are surrounded and many killed or drowned. The episode reminds us that Mahmud’s campaigning in this region also targeted Muslim opponents whom his historians condemned on doctrinal grounds.[6]

The return traditions also contain the story of a guide who deliberately leads the invaders into waterless country to avenge Somnath. A thirteenth-century version gives the guide a declaration of devotion, has him executed, and then describes a miraculous light guiding Mahmud’s army to water. The episode belongs to the history of remembered resistance and providential rescue. Its speeches and miracle cannot be treated as a contemporary transcript of the march.[5]

The broader hardship is better supported. Nazim’s analysis of Gardizi describes losses among men and animals and attacks by Jats along the retreat. The expedition’s final success at Ghazna therefore concealed a journey in which an army burdened with spoils remained vulnerable to thirst, unfamiliar routes, and local enemies.[3]

## What happened after the army left?

The destruction of the image was not the same as establishing a durable Ghaznavid administration over Gujarat. Some much later stories describe Mahmud considering residence near Somnath or choosing a local ruler through elaborate court adventures. Nazim questions the evidence for a governor left there, and the accessible early testimony used here does not establish a permanent occupation regime.[3]

For the people and institutions affected, the army’s departure did not reverse the losses. Worship had been attacked, people killed, valuables removed, and a sacred object carried away for degradation abroad. Describing the expedition as a raid rather than a permanent annexation identifies its political form; it does not diminish its violence.

Nor should later restoration be used to erase the attack. Conversely, a photograph of the present Somnath temple should not be captioned as the building Mahmud saw. The present structure belongs to later reconstruction. Accurate images can show the coastal setting and the continuing importance of the site while making the difference in date clear.

## A powerful later legend

The best-known story puts an offer of enormous ransom before Mahmud and has him choose fame as an idol-breaker rather than an idol-seller. A late version then rewards that apparent refusal of profit with jewels discovered inside the broken object. The story is dramatically satisfying precisely because it makes religious destruction and material gain harmonise perfectly.[5]

It is not verified speech from 1026. The late-sixteenth-century *Tarikh-i Alfi*, quoted by Elliot and Dowson, supplies an elaborate version; later authors repeat or develop it. Even the nineteenth-century editors point out the problem of hidden treasure inside a solid linga. The near-contemporary al-Biruni records precious external trappings, not this ransom exchange.[1][5]

The distinction matters because the legend can displace the harder history. The documented humiliation of fragments is already explicit. The later siege narrative already describes slaughter. Neither needs a fabricated royal quotation to make its moral or historical significance visible.

## The campaign and its long memory

Somnath became one of the most powerful episodes in Mahmud’s reputation because a distant military expedition, a wealthy shrine, destruction of a sacred object, and a public claim of religious triumph converged. Its memory was then expanded by authors writing generations and centuries later. Their accounts preserve material worth examining, but their distance and purposes remain part of the story.

The evidential core is severe enough: Mahmud attacked Somnath, broke its linga, seized associated wealth, and sent fragments to Ghazna for degradation. The detailed later account adds sustained fighting, killings at the shrine, and deaths among those attempting escape. The reported totals remain uncertain, while the nature of the acts does not need to be concealed.[1][2]
