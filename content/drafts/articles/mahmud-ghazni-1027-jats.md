The army that returned from Somnath had carried away wealth and fragments of a sacred image, but its passage home was dangerous. Shortages, difficult country, and attacks by local opponents burdened the march. Among those opponents were Jats living in the Indus region. In 1027, according to the chronology reconstructed from Gardizi, Mahmud returned to punish them. The fighting this time centred on the river.[1]

The account is memorable for its boats armed with projecting iron spikes. Its human conclusion is more important: men drowned or were killed, refuges were searched, and the later chronicle explicitly describes the capture of families. This was a reprisal extending beyond a contest between armed crews. The violence reached people who had been sent away from the fighting in an effort to keep them safe.[1][2]

This is the final numbered dossier in the [guide to Mahmud’s invasions](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions), following the [Somnath campaign of 1025–1026](/article/mahmud-ghazni-1025-somnath). It is often called his seventeenth invasion. That number is a conventional arrangement of campaigns, not a surviving contemporary catalogue. The river expedition can be examined on its own evidence without treating every traditional boundary and date as equally secure.

## A reprisal rooted in the return from Somnath

The Somnath expedition did not end when the shrine was taken. Mahmud’s force had to return through western India and Sind, carrying possessions seized during the campaign. That movement exposed it to a different kind of struggle from an assault on a fortified religious centre. An army on the march needed water, forage, usable crossings, and enough protection for its people and animals.[1]

Gardizi, as analysed by Muhammad Nazim, describes Jat attacks during this difficult retreat. The late-sixteenth-century *Tabaqat-i Akbari* likewise explains the subsequent expedition as punishment for their interference with the returning army. The two traditions give the conflict a concrete cause: the invader’s withdrawal had been contested.[1][2]

Calling the Jats “raiders” or “rebels” without context would simply reproduce the victorious state’s perspective. People opposing an army passing through their region had their own interests, though the surviving accounts do not preserve their deliberations. Equally, a modern reconstruction cannot supply a speech in which they announce that they are avenging Somnath. The account records harassment and reprisal; its evidence for individual motives is much thinner.

The episode shows why possession of great booty did not automatically create security. A force successful at its distant objective could still be vulnerable where waterways, local knowledge, and dispersed opposition made a return route difficult. Mahmud’s next response sought to reverse that vulnerability by attacking opponents in their own river environment.

## Who were the Jats in these accounts?

The medieval term identifies the communities targeted by the expedition, but it should not be turned into a complete description of every present-day Jat community. The sources concern particular people in a particular river region. They do not give us a census, a uniform political organisation, or the personal histories of their crews and families.

Older scholarship also produced uncertain geographical identifications. Elliot and Dowson called them Jats of Jud and connected them with the Salt Range. Nazim objects to that identification when analysing the river campaign. He also rejects a claim attributed through nineteenth-century scholarship that these Jats had forced the ruler of Mansura to abandon Islam, saying the statement lacks authority in the original text.[1][2]

These corrections matter because an incorrect starting place changes the entire military story. If the opponents are imagined as mountain people far from the river, their fleet appears inexplicable for one reason; if they are situated in the Indus environment described by the campaign, the practical setting is different. The safest account locates the fighting in the Indus region while leaving uncertain community geography visible.

There is also no need to make every opponent of Mahmud part of a single coordinated coalition. The sources describe campaigns against different rulers and communities, including Muslim opponents at Multan and Mansura. The Jat conflict belongs to that varied political landscape, even though court narratives fold many enemies into a single language of royal and religious victory.

## Why 1027 rather than 1026?

The chronological issue has a specific answer in the scholarship. Nazim cites Gardizi for an expedition beginning in 418 AH, which he converts to March 1027, with a return at the beginning of summer. The later histories used in Elliot and Dowson generally give 417 AH instead. That lunar year overlaps parts of 1026 and 1027.[1][2]

An article that merely copies the old appendix can therefore move the expedition into the wrong year or make it seem an immediate continuation of the Somnath retreat. Nazim treats it as a renewed expedition after the return to Ghazna. He regards Gardizi as the crucial near-contemporary authority, rather than preferring the later date simply because several subsequent writers repeat it.[1]

The title follows the 1027 reconstruction. It does not suggest that the whole schedule can be recovered to the day from an official calendar. The distinction is enough to preserve the sequence: difficult retreat in 1026, then a campaign of punishment in the following year.

## Preparing a force for the river

The expedition is associated with Multan as the place of preparation. The narrative describes an order to construct 1,400 boats, each equipped with iron projections: one forward and two at the sides. It also describes 20 archers assigned to each vessel and weapons including incendiary material.[1][2]

These figures are striking, but they remain reported figures. A fleet of that size would imply very substantial work in boatbuilding, timber, labour, crews, and supply. Elliot and Dowson considered the total implausible, while Nazim also expresses doubts about the size and the claimed effect of the spikes. Their scepticism does not remove the evidence for a river operation. It limits the confidence with which the account can be turned into a scale model.[1][2]

The older English translation uses the word “grenades.” In this context it should not evoke a modern explosive weapon. The passage places such missiles alongside naphtha, indicating incendiary warfare as understood by its translators. The exact construction of every projectile is not explained in the cited text.[2]

What the preparation makes clear is adaptation. The invader did not rely only on mounted troops pursuing opponents along a road. He sought a flotilla capable of engaging river craft and, in the version transmitted through Nazim, combined it with forces posted on the banks. The operation tried to deny escape by either water or land.

## The Jat preparations and the island refuge

The opposing communities also prepared. The narrative says families and possessions were moved to an island or islands before the engagement. This was an attempt to separate those vulnerable people and valuable resources from the immediate battlefield.[1][2]

The sources give the opposing fleet immense totals. Nazim’s account reports 4,000 boats and immediately questions the figure. The *Tabaqat-i Akbari* version offers either 4,000 or 8,000. The disagreement is not a reason to choose 8,000 for dramatic effect. It is evidence that numerical enlargement and uncertain transmission shape the story.[1][2]

The types and sizes of these boats are not specified with enough precision to compare them with later ocean-going warships. A boat count is not automatically a count of vessels of equal size or equal fighting power. The account establishes a tradition of organised river resistance; it does not give the dimensions, tonnage, or crew lists needed for a technical reconstruction of the fleets.

Even without accepting the largest totals, the decision to move families away and prepare boats indicates that the opponents expected an attack of consequence. The river was both a means of resisting and a possible route of escape. Mahmud’s disposition aimed to control both uses.

## Collision, arrows, and drowning

In the narrated battle, the flotillas meet and the projecting spikes damage the Jat boats that approach. Vessels overturn, men fall into the water, and the victors kill those who escape drowning. Nazim’s reconstruction adds bank detachments of mounted men supported by elephants, preventing fugitives from securing safety ashore.[1]

The spiked-boat mechanism is the account’s signature detail and one of its difficulties. A collision violent enough to overturn an opponent might also endanger the attacking vessel. Nazim notes the problem explicitly. We do not possess a contemporary drawing of the fittings or an archaeological discovery that resolves how they worked.[1]

That uncertainty should not make the human consequences disappear. The source tradition describes drowning, killing, and blocked escape, not simply the capture of abandoned boats. Its military triumph is represented through opponents trapped between water and forces on the banks. For those driven back from shore, the distinction between battle and attempted escape offered no protection.

There is no reliable total of dead. Thousands of boats, even if accepted, would not license multiplying an imagined standard crew into a casualty figure. The surviving narrative provides the nature of the violence and a claimed decisive defeat; it does not provide a verifiable body count.

## The attack on the refuges

After the engagement, the victorious force proceeds to the places where people and property had been concealed. In Nazim’s account, many are killed and spoils captured. The *Tabaqat-i Akbari* passage explicitly says the families are taken prisoner.[1][2]

This is the campaign’s clearest extension beyond armed crews. A refuge chosen before battle becomes a target afterward. The story does not present women, children, and other family members as negotiating participants in a treaty; it presents them as people seized by the conquering force. Captivity was itself a form of violence, even where the text does not describe a captive being physically injured.

The later passage does not record the subsequent fate of every prisoner. It cannot establish exactly how many were enslaved, sold, ransomed, retained, or later released. Mahmud’s other campaigns include explicit slave sales, but those accounts do not supply a missing sale record for this expedition. The responsible statement here is that families were captured and exposed to the coercive power of their captors.

The word “spoils” can otherwise hide that distinction. Property could be carried away; a person could also be forced away from home. In a victory chronicle, both might contribute to the success of the expedition. A modern narrative should make the human content visible instead of allowing a conventional military term to conceal it.

## Early testimony and later amplification

Elliot and Dowson believed the campaign was recorded only by later authorities. That judgment is outdated. Nazim’s work uses Gardizi’s near-contemporary account and cites a passing reference in the contemporary poet Farrukhi. The latter celebrates Mahmud through an image of hunting formidable creatures in the river; it confirms the place of the exploit in courtly praise but does not supply a detailed independent battle narrative.[1]

The more accessible *Tabaqat-i Akbari* passage remains useful because it preserves a complete translated version of the story, including the family captures. It should be identified as a late-sixteenth-century account rather than quoted as eyewitness testimony. The early Gardizi evidence is encountered here through Nazim’s source analysis; readers can distinguish that route of access from the directly available later translation.[1][2]

The combined evidence supports more than a vague rumour of a river fight, while leaving the enormous numbers and exact mechanics uncertain. It also prevents two opposite mistakes: dismissing the entire campaign because the old appendix doubts the fleet, or accepting every detail because an early writer mentions it. A near-contemporary court narrative can still exaggerate and celebrate.

## What victory achieved

The accounts end with Mahmud returning to Ghazna. His opponents had suffered a defeat, property had been seized, and families had fallen into captivity. The reprisal demonstrated that attacks on his returning army could bring a separate campaign directed at the river communities themselves.[1][2]

The evidence does not establish the permanent destruction of every Jat community, the end of opposition throughout Sind, or a new administration over the whole Indus valley. A punitive expedition could be devastating without producing such sweeping political results. No later continuity of a community should be used to erase its losses, and no triumphal phrase should be converted into its total disappearance.

The campaign is frequently presented as the final item in Mahmud’s invasions of India. It was not the end of his reign, which continued until 1030, nor the end of Ghaznavid involvement in the subcontinent. Its place at the end of the familiar list reflects the way later histories organised his Indian campaigns.[1]

## The cost hidden behind a final triumph

At the beginning of this sequence stood an army burdened by the return from Somnath. At its end stood a victorious reprisal narrative: damaged boats, men killed or drowned, refuges entered, and families captured. The river, briefly an environment in which local opponents had made the invader vulnerable, became the setting for an organised act of punishment.

The uncertain fleet totals should stay uncertain. The documented and reported forms of violence should stay visible. Together they produce a more useful history than either a perfectly engineered naval spectacle or a bloodless summary saying Mahmud “subdued the Jats.” Return to the [campaign guide](/article/mahmud-ghazni-invasions) to compare this ending with the earlier battles, negotiated settlements, failed sieges, temple attacks, and forced movements of people that shaped his career.
