An army could leave Ghazna, defeat a king in the Punjab, seize elephants and captives, and return to Afghanistan without having conquered the country through which it had marched. It could come back to the same region years later because the earlier settlement had failed. It could strip a temple treasury while leaving a distant kingdom outside its permanent administration. These distinctions matter throughout the invasions of **Mahmud of Ghazni**, whose Indian campaigns between about 1000 and 1027 combined territorial expansion, tribute, religiously proclaimed warfare, enslavement, and the spectacular extraction of wealth. [1, 5]

The surviving sources describe killings, the taking and sale of captives, the burning of temples, and the removal and public degradation of sacred objects. Those acts belong plainly in the account. So do resistance, failed sieges, negotiated settlements, and the limits of the evidence. A court chronicler’s enormous casualty total is a claim to examine; a speech written centuries later is a tradition to date; a modern photograph of Somnath is a picture of a rebuilt temple. None should be quietly substituted for another. [1–5]

This guide brings together **seventeen separate campaign articles**. The numbering follows a familiar historical reconstruction, with combined operations identified and doubtful dates explained. It is a way of organizing the evidence, rather than a calendar handed down intact by an eleventh-century eyewitness. Each article follows the particular campaign, names the sources behind its claims, and distinguishes secure events from disputed identifications. [5, 6]

## Who Mahmud was, and what “India” means here

Mahmud ruled from Ghazna, in present-day Afghanistan, from 998 to 1030. His dynasty emerged from the military and political world of the Samanids; his father, Sabuktigin, had already fought the Hindu Shahi ruler Jayapala. Mahmud inherited that frontier struggle while pursuing power across territories west and north of the Hindu Kush. His Indian expeditions formed one major part of a much wider reign. Ghazna was their imperial centre, their starting point, and the place to which many spoils and prisoners were carried. [1, 5, 6]

“India” in the sources refers to historical regions of the subcontinent, rather than the borders of the modern Republic of India. Peshawar, Hund, Multan, and Nandana are in present-day Pakistan; Ghazna is in Afghanistan; Mathura, Kannauj, Kalinjar, and Somnath are in present-day India. The frontier was a zone of competing states, defended crossings, mountain approaches, settlements, and commercial routes. Crossing a modern national boundary does not explain the political geography of 1001. [5, 6]

Nor was the subcontinent one state waiting to be defeated in a single battle. Hindu Shahi rulers, the Ismaili rulers of Multan, the rulers of Kashmir, the Pratiharas of Kannauj, Chandelas, and other regional powers faced different threats and made different decisions. Mahmud attacked Muslim opponents as well as Hindu ones. Multan is indispensable to this history precisely because it shows religious conflict within Islam alongside frontier rivalry, tribute, and strategic coercion. [1, 5]

The campaigns also had different outcomes. Ghaznavid power developed a lasting territorial base in the Punjab. Expeditions to the central Gangetic plain and the Gujarat coast were extraordinarily destructive but did not establish continuous Ghaznavid government over all those distant regions. Occupation, tributary submission, a successful raid, and the destruction of a sacred centre describe different kinds of power. [5, 6]

## Why historians count seventeen campaigns

The confident phrase “seventeen invasions” conceals choices about what counts as an invasion. Should a revolt followed by a punitive march be listed separately? Should Waihind and Nagarkot count as one expedition or two? Should two attempts against Kashmir be distinguished when their accounts overlap? Can an undated river battle be securely attached to the campaign named in a later chronicle? [5]

Elliot and Dowson’s nineteenth-century compilation makes the problem unusually visible. Its Appendix, **Note D, “Mahmud’s Expeditions to India,”** begins by comparing totals that fail to match even the events their authors enumerate. It then arranges seventeen entries. That appendix is an editorial comparison of sources, including translated passages, rather than an eleventh-century chronicle in its own right. Its colonial judgments and geographical guesses need scrutiny alongside the courtly language of the medieval texts it reproduces. [5]

This series uses that seventeen-entry framework while incorporating chronological corrections and retaining uncertainty. The Sukhpal episode begins with revolt in late 1007 and the response in early 1008. Nandana belongs to 1013–14; the first Kashmir operation extended across 1015–16. The Chandela and Rahib narratives are difficult to align. The Jat expedition is conventionally dated 1027, although the late narrative printed by Elliot and Dowson gives a Hijri year spanning parts of 1026 and 1027. Dates spanning two years may describe a campaigning season or the conversion of a lunar year, rather than two separate invasions. [5, 6]

## From the Shahi frontier to the Punjab

The early campaigns arose from a conflict already under way before Mahmud became ruler. Sabuktigin’s fighting with Jayapala had weakened the Shahi position; Mahmud carried the struggle forward. The small expedition conventionally placed in 1000 is much less fully documented than the confrontation outside Peshawar in 1001. Treating them as equally well observed would conceal an important difference in the record. [1, 5, 6]

For Peshawar, al-Utbi supplies a narrative of battle, Jayapala’s capture with relatives, the stripping of jewels, demands for elephants and ransom, and release under humiliating conditions. He goes on to describe Jayapala’s death by self-immolation. This is a victor’s account, shaped to magnify Mahmud’s triumph. It nevertheless gives a concrete picture of conquest working through captured rulers, material exactions, and compelled political agreements. The enormous totals of dead and captives in the same passage cannot be treated as independently verified statistics. [1, pp. 24–27]

Bhatia illustrates both the violence of resistance and the practical limits of victory. Al-Utbi describes the ruler Bijai Rai resisting, withdrawing under pressure, and killing himself when pursued; the account includes killing, seizure of elephants, and the establishment of a religious instructor. It also records an uncomfortable return during the rains, with baggage lost in swollen rivers. The chronicler’s triumphal narrative contains evidence of logistical danger. Bhatia’s identification with Bhera or Bathinda is disputed, so a modern monument at either place cannot by itself authenticate the campaign’s route. [1, pp. 28–30; 5, 6]

The struggle over Multan was different. Its Ismaili ruler was a Muslim opponent whom the Ghaznavid source represented as both politically disobedient and religiously heretical. Mahmud also fought Anandapala after being refused passage through Shahi territory. Submission, financial penalties, and changes of authority did not settle the region permanently: Sukhpal’s revolt and the later return to Multan reveal the instability of arrangements that a victory announcement might make sound final. [1, pp. 30–33; 5]

At Waihind in 1008–09, al-Utbi’s account acknowledges that the Ghaznavids came close to defeat before an attack by Mahmud’s household troops helped turn the battle. Later narratives tell the famous story of panic beginning with an elephant; that version should be introduced as later testimony, rather than inserted into the contemporary account without explanation. The distinction matters because military success depended on contingencies, rather than an inevitable superiority established before the armies met. [1, pp. 33–34; 5]

The seizure of Nagarkot’s accumulated wealth then provided a different sort of victory. Al-Utbi describes a fortress opening its gates in fear, officials taking charge of treasures, and the spoils subsequently displayed at Ghazna before envoys. His vast fiscal totals celebrate imperial abundance. The surrender described in this passage does not justify inventing a prolonged storming or a massacre within the fort. The documented extraction of devotional wealth is itself a substantial event. [1, pp. 34–35]

## Sacred centres, tribute, and the reach of the army

Narayan, Nandana, and Thanesar are easy to confuse when older translations render unfamiliar place names differently. They are separate entries in this series. Narayan’s location is disputed; Nandana belongs to the Salt Range and the struggle with the Shahis; Thanesar was a major sacred centre whose removed image is independently mentioned by al-Biruni. Similar-looking names in a modern transcription do not establish that they are one event. [1, pp. 36–41; 3; 5, 6]

The Narayan passage is particularly revealing about the combination of violence and negotiated interest. Al-Utbi describes defeat, killing, the overthrow of images, and plunder, followed by arrangements involving elephants, manpower, and the safe movement of caravans between Hind and Khurasan. Destruction and commercial passage appear within the same account. A campaign could aim to break resistance and then obtain resources or security through an agreement. [1, p. 36]

Nandana’s campaign narrative adds snow, obstructed movement, fighting in a pass, pursuit, and the taking of many captives. Al-Utbi describes the subsequent cheapness of prisoners in the conqueror’s market. That claim turns the reader’s attention from the ruler’s battlefield triumph to the people converted into saleable property. Captivity was an economic result of war as well as an emblem of domination. The text preserves the victor’s view of the market; it does not preserve the names or subsequent lives of most of those sold. [1, pp. 37–39]

At Thanesar, the destruction and removal of a sacred object can be followed beyond the campaign narrative. Al-Biruni identifies the bronze Cakrasvamin from Thanesar at Ghazna, beside material taken from Somnath. His statement is a near-contemporary witness to the relocation and humiliating display of sacred objects. It is stronger evidence for that particular claim than an unexamined succession of later chronicles repeating the same story. [1, pp. 40–41; 3, ch. XI, p. 117]

Kashmir supplies a necessary corrective to accounts in which every march ends in effortless conquest. The expeditions conventionally placed in 1015–16 and 1021–22 did not open the valley to permanent Ghaznavid conquest. Defended approaches, the fort associated with Lohkot, weather, provisions, and retreat all belong to their history. The chronology of the two operations has itself been debated. The more durable extension of authority in the Punjab should not be converted into a claim that Kashmir also fell. [5, 6]

## Mathura and Kannauj: admiration followed by destruction

The expedition of 1018–19 carried Mahmud’s army into the Gangetic plain. Al-Utbi’s account follows river crossings, submissions, fighting, the destruction of sacred buildings, and the capture and dispersal of people. It is among the fullest contemporary narratives available for the series, although its rhetorical numbers and identifications still require examination. [1, pp. 41–50; 2, ch. XLIX]

At Mathura, the account attributes to Mahmud a striking appreciation of the scale and workmanship of the city’s temples. That admiration did not protect them. The text then reports an order for temples to be burned with naphtha and levelled. Architectural admiration and deliberate destruction coexist in the source; quoting the first while omitting the second would misrepresent the sequence. Conversely, the attributed letter and extravagant estimates of construction costs should not become a modern architectural survey. [1, pp. 44–45]

The same expedition involved different kinds of coercion at different places. Al-Utbi describes conversion and submission under threat at Baran, fighting and death around Kulchand’s stronghold, plunder and captivity at Kannauj, and further violence at Asi and Sharwa. The identity of the Kannauj ruler is complicated by the names transmitted in the sources. A continuous literary narrative must not make every place identification or royal name appear equally secure. [1, pp. 42–49]

Its conclusion is explicit about enslaved people. The chronicler says captives were sold and carried toward regions including Khurasan, Iraq, and Transoxiana, giving very low reported prices. Those figures express the scale of the victory as the author wished to present it; they are not a surviving set of market accounts. The underlying practice is clear: human beings were among the commodities extracted by the campaign. A catalogue limited to gold, silver, and elephants would leave out one of its most consequential forms of plunder. [1, p. 50]

Later expeditions toward Chandela power, Gwalior, and Kalinjar require a more cautious reconstruction. The undated Rahib battle in al-Utbi does not simply give us all the protagonists and motives found in much later accounts. In the Kalinjar narrative printed from the *Tabaqat-i Akbari*, elephants and peace are central to the settlement. It describes negotiation; calling it the storming and occupation of an impregnable fortress would exceed even that later account. [1, pp. 50–51; 5, pp. 462–468]

## Somnath: what the earliest evidence establishes

The attack on Somnath in 1025–26 became the most famous of Mahmud’s Indian expeditions. Its fame has also accumulated episodes and speeches of very different dates. The first task is to distinguish the near-contemporary evidence from the expanded narratives and moral anecdotes written later. Alongside al-Biruni, Gardizi and the poet Farrukhi preserve early material on the expedition and its return, discussed here through Nazim’s source comparison. The extended siege narrative quoted below comes from the much later Ibn al-Athir. Al-Utbi’s surviving Indian campaign narrative does not reach this expedition. Citing him for Somnath would therefore give a false impression of contemporary corroboration. [1, 2, 4–6]

Al-Biruni provides a firm starting point. In *India*, volume II, chapter LVIII, he dates the destruction of Somnath’s image to 416 AH. He describes the upper part being broken, the remainder carried to Ghazna with valuable coverings and ornaments, and portions placed at the hippodrome and the mosque entrance. He identifies the sacred object as a linga of Mahadeva. This testimony supports destruction, seizure, transportation, and deliberate humiliation. It does not supply a death toll or a transcript of an exchange about ransom. [4, p. 103]

His following pages also place Somnath within a coastal and religious world. He relates its fame to maritime travel and its harbour, describes offerings and beliefs, and discusses its situation near the Sarasvati’s mouth. These observations help explain why the site mattered beyond the wealth it contained. Somnath was a place of worship and a landmark in a wider geography of movement, patronage, and reputation. [4, pp. 104–105]

The extended account reproduced from Ibn al-Athir was written roughly two centuries later. It describes the desert approach, assaults, fighting around the temple, killing, and the breaking of the sacred object. Its reported totals of more than 50,000 slain and enormous treasure must remain attributed figures. The account’s chronological discrepancy in the printed edition is another reason to anchor the date in al-Biruni rather than silently combine every detail into a seamless eyewitness narrative. [5, pp. 468–471]

The famous claim that Mahmud chose to be an “idol-breaker” rather than an “idol-seller” belongs to later elaborations of a ransom story. The version in which jewels pour from a broken image presents additional problems when compared with al-Biruni’s description of a linga. Readers can study how such stories developed without treating them as recorded speech. Their dramatic appeal does not make them contemporary evidence. [4, p. 103; 5, pp. 471–476]

The return was also part of the campaign. Later sources describe threatened interception, movement through Sind, water and forage shortages, and attacks in the lower Indus region. They differ about names, routes, and narrative details. The famous story of a deliberately misleading guide and miraculous rescue is a later religious narrative, rather than a route survey. The army’s ability to reach a distant shrine did not remove the hazards of getting its men, animals, and spoils home. [5, pp. 473–475]

## The Indus reprisals and the limits of a final victory

The last conventionally counted expedition concerns Jat communities on the Indus, said to have attacked Mahmud’s army during the return from Somnath. A much later narrative describes boats built at Multan, iron projections, archers and incendiaries, fighting against opposing craft, drowning and killing, and the capture of families concealed on islands. Its treatment of families makes the violence extend beyond a contest between armed crews. [5, pp. 477–478]

The spectacular fleet totals in that account should not be turned into modern naval statistics. Elliot and Dowson themselves express serious doubts about the scale, but their claim that only late authorities recorded the expedition is outdated. Nazim cites the near-contemporary Gardizi for the river operation and its boats, weapons, and archers, and dates it to March 1027 in 418 AH. Gardizi is consulted here through that scholarly discussion, rather than a newly checked direct translation. The earlier strand strengthens the case for the campaign without independently validating every fleet total or mechanical detail. The dating and scale problems belong alongside the described punitive violence. [5, 6]

This final episode also changes the perspective on the preceding victory. Populations along a returning army’s route could threaten its movement. Control of a great treasury did not guarantee control of river channels, landing places, provisions, or local communities. The account of reprisals represents Ghaznavid power answering such resistance; it does not prove that resistance had been impossible or that the entire Indus region could be managed through one battle. [5]

## Religion, wealth, and political power in the sources

Religious purpose is explicit in the Ghaznavid court narrative. Al-Utbi links Mahmud’s programme to holy war, celebrates the defeat of non-Muslim rulers and the destruction of images, and treats the suppression of Multan’s Ismailis as a religious achievement. Those statements should be read as evidence of the monarchy’s proclaimed legitimacy. Removing religion from the account would discard a language through which campaigns were justified and triumphs displayed. [1, pp. 24, 30–41]

The same passages repeatedly concern money, elephants, captives, tribute, appointments, and routes. Religious hostility and material extraction could reinforce each other. A temple could be attacked as a sacred institution, stripped of accumulated valuables, and made into evidence of the conqueror’s triumph at home. A defeated ruler might survive after surrendering resources. A subordinate could be retained or replaced according to the political settlement. The sources support examining these mechanisms together, rather than assigning one exclusive motive to every expedition. [1, 5]

Ghazna’s display of captured wealth made victory visible to audiences far from the battlefield. Al-Utbi describes the exhibition of Nagarkot’s treasure, the market in captives, and monumental building in the capital. Al-Biruni describes sacred objects placed where they could be exposed to public degradation. The journey of a captured object or person thus continued after the army left the place it had attacked. It helped turn distant violence into imperial prestige and resources. [1, pp. 34–35, 50; 2, ch. L; 3, 4]

The human consequences cannot be measured simply by repeating the largest number in a chronicle. Al-Biruni’s opening discussion of India associates Mahmud’s wars with ruined prosperity, dispersal, deepened hostility, and the retreat of learning toward places such as Kashmir and Benares. It is a broad, near-contemporary assessment with its own perspective and generalizations, rather than a census of every region. It belongs beside the triumphant court narrative because it records a different understanding of what conquest had done. [3, ch. I, p. 22]

## How to read the original sources

**Al-Utbi** was close to the Ghaznavid political world. His *Kitab al-Yamini* is foundational for the early campaigns and the expedition to Mathura and Kannauj. Proximity gives the work value but does not turn its praise, casualty totals, speeches, or religious polemic into neutral reporting. The English editions used here also have a history: Reynolds’s 1858 translation works through a medieval Persian version of the Arabic chronicle, while Elliot and Dowson provide selected translated extracts with editorial notes. Wording should be checked against the particular edition cited. [1, 2]

**Al-Biruni**, writing about India around 1030, offers a different kind of evidence: scholarly description, geography, religious explanation, and observations about the effects of conquest. His remarks on the Thanesar and Somnath objects are especially important. He is not a full campaign diarist, and his proximity to the period does not make him an eyewitness of every action. [3, 4]

**Gardizi** provides another near-contemporary strand used in scholarly reconstruction of the chronology. In this guide, claims that depend on its comparison with other sources are identified through Muhammad Nazim’s source-critical study. A scholarly citation to Gardizi is not silently presented as a fresh direct translation of a manuscript. This is particularly important where campaign dates in older compilations have been corrected. [6]

**Ibn al-Athir, Minhaj-i Siraj, Nizam al-Din Ahmad, and Firishta** wrote at differing distances from Mahmud’s lifetime, extending from roughly two centuries to about six centuries after the campaigns. They preserve valuable traditions, and sometimes material unavailable elsewhere, but their accounts are not interchangeable contemporary witnesses. Repetition among dependent narratives does not create several independent confirmations. Later speeches and precise figures need the author’s name and date beside them. [5, 6]

The result is a history with different degrees of resolution. We can speak directly about the killing, enslavement, temple destruction, extraction, and sacred-object humiliation attested in particular sources. We can also say plainly when a location, date, head count, itinerary, or dramatic anecdote remains uncertain. The seventeen articles that follow keep both kinds of statement visible, so readers can follow the campaigns and examine the evidence on which their reconstruction rests.

3. Al-Biruni, [*Alberuni’s India*, translated by Eduard C. Sachau, volume I](https://archive.org/details/alberunisindiaac01biru), 1888 translation, subsequently reprinted; chapter I, p. 22, and chapter XI, pp. 117–118. Near-contemporary observations on the effects of conquest and sacred objects at Ghazna.
4. Al-Biruni, [*Alberuni’s India*, translated by Sachau, volume II](https://archive.org/details/alberunisindiaac00biruiala), chapter LVIII, pp. 103–105. Near-contemporary account of Somnath, its destruction in 416 AH, and transported fragments; [Columbia University scan](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_5949073_002/ldpd_5949073_002.pdf).
5. Elliot and Dowson, [volume II, Appendix Note D, “Mahmud’s Expeditions to India,” pp. 434–478](https://persian.packhum.org/text/080201012/all#p-434). Nineteenth-century editorial reconstruction, containing separately attributed extracts from medieval and early modern writers. See pp. 462–468 for the later central Indian campaigns, pp. 468–476 for Somnath and its return, and pp. 477–478 for the Jat expedition; distinguish those extracts from the editors’ discussion. Ibn al-Athir’s shorter extracts also appear at pp. 244–250.
6. Muhammad Nazim, [*The Life and Times of Sultan Mahmud of Ghazna* (1931)](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/pdf-library/Biographies/998-1030_Sultan-Mahmud-of-Ghazna.pdf), especially the Indian campaign discussion, pp. 86–122, and geographical appendices. A modern source-critical study, used to compare chronology, Gardizi’s testimony, and disputed place identifications; it is not an eleventh-century original source.
