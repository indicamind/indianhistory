## An heir after the age of Pratap

**Raj Singh I**, a son of **Jagat Singh I**, belonged to the later Sisodia ruling house of Mewar. He was not Maharana Pratap or the ruler who first agreed to the 1615 settlement. His political inheritance already included decades of dealings with the Mughal court. [1, 2]

## He inherited a court that commissioned art

His father **Jagat Singh I** had enlarged Udaipur’s courtly and religious landscape. Raj Singh inherited workshops, ritual establishments, and relationships maintained during decades of relative peace. The resources available to a new Rana came from that settled court as well as from the dynasty’s much older reputation for war. [2, 8]

## The great temple belonged to his father’s building programme

The **Jagdish temple** was completed under Jagat Singh I, shortly before Raj Singh’s accession. Identifying its builder matters because the temple later appears in accounts of the invasion during Raj Singh’s reign. He inherited its protection and religious importance; its foundation should not be reassigned to him. [2, 6]

## He met the imperial court as a prince

The Mewar dynastic record places **Prince Raj Singh** at Shah Jahan’s court at **Ajmer in 1643**, representing his father. Sending the heir was consistent with the distinction between a Rana’s own attendance and service by his family. His experience of imperial dealings therefore began before his accession and the later dispute over Chittor. [9]

## He became Rana within an imperial order

Raj Singh succeeded in **1652**. Mewar retained its hereditary government while acknowledging the relationship established after the long war. The reign therefore began inside an existing political arrangement, not as an entirely independent kingdom whose first encounter with Mughal demands occurred only under Aurangzeb. [1, 2, 3]

## A manuscript crossed from one reign into another

The great illustrated **Ramayana**, begun for Jagat Singh, continued into **1653**, after Raj Singh had succeeded him. Colophons make that continuity visible. The manuscript offers evidence for the work of scribes and painters across an accession, rather than requiring every surviving volume to be assigned to a wholly new royal commission. [10]

## Repairing the old fortress provoked a dispute

Attempts to repair Chittor raised the restrictions connected with the **1615** settlement. The Rajasthan textbook describes Mughal intervention against the repairs during Shah Jahan’s reign. The dispute shows why the return of the fortress had not restored an unrestricted right to fortify it. [2, 3]

## Shah Jahan sent Saadullah Khan against the repairs

The Chittor dispute escalated into an imperial expedition under **Saadullah Khan** in **1654**. Works on the fortress were dismantled and Raj Singh had to negotiate under pressure. This confrontation preceded Aurangzeb’s accession: restrictions on Mewar’s fortifications were an issue within the earlier settlement itself. [2, 3]

## The relationship changed across the reign

Cooperation, negotiation, dynastic interests, and conflict all belonged to Mewar’s dealings with the empire. The court’s own writings did not always portray Aurangzeb identically. Treating the entire reign as one uninterrupted war erases changes that help explain the much more serious confrontation at its end. [1, 4]

## The Mughal succession opened opportunities

Shah Jahan’s illness and the conflict among his sons disrupted imperial authority. Regional histories associate Raj Singh with recovering influence and territory during this struggle while dealing cautiously with rival princes. Those opportunities help explain why Mewar’s interests cannot be reduced to permanent loyalty to one Mughal faction. [2, 8]

## Charumati’s marriage acquired a heroic afterlife

Regional tradition places Raj Singh’s marriage to **Charumati of Kishangarh** in **1660**. Tod presents it as the dramatic rescue of a princess threatened with an unwanted imperial marriage. The marriage and its political significance can be discussed while treating the celebrated appeal, conversations, and rescue scenes as transmitted tradition rather than verified dialogue. [3, 8]

## A ruler’s work could be a reservoir

Raj Singh’s reign is associated with major water works. Such projects connected court resources with cultivation and settlement, even while inscriptions celebrated the patron. Their importance is not only the later beauty of the lake: water storage formed part of the country’s material security. [1, 5, 6]

## Badi was another reservoir, with a different dedication

The **Badi lake**, also called **Jana Sagar**, is associated with Raj Singh and named for his mother, **Jana Devi**. It shows that his water patronage was not confined to Rajsamand. Keeping the two reservoirs distinct also prevents the better-known monument from absorbing the history of every lake attributed to this reign. [9, 11]

## Later accounts connect the lake with famine relief

Tod preserves a regional account of severe hunger and failed rains around the beginning of the Rajsamand project. It presents employment on the works as relief as well as royal benefaction. Its dramatic descriptions are literary testimony, not a demographic survey, but they connect water construction with the vulnerability of an agrarian country. [8]

## The lake that carried his name

The construction of **Rajsamand** is conventionally placed between **1662 and 1676**. A dam and worked embankment transformed the landscape. The surviving ghats and pavilions connect the reservoir with an elaborate courtly statement, making the work a source of royal reputation as well as water. [5, 6]

## A river supplied the new lake

Rajsamand stored water from the **Gomti** and its catchment. Choosing a site and holding seasonal inflow were the practical basis of a project later celebrated in poetry. The lake’s scale depended on the relationship between hills, watercourses, and cultivated land, not only on the decoration visible along its shore. [5, 8]

## The ornamental waterfront needed a working dam

Behind the carved lakefront lay the problem of retaining a large body of water. Tod describes the substantial supporting embankment as well as the worked stone. Distinguishing those parts lets the reservoir appear as engineering and infrastructure, alongside the ceremonies and royal praise associated with its completion. [5, 8]

## Rajnagar joined settlement to the water works

**Rajnagar**, associated with Raj Singh’s project, gave the reservoir a neighbouring settlement and another expression of the royal name. Tod describes the town and its defences beside the lake. The undertaking changed an inhabited landscape rather than adding an isolated decorative monument to empty country. [8]

## Navchowki carried the visitor down to the lake

At **Navchowki**, repeated flights of nine steps lead towards the water. These ghats join access, ritual use, and a formal approach to the reservoir. Their surviving arrangement makes a part of Raj Singh’s public works directly legible on the ground. [5]

## Pavilions and gateways framed the patron’s achievement

The surviving marble **chhatris** and **toranas** carry carved divine, animal, and decorative imagery. They turn an embankment into a ceremonial setting. Their craftsmanship also records the labour of designers and stone workers behind a monument remembered principally under the ruler’s name. [5]

## Consecration joined gifts to construction

Accounts of the reservoir include rites and donations at its dedication. Raj Singh’s public identity as a donor accompanied the physical creation of the lake. Such records describe the court’s ideals and expenditure; their praise should not be mistaken for evidence that every inhabitant shared equally in the benefits. [4, 8]

## The court placed its history on stone

The **Rajprashasti** was commissioned to praise Raj Singh and narrate Mewar’s past. Composition during his reign and the later installation of its stone text belong to different stages of the monument’s history. The surviving slabs present an official dynastic argument, rather than an independent account of every event they describe. [4, 5, 9]

## Ranchhod Bhatt gave the commission its literary form

**Ranchhod Bhatt**, also rendered Ranachoda Bhatta, composed the **Rajprashasti**. Naming the author restores a participant whom the monument’s royal emphasis can obscure. A ruler supplied patronage and a political setting; a learned poet shaped the genealogy, narrative, and praise that readers encounter. [4, 9]

## The poem linked the present ruler to a long ancestral past

The work reaches beyond Raj Singh to earlier rulers of Mewar. That structure made his building programme part of a continuous dynastic story. Accounts of remote ancestors require their own evidence: inclusion in a seventeenth-century royal poem does not turn every early episode into contemporary testimony. [4, 5]

## Man Kavi offered another literary record

**Man Kavi’s Rajvilas** belongs to the court’s poetic accounts of Raj Singh. Read alongside the Sanskrit praise and the Persian imperial chronicle, it shows that the reign was recorded in several literary settings. Differences between those works help explain changing political loyalties and descriptions of the same conflict. [4, 9]

## Sadashiv composed the Rajratnakar

The Sanskrit **Rajratnakar**, associated with **Sadashiv**, adds another named work to the reign’s literary record. Its existence broadens the picture of patronage beyond one famous inscription. Courtly historical writing was an active production involving several authors, rather than a single transparent voice of the monarch. [9]

## Images found refuge within Mewar

The reign is remembered for providing refuge to Vaishnava images and their custodians moving from the Mathura region. Their establishment in Mewar became part of its religious landscape. The broad movement is significant, while the exact dates and details of each shrine require the specific tradition and record concerned. [2, 6]

## Shrinathji’s establishment created an enduring centre

The image of **Shrinathji**, brought from the Mathura-Govardhan region, was established at what became **Nathdwara** in **1672**. Raj Singh’s protection helped make Mewar a new home for an existing devotional community. The shrine’s subsequent wealth and importance grew over time and should not all be projected back into that first arrival. [2, 6]

## Dwarkadhish formed a second important sacred centre

The **Dwarkadhish** image and its custodians found protection in Mewar during Raj Singh’s reign. Its later establishment at **Kankroli**, beside Rajsamand, belongs to the shrine’s subsequent history, including work under Sangram Singh II. This community was distinct from Shrinathji’s at Nathdwara; neither shrine’s later buildings should all be credited to Raj Singh. [6, 8, 9]

## Jaswant Singh’s death unsettled Marwar

**Jaswant Singh of Marwar** died at **Jamrud** in late **1678**, away from his home kingdom. The uncertainty over his succession gave the imperial court an opportunity to intervene. Mewar’s later choices developed partly in response to this neighbouring crisis, rather than from a dispute confined to its own territory. [1, 2, 7]

## Ajit Singh’s claim gave the struggle a dynastic focus

The posthumous birth of **Ajit Singh** supplied Marwar’s supporters with an heir around whom to organise resistance. Control of a minor ruler’s person and recognition were political questions with consequences for land and office. Raj Singh’s involvement therefore concerned the future of a neighbouring ruling house as well as imperial policy. [1, 2, 8]

## Durgadas helped sustain the Marwar cause

**Durgadas Rathore** became a leading defender of Ajit Singh’s interests. His work connected Marwar’s scattered resistance with potential allies, including Mewar. The relationship brought Raj Singh into a regional network of chiefs whose aims overlapped without making their territories or dynastic interests identical. [1, 2, 8]

## Kelwa appears in accounts of protection for the heir

Later regional histories associate **Kelwa** in Mewar with shelter for the young Ajit Singh under Raj Singh’s protection. Giving refuge exposed the host to imperial pressure while sustaining the neighbouring dynasty’s claim. The tradition’s extravagant gifts and precise reported exchanges need more caution than the broad political alliance. [2, 8]

## The conflict was not confined to Mewar

The crisis in neighbouring **Marwar** and the struggle over its ruling succession affected the wider region. Raj Singh’s confrontation with Aurangzeb developed within those dynastic and military pressures. Religious policy mattered too, but it was not the only force shaping the changing relationship between the courts. [1, 4]

## The return of jizya sharpened opposition

Aurangzeb reimposed **jizya** in **1679**. Raj Singh became associated with opposition to the policy in later historical accounts. The tax belonged to a larger contest over authority and religious obligation; a biography should not treat it as the sole cause explaining every military decision that followed. [1, 2, 4]

## A famous letter cannot be securely assigned to him

Tod prints a celebrated protest against jizya as Raj Singh’s letter. The later editor notes a competing attribution to **Shivaji**, supported by Jadunath Sarkar’s reading of its internal evidence. The letter belongs to the history of opposition and its transmission; its eloquent words should not be presented as a verified personal quotation from Raj Singh. [8]

## The imperial army entered the kingdom

War brought Mughal forces into Mewar. Raj Singh’s government used the hill country and withdrew from positions it could not securely defend. Such movement preserved capacity to fight, while exposing towns, shrines, and inhabitants to the consequences of an invading army’s operations. [1, 2, 7]

## Withdrawal moved households as well as troops

Regional accounts describe inhabitants leaving exposed lowland positions before imperial occupation. Moving people, provisions, and animals could deny supplies to an invading army, but displacement also carried hardship. Preserving the court and its forces did not mean that ordinary households escaped the material costs of war. [7, 8]

## Jai Singh took part before becoming Rana

Later Mewar accounts place the heir **Jai Singh** with forces operating in the Aravalli country during the invasion. His role connected the defence with the next generation of government. He should be distinguished both from the earlier Amber ruler of the same name and from the imperial princes involved in the campaign. [2, 8]

## Prince Bhim’s operations widened the field

Tod’s regional narrative assigns **Prince Bhim** a western role towards the routes connecting Mewar and Gujarat. It presents attacks on communications and territory as a means of drawing pressure away from the centre. This is the later account’s reconstruction of the campaign, not a verified transcript of Raj Singh’s orders. [8]

## The palace temple appears in the chronicle

The **Ma’asir-i-Alamgiri** records an attack on the great temple before the Rana’s palace at Udaipur. Its account includes the killing of defenders and breaking of images. This is an imperial chronicle preserving destructive acts as part of the campaign, not a later invention added solely by opponents. [1, 7]

## Further temple destruction was reported

The chronicle reports orders and large totals for temple destruction at Udaisagar, around Udaipur, and at Chittor. The detailed article sets out the dated entries. These are the source’s reported figures, not a modern archaeological count; the violence against places of worship nevertheless belongs explicitly to its narrative. [1, 7]

## Resistance depended on more than a fortress

Mewar’s forces continued acting from difficult country despite the devastation of urban and sacred sites. The terrain could complicate the invader’s operations without shielding every settlement. The military history and the suffering of inhabitants should be read together rather than treating survival in the hills as proof that no damage mattered. [1, 2, 4]

## Holding towns did not secure every road

The imperial army’s occupation of important places left the continuing problem of movement through difficult country. Regional accounts emphasise pressure on routes and provisions. The campaign therefore involved a contest over communications as well as possession of Udaipur, explaining how organised resistance could continue after the city had been entered. [7, 8]

## He died before the conflict was settled

Raj Singh died in **1680**. His son **Jai Singh** inherited the continuing confrontation, and the subsequent **1681** settlement belonged to that next reign. The distinction matters: Raj Singh did not personally make every later agreement attached to the war begun during his rule. [1, 2]

## Jai Singh’s accession changed the ruler, not the crisis

**Jai Singh** succeeded his father while the confrontation continued. Command, negotiation, and the maintenance of allied relationships now became the new Rana’s responsibility. Following the succession prevents the last phase of the war from being compressed into achievements belonging entirely to Raj Singh. [1, 2]

## Prince Akbar’s rebellion followed Raj Singh’s death

The rebellion of Aurangzeb’s son **Prince Akbar**, supported by Rajput allies in **1681**, occurred after Raj Singh had died. It grew from the regional crisis he had confronted, but his successor and surviving allies made the decisions involved. It belongs in the aftermath rather than among Raj Singh’s personal military actions. [1, 2, 8]

## His successor carried the praise onto stone

The dynasty’s later account credits **Jai Singh** with having the Rajprashasti engraved. That distinction separates Raj Singh’s original literary patronage from his son’s act of monumental preservation. The text’s continuing public presence therefore records an investment by more than one generation of the ruling house. [9]

## The sources preserve different political moments

The Rajprashasti, court poetry, Mughal chronicle, and later histories record different purposes and moments. A praise inscription and a hostile military account cannot simply be merged into one unqualified voice. Comparing them helps recover a ruler who built, negotiated, patronised, and fought under changing conditions. [1, 4–7]

## Public works and late war shaped his legacy

Raj Singh left a major reservoir and an unusually durable statement of courtly history, while his final years brought destructive conflict with Aurangzeb. Those different parts of the reign belong together. The lake and inscriptions show what the court wished to preserve; the war shows the costs of its political struggle. [1–7]
