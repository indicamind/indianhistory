**The queen rode into battle on an elephant called Sarman. Across the approaches to her mountain refuge, a Mughal commander was bringing up his guns.**

Rani Durgavati had already won one encounter. She had wanted to strike again that night, before the enemy could strengthen its position. Her commanders would not agree. By morning, the opportunity had hardened into a much more dangerous battlefield. That is how the *Akbarnama*, the official chronicle of the very empire attacking her, tells this extraordinary turn in the campaign of 1564. [1]

The detail catches the imagination because it brings her close: a ruler thinking about hours, ground, and the next enemy move. The famous last stand was still ahead. At this moment, she was trying to win.

To understand what she was fighting for, we have to leave the battlefield for a while. Behind the queen stood a kingdom, a governing household, a young son, and a landscape that rewards a longer look.

## A kingdom worth looking at twice

Garha-Katanga occupied a substantial part of central India, in the wider region often called Gondwana. The names **Garha**, **Garha-Mandla**, and **Garha-Katanga** appear in accounts of its history. They refer to a particular political formation within a larger Gond world; Gondwana should not be imagined as a single, permanently unified state. [1][3]

Look at the country around Jabalpur and the attraction becomes tangible. Rock rises abruptly from cultivated land. Hilltops offer long views. The Narmada moves through a landscape of striking contrasts, including the pale cliffs of Bhedaghat. Water, stone, woodland, and fields make an arresting setting for a story of power.

They also suggest practical questions. Who controlled the approaches? Where could supplies be stored? Which routes would accommodate an army, and which would force it into a narrow front? A beautiful landscape can be demanding military ground.

The surviving image of Durgavati belongs inside that landscape. An elephant, an armoured rider, a defended pass: each becomes more intelligible when the hills and rivers come into view.

## From the Chandela world to a Gond court

Durgavati is conventionally remembered as having been born on **5 October 1524**, into a Chandela ruling family associated with the Mahoba–Kalinjar region. Her university’s commemorative biography gives that date and places her birth at Kalinjar. Such precise early biographical details reach us through later accounts, and deserve more caution than the well-attested fact of her rule and resistance. [2]

Her marriage to **Dalpat Shah**, son of the powerful Gond ruler **Sangram Shah**, connected two ruling houses. The *Akbarnama* explains the match in terms of the families’ unequal fortunes and contains its author’s prejudices about status. That is one court historian’s explanation of an alliance, rather than a transparent record of what either spouse wanted. [1]

What can be said confidently is that her adult political life belonged to Garha-Katanga. Calling her a Chandela princess describes her ancestry. Calling her a queen of a Gond kingdom describes the state she governed. Both are part of the story.

The union also invites us to look beyond neat boxes. Royal households could bring together different lineages, military followers, religious affiliations, and political expectations. Their ability to work together mattered more than the tidy labels on a modern family tree.

## The child had the title. His mother held the power.

Dalpat Shah’s death left a young son, **Vir Narayan**, also written Bir Narayan. The conventional chronology places the beginning of Durgavati’s regency around **1550**. The *Akbarnama* is unusually clear about the arrangement: the boy received the royal title, while his mother exercised authority with the support of senior officials. [1][2]

That distinction gives the story its political charge. A regent had to turn a dynastic claim into functioning government. Orders needed obedience. Officers needed confidence in the court. A royal child could embody continuity; somebody still had to make decisions.

Durgavati’s survival in power is therefore an achievement before we reach a single battlefield. Her name remained attached to the state because she governed it. Her final campaign becomes more substantial when we remember the years of responsibility that preceded it.

There is a small chronological wrinkle. The usual 1550–1564 dates suggest about fourteen years, while Abu’l Fazl assigns her sixteen years of rule. The difference should remain visible. It is not a reason to invent an exact accession date that reconciles every account. [1][2]

## The allure of a queen who could shoot

Abu’l Fazl credits Durgavati with skill in both the bow and the gun. He also records her reputation as a hunter. His account of her determination to kill a reported tiger has the flavour of a royal character story: a memorable illustration of resolve rather than a diary entry we can independently check. [1]

Still, the combination is revealing. Her military image includes firearms as well as older weapons. She inhabited a world in which technologies overlapped, and skill could be expressed through several of them.

There is glamour in the image: the poised archer, the hunter, the queen whose presence could animate a court and an army. But the attraction of her story lies in the connection between that reputation and actual command. Courage was something her officers had to act alongside.

We can appreciate the force of the image without fabricating her appearance, private conversations, or thoughts. The recorded career supplies enough electricity of its own.

## Government before glory

The familiar battle story can crowd out everything else. Yet the university’s account also remembers Durgavati for water works, patronage of learning, and the welfare of her subjects. These are broad retrospective claims; they are best treated as evidence of her governing reputation rather than as a complete administrative record. [2]

Water deserves particular attention. A reservoir can be less spectacular than a royal charge and more consequential to the people living beside it. Storage, irrigation, and reliable access connect the authority of a court to the rhythms of cultivation and daily life.

Thinking about those responsibilities changes the emotional scale of the invasion. The object under attack was a working kingdom. A court’s loss could disturb revenue, local authority, military service, and the security of households far from the royal residence.

It is easier to paint a sword than a functioning administration. Remembering the latter gives the sword something worth defending.

## Forts, stone, and the temptation of treasure

**Singorgarh**, also written Singaurgarh, survives in the Damoh region as a fort associated with Dalpat Shah and Durgavati. **Chauragarh**, near Chaugan in the Narsinghpur region, was another major stronghold of the Gond rulers. Its name will return at the darkest point in the campaign. [4][5]

The remains encourage a different way of looking at royal wealth. Treasure needed walls, guards, routes, and places of refuge. A fortress could gather resources into a defensible position. That concentration could also make it an irresistible target.

Jabalpur’s **Madan Mahal** supplies another powerful image of the regional past: masonry set against an enormous natural rock, a structure whose relationship with its hill is impossible to ignore. It belongs to the older Gond architectural landscape associated with Durgavati’s memory. It should not be presented as a building whose construction by her has been demonstrated. [6]

Stand back from the photographs and the political picture begins to take shape: authority dispersed across difficult country, yet anchored at places that could be occupied, besieged, or lost.

## An earlier enemy: Baz Bahadur of Malwa

Durgavati’s military reputation preceded the Mughal invasion. Accounts of her reign credit her with repelling **Baz Bahadur**, the ruler of Malwa, whose court is also remembered for his association with Roopmati. Modern biographical treatments place this success among the achievements that established her standing as a ruler. [2][3]

That matters because the queen of 1564 already had experience of a dangerous neighbour. Her willingness to resist did not emerge suddenly at the sight of a Mughal banner.

It also complicates a story too easily reduced to one overwhelming empire and one helpless opponent. Garha-Katanga had its own military history, ambitions, and capacity to act. Durgavati had reasons to believe her kingdom could defend itself, even as the balance of power around it changed.

The drama comes from the gap between proven ability and a new scale of threat. A victory over one enemy could strengthen confidence without guaranteeing success against another.

## The empire came through Asaf Khan

The commander who invaded was **Khwaja Abd al-Majid Asaf Khan**, an officer of Akbar. Akbar himself did not lead the fighting against Durgavati. The campaign nevertheless formed part of Mughal expansion, and the court chronicle places it under imperial authority. [1]

This distinction keeps responsibility and action in focus. The emperor’s power enabled a commander’s campaign; the commander made decisions on the ground. A reader can recognise both without placing Akbar personally in the valley.

Abu’l Fazl’s narrative gives wealth a conspicuous role in Asaf Khan’s interest. Reconnaissance, revenue, stores, and the prospect of acquisition enter the account before the final clash. The invasion was aimed at a state with resources worth taking. [1]

Once we see that, the queen’s refusal becomes a refusal of subordination with concrete consequences. Behind the language of submission stood control over land, people, and wealth. Behind the language of conquest stood the force required to obtain it.

## Narrai: where the landscape entered the battle

Durgavati’s final campaign is associated with **Narrai**, in country shaped by hills and the **Gaur and Narmada rivers**. Abu’l Fazl calls the refuge Narhi; later accounts commonly use Narrai. The geographical descriptions emphasise difficult access and a narrow approach. [1][2]

A pass can reduce the advantage of numbers. An army unable to deploy its full strength has to win room in which to fight. A defender who knows the approaches may be able to turn movement itself into a hazard.

The advantage is conditional. A narrow entrance can protect a position while the defender controls it. If the attacker occupies it and brings guns into place, the same geography can restrict escape and reinforcement.

This is the central tension in the Narrai story. The hills offered an opportunity. They did not make the queen invulnerable. Her decisions had to preserve the value of the ground before her enemy changed the conditions of the fight.

## The night that might have changed the morning

The *Akbarnama* describes an initial reverse for the Mughal force. It then attributes to Durgavati a proposal for a night attack, intended to act before Asaf Khan could secure the approach with artillery. Her officers opposed it. The chronicle presents the following morning as a vindication of her warning. [1]

It is a gripping episode because the alternative remains imaginable. A few hours separate a vulnerable enemy from a stronger position. A council’s refusal becomes a turning point.

Yet imagination has to stop short of certainty. We cannot know that a night attack would have won the campaign. Darkness might have created opportunities and confusion for either side. The possibility of a better outcome is not proof of one.

The scene tells us something firmer about Durgavati’s remembered command: she could identify a changing tactical problem, argue for action, and fail to persuade the people whose cooperation she needed. Even a forceful ruler depended on others.

## The queen on Sarman

The next image has extraordinary staying power: **Durgavati mounted on Sarman, her war elephant**, with fighting closing around her. The elephant’s name comes from the court chronicle, giving the episode one of its most memorable individual details. [1]

An elephant makes its rider conspicuous. That visibility helps explain the emotional force of the image: the commander can be seen by her own followers, and by the opposing army. In a painting, it lifts the human figure above the press of combat.

Vir Narayan also fought. He was wounded and taken from the field, an event after which the queen’s position deteriorated in the chronicle’s account. [1]

Here the regency acquires an unbearable intimacy. The son for whom she had governed was now exposed to the same campaign that threatened the kingdom. The dynastic future and the immediate battle occupied the same ground.

Abu’l Fazl describes the fighting passing from missiles to close combat. He credits Vir Narayan with repeatedly driving back the attackers before the prince was wounded. Durgavati ordered trusted men to carry him to safety. As her force broke up, she continued fighting with those who remained. This is the conqueror’s account, and even its triumphal language leaves room for the determination of the people being defeated. [1]

We do not need invented farewell speeches to feel the pressure of that situation. The recorded circumstances carry it.

## What an imperial painting lets us see

The painting above deserves more than a passing glance. Follow the horses, the crowded bodies, and the stony ground. The composition makes battle feel packed and unstable, a contest whose movements the eye has to work to follow.

The Victoria and Albert Museum identifies the artists as **Kesav Kalan and Nar Singh**, and dates the work to approximately **1590–1595**. It belongs to the illustrated *Akbarnama*, a work produced for Akbar’s court. It is therefore evidence of how the campaign was represented within the victorious empire, decades after the event. [7]

That is a different kind of evidence from a battlefield plan. The picture can show artistic choices, a court’s visual language, and the importance attached to an episode. Its painted arrangement cannot by itself establish the exact position of every soldier in 1564.

The distinction makes the image more interesting. We are looking at conquest being turned into a royal memory—and at an opponent important enough to occupy that memory.

## 24 June 1564

Durgavati’s death is commemorated on **24 June 1564**. Accounts describe her being wounded by arrows and, facing capture, ending her own life with a dagger. The university biography preserves the familiar commemorative version; Abu’l Fazl provides a much fuller narrative of the final exchange. [1][2]

It is a terrible ending. The language of honour in the sources explains the terms in which the event was remembered. It does not make death a measure of anyone’s worth, or captivity a fault of those who suffered it.

The most enduring image is of a ruler refusing to be carried into an enemy’s victory. But the life should remain larger than that final act. The woman at Narrai had governed, maintained a court, raised a son, confronted neighbours, and made military decisions. The ending draws its force from everything it cut short.

Her death did not turn the campaign into a victory for her kingdom. It turned defeat into a memory that conquest could not easily absorb.

## Chauragarh: the story does not end with the queen

After Durgavati’s death, the fighting reached **Chauragarh**, where Vir Narayan’s resistance also ended in death. The fort’s association with the prince survives in the Narsinghpur district’s historical account. Its loss joined the fate of the dynasty to the capture of a major stronghold. [5][8]

The *Akbarnama* also records a mass burning of women at the fort, described as **jauhar**, and makes coercion explicit in its account. This part of the narrative should be read as a catastrophe experienced by people inside a collapsing defence. Turning it into a decorative scene of heroism would conceal its violence. [1]

The difference between the queen’s death and what followed at the fort matters. They were separate episodes, involving different circumstances and people. A dramatic biography can compress them until the distinction disappears; history is better served by keeping the sequence clear.

And the people affected extend beyond the named rulers. A campaign reaches households, attendants, soldiers, and dependants whose names a chronicle may never preserve. Remembering them gives the royal tragedy its human surroundings.

## The treasury that tempted the victors

Then the chronicle opens the treasure rooms. Its inventory includes gold and silver, pearls and jewels, decorated vessels, images, and animal figures made of gold. These details belong to Abu’l Fazl’s description of the booty at Chauragarh. The abundance is part of his explanation of why the fort attracted such determined attackers. [1]

The story takes an almost bitter turn. Wealth accumulated as a source of royal security had become a reason for soldiers to risk their lives taking the fortress. The walls guarded a prize whose existence helped draw danger towards it.

Asaf Khan’s conduct then becomes a problem for the empire that had enabled his victory. Abu’l Fazl accuses him of withholding valuable spoils and sending only two hundred elephants out of a thousand captured. Those figures are the chronicler’s claims, rather than an audited inventory. His indignation nevertheless reveals another struggle inside conquest: who would possess its rewards? [1]

For Durgavati’s household, the loss was a dynasty’s catastrophe. For the imperial court, the same campaign could become a dispute over a commander’s obedience and the treasure he failed to forward. The change of perspective is chilling.

## The people inside her resistance

Among the names Abu’l Fazl records on Durgavati’s side are **Shams Khan Miyana** and **Mubarak Khan Baluch**, whom he credits with fighting bravely. Elsewhere in the account appear the officials and commanders who advised her, defended the approaches, and remained with her in danger. [1]

The names give the resistance texture. They also make a simple division into religious camps inadequate to describe the people actually fighting. A campaign of Mughal expansion could bring a queen into conflict with an emperor while men of different backgrounds served her cause.

The contrast between an imperial army and a provincial court remains central. But inside each were personal loyalties, offices, obligations, and ambitions. Recovering even a few of those individuals makes the queen’s command more concrete.

Sarman is remembered by name. These men deserve to be remembered by name too.

## Read her enemy’s praise—and his contempt

Abu’l Fazl’s account is indispensable, but its loyalties are visible. He writes from within Akbar’s imperial project. He can admire Durgavati’s ability while treating her refusal to submit as a failing. His description of the Gonds also carries social prejudice. [1]

This creates a productive tension for the reader. Praise from an opponent is striking, but praise does not make the account neutral. A historian still has to examine what its author wants victory, obedience, gender, and status to mean.

The same care applies to later celebration. A commemorative biography can preserve a ruler’s importance while smoothing uncertainty into a confident story. A mural can give courage a magnificent form while imagining a scene its artist never witnessed.

Reading these sources together gives us more than a choice between admiration and suspicion. It lets us ask better questions of each: what does this account know, what does it infer, and what does it want its audience to remember?

## A queen in public memory

Durgavati’s visual afterlife ranges from courtly portraiture to modern public art and a postage stamp. Each form changes the encounter. A portrait invites attention to bearing and presence. A battle scene fills the frame with conflict. A stamp carries an official act of remembrance into ordinary circulation.

**India Post issued a commemorative stamp on 24 June 1988.** The university at Jabalpur had been renamed in her honour in **1983**, according to its own account. In **2023**, the Government of India marked her birth commemoration with another coin and stamp and a memorial foundation ceremony at Jabalpur. These are dated acts of remembrance, rather than evidence for every detail of her sixteenth-century biography. [2][9][10]

The 1954 mural at the beginning of this article makes a particularly powerful modern choice. Durgavati is preparing for action. The viewer meets a queen with work still before her, surrounded by the people through whom command becomes possible.

## The authority that survives the last stand

Durgavati’s story holds the eye because it has such vivid forms: the rock fortress, the river gorge, the elephant, the queen visible above her followers. Its lasting interest comes from the decisions inside those images.

She had to govern through other people. She had to judge an enemy. She had to decide what the terrain could give her, and how quickly its advantages could disappear. The surviving accounts let us see flashes of that work, even through the purposes and prejudices of their authors.

The campaign ended in conquest. That fact should retain its weight. So should the fact that the ruler it destroyed remained an unmistakable presence in the story told by the conquerors.

Look again at the mural. The queen is still preparing. Her attendants are still gathered. The outcome has not yet closed around her.

**Rani Durgavati deserves to be remembered in that fullness: a sovereign with a kingdom to run, a battle to think through, and a future she was still trying to secure.**
