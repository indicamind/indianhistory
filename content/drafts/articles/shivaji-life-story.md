## A warrior's birth at Shivneri fort

Shivaji Bhonsle was born at the hill fortress of **Shivneri** near Junnar on **19 February 1630**. His mother named him after the local goddess Shivai Devi, seeking divine protection during an era of constant warfare. His birth took place in a mountain stronghold surrounded by rugged Sahyadri ravines. [1, 2]

## Lineage of Shahaji and Jijabai

His father **Shahaji Bhonsle** was an accomplished military commander who served the Deccan sultanates of Ahmadnagar and Bijapur. His mother **Jijabai** was the daughter of Lakhuji Jadhav of Sindkhed, claiming descent from the ancient Seuna Yadavas. The marriage linked two of the most distinguished Maratha noble houses. [1, 3]

## The devastating Deccan famine of 1630

Shivaji’s earliest infancy coincided with the catastrophic **Deccan famine of 1630–1632**, which devastated western India. Prolonged drought followed by pestilence killed millions, depopulating agricultural villages across Maharashtra. Witnessing the suffering of peasants instilled in the Bhonsle family an enduring concern for rural rehabilitation and grain security. [1, 2]

## Growing up in the ruined township of Pune

While Shahaji campaigned in Karnataka for the Bijapur sultanate, Jijabai brought the young Shivaji to the ancestral estate (*jagir*) at **Pune**. Pune had been burned and razed by Adilshahi forces during earlier border wars. Mother and son set up residence in a modest mansion, beginning the reconstruction of the desolated district. [1, 3]

## Driving the golden plow through Pune’s soil

To dispel a local curse that warned anyone who rebuilt Pune would face ruin, Jijabai and Shivaji drove a ceremonial **golden plow** through the earth. They distributed agricultural tools and encouraged displaced farmers to reoccupy their fields. The symbolic act transformed popular dread into collective hope for a new beginning. [1, 3]

## The mentorship of Dadoji Konddeo

Shahaji appointed his trusted Brahmin administrator, **Dadoji Konddeo**, to manage the Pune estates and mentor his son. Dadoji instructed Shivaji in revenue assessment, local justice, horsemanship, and martial discipline. Under his strict guidance, Shivaji learned the administrative fundamentals of good governance and fair land settlement. [1, 2]

## Exploring the hills with Mavala comrades

In the forested valleys west of Pune, Shivaji spent his youth exploring the Sahyadri mountains alongside the local hill dwellers, the **Mavalas**. He gained an intimate knowledge of secret mountain passes, ravines, and fort approaches. Hill youths like Tanaji Malusare and Yesaji Kank formed an intensely devoted circle of comrades. [1, 3]

## The sacred oath at Rohideshwar temple

Tradition records that around 1645, the teenage Shivaji and his Mavala companions gathered at the secluded Shiva temple of **Raireshwar** near Rohida. They took a solemn blood-oath dedicating their lives to the establishment of **Hindavi Swarajya** ("self-rule of the native people"). They renounced vassalage to distant sultanates in pursuit of freedom. [1, 2]

## The capture of the hill fortress of Torna

At approximately sixteen years of age, Shivaji executed his first major tactical strike by seizing the high-altitude hill fort of **Torna** from Bijapur. Exploiting the lax vigilance of the garrison, the Mavalas took the fortress without bloodshed. The coup announced the emergence of an ambitious, fearless Maratha leader. [1, 3]

## Buried treasure and fort renovations

While renovating the battlements of Torna (renamed **Prachandagad**), Shivaji's workers discovered jars of hidden gold buried in the masonry. Rejecting personal enrichment, Shivaji used every coin to purchase firearms, ammunition, and construction materials. The windfall financed the fortification of neighboring mountain ridges. [1, 2]

## Raising the impregnable citadel of Rajgad

On the high Murumbdev hill nearby, Shivaji constructed the majestic mountain fortress of **Rajgad**. Its triple natural spurs (*machis*) and towering central citadel (*balekilla*) made it nearly unassailable to seventeenth-century siege artillery. Rajgad served as Shivaji's primary royal capital and administrative headquarters for more than twenty-five years. [1, 2]

## Securing Chakan and Kondana

Through swift surprise and local diplomacy, Shivaji secured control over the forts of **Chakan** and **Kondana** (later Sinhagad). These strongholds guarded the eastern and southern approaches to Pune, protecting the agrarian heartland. Bijapur authorities initially hesitated to retaliate, regarding the actions as manageable local insubordination. [1, 3]

## Gaining the mountain gateway of Purandar

Shivaji next gained the strategic hill fortress of **Purandar**, which controlled the route between Pune and the Saswad basin. Resolving an internal succession dispute among the deceased keeper's sons, Shivaji took possession of the fort without bloodshed. Purandar became his principal military redoubt in times of invasion. [1, 2]

## Shahaji’s arrest and hostage crisis in Bijapur

Alarmed by Shivaji's expanding power, the Adilshahi sultan arrested **Shahaji** in Karnataka in 1648, holding him in irons as a hostage. The sultan demanded that Shivaji surrender his forts in exchange for his father’s life. Shivaji faced a grave choice between abandoning Swarajya and risking his father's execution. [1, 2]

## Diplomatic appeals to the Mughal Emperor

Shivaji demonstrated sharp diplomatic instincts by opening direct communications with Mughal Prince **Murad Baksh** and Emperor Shah Jahan. He offered Maratha military service in exchange for imperial pressure on Bijapur. Fearing Mughal intervention, the Bijapur court released Shahaji, teaching Shivaji the value of diplomatic counterweights. [1, 2]

## The strategic conquest of the Javli valley

In 1656, Shivaji seized the dense, rugged mountain valley of **Javli** from the More family, who ruled as staunch Bijapuri vassals. The conquest removed a dangerous regional rival and opened direct access to the wealthy Konkan coastline. Thousands of skilled Mavala infantry joined Shivaji’s army following the victory. [1, 2]

## Constructing the sentinel fortress of Pratapgad

To secure the newly conquered Javli territory, Shivaji ordered his chief builder Moropant Pingle to construct the fortress of **Pratapgad** atop a high ridge. Overlooking the Radtondi pass, the citadel commanded the highway between the Deccan and the coast. It soon became the stage for his most famous confrontation. [1, 3]

## First clashes with the Mughal Empire

During Aurangzeb’s viceroyalty of the Deccan in 1657, Shivaji launched bold surprise raids on the Mughal garrisons at **Ahmednagar** and **Junnar**. His horsemen carried off gold and horses, retreating swiftly into the hills. The raids alerted the Mughal authorities that a formidable new power had arisen in western India. [1, 2, 4]

## Establishing shipyards at Kalyan and Bhiwandi

Recognizing that control of western India required coastal access, Shivaji captured the ports of **Kalyan** and **Bhiwandi** in the late 1650s. He established shipyards along the Ulhas river, employing local Portuguese-trained carpenters to lay the keels of his first armed vessels. His maritime ambitions had begun. [1, 3]

## The Bijapur court dispatches General Afzal Khan

In 1659, the Adilshahi dowager queen Badi Sahiba commissioned the veteran general **Afzal Khan** to destroy Shivaji. Afzal Khan commanded a powerful army of ten thousand cavalry, heavy artillery, and elite infantry. He bragged openly in full court that he would bring the rebel back in chains. [1, 2, 4]

## Desecration of sacred shrines in Maharashtra

Marching northward, Afzal Khan deliberately attacked and desecrated the celebrated Hindu pilgrimage shrines at **Tuljapur** and **Pandharpur**. By desecrating the temples of Bhavani and Vithoba, he sought to provoke Shivaji out of his mountain retreats into open plains where Bijapuri cavalry held the advantage. [1, 2]

## Shivaji retreats into the forests of Pratapgad

Shivaji refused to take the bait on the plains, withdrawing his forces into the dense, impassable forests surrounding **Pratapgad**. The thick jungles and narrow mountain trails completely neutralized the Khan’s heavy artillery and cavalry. Afzal Khan was forced to halt his army at Wai and open negotiations. [1, 3]

## Arranging the summit at the foot of the fort

Diplomatic envoys Krishnaji Bhaskar and Pantaji Gopinath arranged a personal summit between the two commanders at the foot of Pratapgad. The meeting was set in an ornate pavilion, with both leaders agreeing to bring only two bodyguards. Shivaji suspected treachery and prepared his defense with meticulous care. [1, 2]

## Concealed chainmail and the steel claws

Beneath his white muslin tunic, Shivaji wore a light coat of **chainmail** (*chilkhath*) and fitted a concealed steel skullcap inside his turban. In his left hand he concealed the steel tiger claws (**bagh nakh**), and in his right sleeve hid a curved dagger (*bichwa*). He placed his army on high alert in the surrounding woods. [1, 2, 4]

## The fateful embrace in the summit tent

On **10 November 1659**, Shivaji entered the pavilion where the towering Afzal Khan awaited him. As they stepped forward for the formal embrace, the Khan locked Shivaji’s neck in a powerful chokehold and plunged his dagger into his ribcage. The hidden steel chainmail deflected the blade, saving Shivaji's life. [1, 2]

## Retaliation and the death of Afzal Khan

Breaking free from the Khan’s grip, Shivaji drove the **bagh nakh** into the general’s abdomen and struck with his dagger. Wounded mortally, Afzal Khan cried out for help as his bodyguards rushed forward. Shivaji’s personal guards Jiva Mahala and Sambhaji Kavji killed the Khan’s attendants and decapitated the general. [1, 2, 4]

## The ambush and rout at the Battle of Pratapgad

On a prearranged cannon signal from Pratapgad’s ramparts, Maratha infantry under Moropant Pingle and Kanhoji Jedhe charged out of the dense forests. The disoriented Bijapuri army was routed in the **Battle of Pratapgad**, losing its artillery, elephants, and treasury. The victory established Shivaji as a premier military power. [1, 3]

## Humane treatment of captives and spoils

Following the victory, Shivaji enforced strict rules of chivalry: wounded Bijapuri soldiers were provided medical treatment, captive officers were ransomed or released with gifts, and women were protected. Afzal Khan’s body was buried with full Islamic military honors on Pratapgad, where his tomb stands to this day. [1, 3]

## Capturing the southern fortress of Panhala

Capitalizing on his triumph at Pratapgad, Shivaji swept southward through the Kolhapur district, capturing the massive fortress of **Panhala** in late 1659. He also took the strongholds of Vasantgad and Khelna (renamed Vishalgad). His kingdom expanded rapidly across the southern Sahyadri ranges. [1, 2]

## Encircled by Siddi Jauhar at Panhala

In early 1660, Bijapur dispatched a veteran Abyssinian commander, **Siddi Jauhar**, at the head of a massive army to besiege Panhala. Siddi Jauhar threw an airtight siege cordon around the mountain, cutting off all food and supplies. For five grueling months, Shivaji remained trapped inside the fortress. [1, 2]

## The sacrifice of the barber Shiva Kashid

Realizing that surrender was inevitable if he stayed, Shivaji devised an escape plan using a loyal lookalike, a humble barber named **Shiva Kashid**. Disguised as Shivaji, Shiva Kashid led a decoy palanquin out of the fort, deliberately allowing himself to be captured to give the real king a head start. [1, 2]

## Midnight breakout through torrential monsoon rains

On a stormy night in July 1660, while monsoon downpours lashed the cliffs, Shivaji and six hundred Mavalas slipped down the western ramparts of Panhala. They marched swiftly through mud and swollen streams toward Vishalgad, thirty miles away. Pursuing Bijapuri cavalry discovered the decoy and rode hard to intercept them. [1, 2]

## Baji Prabhu Deshpande holds the narrow gorge

At the narrow mountain gorge of Ghodkhind, Shivaji’s commander **Baji Prabhu Deshpande** volunteered to hold the pass with three hundred men while Shivaji pushed on to Vishalgad. Armed with two-handed swords, the defenders repelled wave after wave of Bijapuri cavalry for hours in desperate hand-to-hand combat. [1, 2]

## The cannon signal from Vishalgad

Baji Prabhu was mortally wounded but refused to yield until distant cannon fire echoed across the hills, signaling that Shivaji had safely breached the enemy lines at Vishalgad. The heroic commander then died peacefully. The bloodstained gorge was forever renamed **Pavankhind** ("the sacred pass") in Maratha memory. [1, 2]

## Shaista Khan occupies Pune and Lal Mahal

In 1660, Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb sent his maternal uncle **Shaista Khan** with one hundred thousand troops to subjugate the Deccan. Shaista Khan drove the Marathas back, captured Pune, and took up residence in Shivaji’s own childhood palace, **Lal Mahal**. The Mughal army laid waste to the surrounding countryside for three years. [1, 2, 4]

## Firangoji Narsala’s epic defense of Chakan

At the plains fort of **Chakan**, Maratha commander Firangoji Narsala held out against Shaista Khan’s entire army for fifty-six days. When a mined bastion collapsed, the defenders fought courageously in the breach. Shaista Khan was so impressed by Firangoji’s bravery that he offered him imperial rank, which the commander respectfully declined. [1, 2]

## The midnight commando raid on Lal Mahal

On the night of **5 April 1663**, Shivaji executed one of the most daring commando raids in military history. Leading four hundred handpicked Mavalas disguised as a wedding party, he infiltrated Pune, bypassed sentries, and breached the walls of Lal Mahal. Shaista Khan escaped through a window with severed fingers, but his son and guards were killed. [1, 2, 4]

## The lightning strike on the port of Surat

In January 1664, Shivaji marched hundreds of miles across rugged terrain to strike **Surat**, the wealthiest commercial emporium of the Mughal Empire. For six days, Maratha forces systematically looted imperial treasuries and merchant storehouses to replenish their war finances, while deliberately sparing European trading factories and poor residents. [1, 2, 4]

## The loss of Shahaji in Karnataka

In early 1664, Shivaji received the sorrowful news that his father **Shahaji** had died in a hunting accident near Basavapatna in Karnataka. Shivaji was deeply grief-stricken, and persuaded his mother Jijabai not to perform sati, urging that her guidance was essential for the survival of Swarajya. [1, 2]

## Building the island fortress of Sindhudurg

To secure the Konkan coastline against European navies and the Siddis of Janjira, Shivaji began constructing the ocean fortress of **Sindhudurg** in 1664. Situated on the rocky islet of Kurte at Malvan, its two miles of stone walls were anchored directly in the sea using molten lead foundations. [1, 3]

## Founding the independent Maratha Navy

Shivaji appointed **Darya Sarang** and **Maynak Bhandari** as his chief admirals, creating an indigenous navy consisting of gunboats (*gurabs*) and swift patrol craft (*galbats*). The fleet protected coastal trade, combated piracy, and challenged Portuguese and British naval dominance in the Arabian Sea. [1, 3]

## Aurangzeb dispatches Mirza Raja Jai Singh

Enraged by the humiliation at Pune and the sack of Surat, Aurangzeb dispatched his premier general, **Mirza Raja Jai Singh I** of Amber, to crush Shivaji. Jai Singh combined seasoned Rajput diplomacy with extensive military force, enlisting Bijapur and the Portuguese while encircling Shivaji's mountain heartland. [1, 2]

## Murarbaji’s heroic charge at Purandar

In 1665, Jai Singh and Diler Khan laid siege to the pivotal fortress of **Purandar**. Maratha commander **Murarbaji Deshpande** led a fierce sally of seven hundred Mavalas against Diler Khan's trenches, slaying hundreds of attackers before being shot through the chest. Purandar’s fall became only a matter of time. [1, 2]

## The Treaty of Purandar: twenty-three forts ceded

Recognizing that continued resistance against overwhelming imperial hosts would destroy his people, Shivaji entered Jai Singh’s camp to negotiate terms in June 1665. Under the **Treaty of Purandar**, Shivaji surrendered twenty-three forts and pledged military assistance against Bijapur, while retaining twelve strongholds. [1, 2, 4]

## Riding north to the imperial court at Agra

Persuaded by Jai Singh’s personal assurances of safety and honor, Shivaji traveled to the Mughal capital of **Agra** in May 1666, accompanied by his nine-year-old son Sambhaji. He expected to be treated as a major sovereign ally in the Deccan. The journey took him across the heart of imperial India. [1, 2, 4]

## The public confrontation at Aurangzeb’s darbar

On **12 May 1666**, Shivaji attended Aurangzeb’s fiftieth birthday darbar in the Diwan-i-Khas at Agra. The Emperor received him coldly, presenting no robe of honor and placing him behind third-tier courtiers (*panchhazari* mansabdars). Feeling publicly insulted, Shivaji voiced his outrage aloud in open court and walked out in defiance. [1, 2, 4]

## House arrest under armed guard in Agra

Following his outburst, Shivaji was placed under house arrest in a guarded camp outside Agra under the watchful eye of the city kotwal, Fulad Khan. Aware that assassination or execution was imminent, Shivaji feigned illness and dismissed his Maratha retainers back to the Deccan to lower the guards' suspicions. [1, 2]

## The legendary escape in sweet baskets

Shivaji began distributing large wicker baskets filled with sweets, fruits, and alms to nobles, Brahmins, and the poor as religious offerings. On **17 August 1666**, once guards had grown careless in inspecting the containers, Shivaji and Sambhaji concealed themselves inside two baskets and were carried past the perimeter to freedom. [1, 2, 4]

## Traveling disguised as an ascetic

To throw off imperial search parties scouring the Deccan roads, Shivaji disguised himself as a religious mendicant (*bairagi*). He traveled by a wide eastward detour through Mathura, Allahabad, Varanasi, and Puri. Young Sambhaji was left safely in the care of trusted Brahmin allies at Mathura. [1, 2]

## Triumphant return to Rajgad

In November 1666, after months of perilous journeying, Shivaji arrived back at **Rajgad** dressed as an ascetic. His mother Jijabai initially failed to recognize the dusty pilgrim until he revealed himself. His astonishing return electrified Maharashtra and dealt a devastating blow to Aurangzeb's prestige. [1, 2]

## The Sahyadri counteroffensive of 1670

After four years of quiet rebuilding and peace, Shivaji launched a ferocious counteroffensive in 1670 to recover the twenty-three forts surrendered under the Treaty of Purandar. In rapid succession, Maratha forces assaulted and recaptured one mountain fortress after another across western Maharashtra. [1, 2]

## Tanaji Malusare’s night assault on Sinhagad

On a freezing night in February 1670, Shivaji’s close friend **Tanaji Malusare** led a daring night assault on the sheer cliffs of **Kondana** (Sinhagad). The Mavalas scaled the vertical precipice using rope ladders and breached the ramparts, engaging the Rajput garrison in fierce combat. [1, 2]

## "The fort is won, but the lion is lost"

Tanaji fought in the frontline, slaying the Rajput governor Udaybhan Rathod before falling mortally wounded on the battlements. His brother Suryaji rallied the troops and secured the fortress. When told of the victory, Shivaji wept bitterly, remarking: "The fort is won, but the lion is lost" (*Gad ala pan sinh gela*). [1, 2]

## Striking Surat a second time

In October 1670, Shivaji struck the wealthy port of **Surat** a second time, carrying off three days of treasure, silver, and military supplies to finance his expanding administration. The second raid proved that the Mughal administration was powerless to protect its most valuable commercial emporium. [1, 2, 4]

## Open-field victory at the Battle of Dindori

On his return march from Surat, a large Mughal cavalry army under Daud Khan attempted to intercept the Marathas near **Dindori** (near Nashik). In a pitched open-field battle, Maratha light cavalry outmaneuvered and routed the imperial forces, proving they could defeat Mughal troops in conventional combat. [1, 2, 4]

## The fort doctrine of the Ajnapatra

Shivaji maintained a network of over three hundred fortresses across western India, described in the contemporary state treatise **Ajnapatra** as the essence of the kingdom. Every fort was stocked with grain, water tanks, and ammunition, with command divided among three officers of different castes to prevent treason. [1, 3]

## Formal Vedic coronation as Chhatrapati

On **6 June 1674**, Shivaji held a formal Vedic coronation (*Rajyabhisheka*) at the mountain capital of **Raigad**, officiated by the scholar **Gaga Bhatt** of Varanasi. He was anointed with sacred river waters and assumed the royal title of **Chhatrapati** ("Lord of the Royal Umbrella"), establishing an undeniable, sovereign Maratha kingdom. [1, 2, 4]

## The passing of Queen Mother Jijabai

Just twelve days after witnessing her son crowned as an independent sovereign monarch, Queen Mother **Jijabai** passed away peacefully at the foot of Raigad. She lived to see her lifelong dream of Swarajya realized. Shivaji mourned her deeply, honoring her memory through charitable grants and state memorials. [1, 2]

## The Ashtapradhan council of ministers

As Chhatrapati, Shivaji codified the **Ashtapradhan** (Council of Eight Ministers), headed by the Peshwa (Prime Minister). Crucially, he decreed that all ministers be paid directly in cash salaries from the royal treasury rather than through hereditary land grants (*jagirs*), preventing feudal rebellion. [1, 3]

## Agrarian protection and the kathi survey

Shivaji overhauled rural governance to protect the cultivator (*ryot*). Land was surveyed using a standardized measuring rod (**kathi**), with taxes assessed strictly as a proportion of actual harvest yields. The state provided interest-free loans (*tagai*) for seeds and cattle during drought, ensuring peasant prosperity. [1, 3]

## Strict code of conduct in warfare

Shivaji enforced strict moral discipline throughout his military forces. Standing regulations forbade the mistreatment of women, the destruction of crops, the plundering of peasants, and the desecration of religious scriptures, whether the Quran or the Vedas. Captured holy books were treated with respect and returned. [1, 3, 4]

## The southern expedition: Dakshin Digvijay

Between 1676 and 1678, Shivaji led his most expansive military campaign: the **Dakshin Digvijay** (Southern Conquest). Marching through Golconda, where he formed an alliance with Sultan Abul Hasan Qutb Shah, Shivaji conquered vast territories across Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, taking **Vellore** and **Gingee**. [1, 2]

## The impregnable bastion of Gingee

The capture and extensive refortification of **Gingee** (Senji) in Tamil Nadu proved to be one of Shivaji's most brilliant strategic decisions. A decade later, when Aurangzeb invaded Maharashtra with the entire Mughal army, the Maratha royal court retreated to Gingee, sustaining resistance for eight years and exhausting the empire. [1, 2]

## Final illness and passing at Raigad

In late March 1680, Shivaji fell ill with severe fever and dysentery at Raigad fort. On **3 April 1680**, at fifty years of age, the founder of the Maratha state passed away. Contemporary observers noted that even hostile imperial chroniclers conceded his strategic genius, personal bravery, and administrative integrity. [1, 2, 4]

## The enduring legacy of Hindavi Swarajya

Following Shivaji's death, Aurangzeb spent twenty-five years personally campaigning in the Deccan with the full might of the Mughal Empire. Yet the forts, military discipline, and national consciousness Shivaji had created survived the onslaught, leading to the collapse of imperial power and the rise of the pan-Indian Maratha Empire. [1, 3]
