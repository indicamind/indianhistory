Vinayak Damodar Savarkar’s life connects revolutionary networks, colonial imprisonment, writing, social reform and Hindu nationalist politics. These dated moments draw on contemporary records, his own texts, court judgments and later historical documentation.

## A childhood near Nashik

Vinayak Damodar Savarkar was born at Bhagur, near Nashik, in the Bombay Presidency. Marathi literary culture and stories of earlier Indian rulers formed part of his early education. [1]

## Ganesh, Narayan and Vinayak

He grew up with brothers Ganesh and Narayan and sister Maina. Ganesh, often called Babarao, became both a revolutionary associate and a fellow prisoner. [2]

## Plague and political awakening

Later biographical accounts connect his political awakening with plague, family bereavements and the execution of the Chapekar brothers. These accounts describe the setting of his early commitment to armed resistance. [5]

## The Mitra Mela circle

Savarkar helped organise Mitra Mela, a circle combining patriotic discussion with revolutionary aims. Sources date its beginnings around 1899–1900, before its expansion into Abhinav Bharat. [1, 27]

## Yamuna enters the story

He married Yamuna, known as Mai. Their married life would be repeatedly interrupted by overseas activism, imprisonment and restrictions; she also petitioned for the imprisoned brothers’ release. [27, 30]

## Fergusson College

At Fergusson College, Savarkar joined a politically active student world. Pune connected his reading and organising with Tilak’s nationalist politics and the developing swadeshi movement. [2]

## Abhinav Bharat takes shape

The earlier circle developed into Abhinav Bharat. The organisation sought independence through secret networks, political education and preparation for revolutionary action, extending beyond one student association. [2, 27]

## Foreign cloth on the bonfire

A contemporary police report records Savarkar urging a crowd to discard foreign goods during Pune’s boycott agitation. Tilak and Paranjpe participated in the procession and speeches. [3]

## A penalty at college

His participation in the foreign-cloth bonfire brought a fine and removal from the college hostel. The punishment made the relationship between student politics and institutional authority immediately personal. [7]

## A scholarship to London

With support from Tilak and Shyamaji Krishnavarma’s scholarship network, Savarkar travelled to Britain to study law. London also offered a base for revolutionary organising. [4]

## Inside India House

He lived at India House, 65 Cromwell Avenue. The hostel gathered Indian students and political exiles, providing accommodation, discussion and an organisational setting for anticolonial activism. [4]

## The Free India Society

Savarkar organised the Free India Society in London. Meetings combined political discussion, commemorations and advocacy of independence, connecting student life with a programme of national liberation. [1, 2]

## Mazzini in Marathi

His Marathi treatment of Giuseppe Mazzini’s life and writings brought an Italian revolutionary model to Indian readers. Secret association, sacrifice and national unification supplied political lessons. [7]

## Remembering the rebels of 1857

Savarkar and his associates commemorated the uprising of 1857 in London. Badges, meetings and publications recast the rebels as martyrs whose struggle remained unfinished. [2, 12]

## An appeal to the dead

His leaflet “Oh Martyrs” addressed the participants of 1857 as predecessors of a continuing struggle. Commemoration became a call to future political action rather than a detached anniversary. [12]

## The Indian War of Independence

His history of 1857 appeared in English in 1909 after difficulties publishing the Marathi manuscript. A work of history also became an intervention in the politics of independence. [6]

## An argument for common resistance

The book emphasised shared Hindu-Muslim resistance and presented the uprising as a war for independence. Its organising interpretation challenged British descriptions centred on military mutiny. [6]

## A book beyond the censor

British authorities sought to suppress the book’s circulation. Publication and distribution through overseas networks made the history itself part of the contest over imperial power and political memory. [4, 6]

## Dhingra’s assassination of Wyllie

Madan Lal Dhingra killed Curzon Wyllie and Cawas Lalcaca in London. Savarkar supported Dhingra’s cause, while many other Indian public figures condemned the assassination. [2]

## Law examinations and political scrutiny

He pursued legal studies, but the Inn withheld his call to the Bar amid scrutiny of his political activities. His intended professional qualification became entangled with revolutionary politics. [1]

## Babarao receives transportation

Ganesh Savarkar’s prosecution and sentence placed the revolutionary network under increasing pressure. Vinayak’s later prison memoir recalls the anguish of discovering his brother in the Andamans. [5]

## Jackson is assassinated

Anant Kanhere killed Nashik collector A. M. T. Jackson. The investigation into revolutionary networks and weapons supplied from abroad led to a separate prosecution of Savarkar for abetment. [10]

## Arrest on his return

After a stay in Paris, Savarkar returned to London and was arrested. Proceedings under the Fugitive Offenders Act led to his transfer to India to face trial. [4, 8]

## A prisoner aboard the Morea

British officers escorted him aboard the Morea. The vessel’s stop at Marseilles created the opportunity for the escape that would bring France and Britain into an international dispute. [9]

## Through a porthole to French soil

Savarkar escaped through a porthole, entered the water and reached the French shore. The escape briefly placed him outside the ship and within French territory. [9]

## Returned to the ship

A French maritime policeman detained him and handed him back to British custody. France subsequently demanded his return, disputing how the prisoner had been removed from French territory. [9]

## The court proceeds with the case

Savarkar challenged the consequences of his recapture. The Bombay High Court held that an irregular overseas arrest did not remove its jurisdiction to try offences for which he was committed. [8]

## The first life sentence

The special tribunal sentenced Savarkar to transportation for life in the revolutionary conspiracy prosecution. Confiscation of property accompanied the sentence, extending punishment beyond the prison term. [10]

## A second transportation for life

A separate trial concerning abetment of Jackson’s murder ended in another life sentence. The consecutive sentences were commonly reckoned as fifty years of transportation. [10]

## The Hague award

The arbitral tribunal found no obligation requiring Britain to return him to France. Its decision concerned the international custody dispute rather than the merits of the Indian criminal charges. [9]

## The Cellular Jail

Savarkar arrived at the Cellular Jail in the Andamans. His account describes isolated cells and the discipline of a penal settlement far from his family. [11]

## A dangerous-prisoner designation

In his 1913 petition, he described being classified as dangerous and subjected to solitary confinement. He contrasted his treatment with that of other prisoners transported alongside him. [13]

## Coir pounding

The petition describes pounding coir until his hands bled. Such details appear in his own account of the physical demands and unequal treatment he asked the government to reconsider. [13]

## At the oil mill

He described labour at the oil mill as exceptionally severe. The memoir and petition connect the work with exhaustion, discipline and deteriorating health. [11, 13]

## Letters to Narayan

Letters to his younger brother carried news, requests for books and reflections on education and public life. Their passage through prison censorship shaped this fragile connection with home. [12]

## Books and fellow prisoners

His memoir describes teaching, shared reading and efforts to organise prisoners. Intellectual work became part of how he remembered surviving confinement. [11]

## Poetry without a writing desk

Accounts of his imprisonment describe composing and memorising verse when writing materials were restricted. Poetry, including work later associated with Kamala, occupied a substantial place in his literary life. [1, 11]

## The petition’s political promises

Alongside complaints about prison treatment, he offered support for constitutional progress and loyalty to the British government. The text also referred to a previous clemency petition in 1911. [13]

## An offer framed around the Empire

Another petition welcomed Indian military service and proposed concessions and release for political prisoners. He presented constitutional cooperation within the Empire as a possible path forward. [13]

## Protest inside the jail

His retrospective account describes successive prisoners’ strikes, punishment and struggles over work and treatment. These episodes form part of the prison history alongside his petitions. [11]

## Gandhi advises Narayan

Responding to Narayan Savarkar, Gandhi suggested a brief petition emphasising the political character of the offences. The letter addressed the brothers’ release campaign in 1920. [14]

## Another application for release

Savarkar again requested clemency, affirming constitutional development and offering a defined period away from politics. He sought release for himself and his brother under the postwar proclamation. [13]

## A public case for release

Gandhi publicly questioned the brothers’ continued imprisonment under the royal amnesty. His article relied on their declared political position and argued that their case deserved consideration. [15]

## Leaving the Andamans

The brothers were transferred to the mainland. Vinayak passed through further imprisonment, including Ratnagiri, remaining in custody until his conditional release in 1924. [11, 30]

## Essentials of Hindutva

Essentials of Hindutva appeared under the name “A Maratha.” Written during his imprisonment, it set out a definition of Hindu nationhood that became central to his subsequent politics. [16]

## Hindutva and Hinduism

He distinguished Hindutva from religion alone, describing a broader historical and cultural identity. Territory, ancestry and a shared civilisation were central components of the argument. [16]

## Fatherland and holy land

His definition joined attachment to India as fatherland with its recognition as holy land. The formulation included several Indian religious traditions within a common Hindu identity. [16]

## The boundary in the definition

Muslims and Christians could share ancestry and residence, but their sacred centres abroad placed them outside his full definition. This was his proposed national boundary, distinct from birthplace alone. [16]

## Release under conditions

Savarkar left prison under conditions restricting residence and political participation. Ratnagiri became his base; the conditions continued to shape his public activity for more than a decade. [11, 27]

## A constrained public life

He lived under restrictions in Ratnagiri district. Writing and work against caste disabilities became major activities during his restricted residence, alongside arguments for Hindu political consolidation. [2, 17]

## The Maratha past in print

Hindu-Pad-Padashahi presented the rise of Maratha power through a Hindu national interpretation. Historical writing remained one of his principal methods of explaining political identity and collective action. [21]

## Majhi Janmathep

His Marathi prison memoir, later translated as My Transportation for Life, made confinement a sustained narrative. Its descriptions are a participant’s retrospective account of the penal system. [11]

## A meeting with Gandhi

Gandhi visited Savarkar at Ratnagiri. The encounter brought together two men whose approaches to religious reform, political methods and national identity differed substantially. [27]

## Breaking seven prohibitions

He identified seven social prohibitions involving touch, dining, marriage, occupation, sea travel, Vedic rites and reconversion. Removing these disabilities formed a programme for reorganising Hindu society. [18]

## Patit Pavan Mandir

The Patit Pavan temple opened worship to Hindus across caste boundaries. Savarkar supported the institution, built with patron Bhagoji Keer, as a practical challenge to exclusion. [19, 20]

## A common meal

He promoted meals across caste boundaries and argued that eating together did not change a person’s religion. Such events made social reform visible in everyday conduct. [18]

## Priesthood beyond birth

His writings argued for choosing priests by merit rather than caste and encouraged worship without obligatory priestly mediation. Access to ritual authority was part of his reform agenda. [17]

## Against inherited disabilities

He challenged the assumption that birth guaranteed a caste’s special abilities or privileges. His arguments connected social equality with the strength of a consolidated Hindu society. [17]

## Working through existing associations

He could oppose caste discrimination while proposing tactical use of caste associations during reform. Their eventual redundancy, rather than an immediate disappearance by declaration, figured in his programme. [17]

## Poems, plays and language

Alongside political prose, he wrote poetry and drama and campaigned over Marathi vocabulary. His literary career encompassed historical narrative, translation, public speech and formal experiments in verse. [1]

## The restrictions end

The government lifted the restrictions on his residence and political activity. Savarkar could now travel and organise publicly on a national scale. [2]

## President of the Hindu Mahasabha

Elected Mahasabha president, he proposed organised Hindu political power as an independent force in the struggle over India’s future. [22]

## Two nations within India

He described Hindus and Muslims as two principal nations, while advocating a common Indian state rather than accepting territorial partition. [22]

## Equal citizenship in his programme

He advocated common civic rights, minority cultural safeguards and one-person-one-vote, while insisting on undivided loyalty to the Indian state. [22]

## Europe in his political argument

He used German Jews as an example of separate nationhood. In 1940, he praised Nazism’s role in Germany’s revival while arguing that India should choose allies by national interest. [22]

## Support for unarmed resistance

He supported the campaign against restrictions on Hindus under Hyderabad’s Nizam. The Mahasabha’s programme included civil resistance as well as military preparedness. [22]

## Recruitment and military training

He urged Hindus to enlist in the armed forces and gain military skills during Britain’s war, presenting militarisation as a national objective. [22]

## Opposition to Quit India

He opposed Congress’s Quit India course and instructed Mahasabha supporters to retain positions in government and wartime service rather than abandon them. [22]

## Coalitions with political opponents

He defended practical compromises, citing the Sind coalition with the Muslim League and the Bengal ministry involving Syama Prasad Mookerjee and Fazlul Huq. [22]

## A change in the presidency

Ill health prompted attempts to relinquish the presidency. By 1944 leadership passed to Syama Prasad Mookerjee, while Savarkar remained an influential ideological figure. [23]

## Against the division of India

He opposed Partition and continued to argue for an undivided state. The transfer of power nevertheless produced independent India and Pakistan rather than the settlement he advocated. [23]

## Independence at Savarkar Sadan

He welcomed the end of British rule while continuing to oppose the division of the country. His statements sought to combine independence with an undivided national ideal. [23]

## Accused after Gandhi’s murder

After Nathuram Godse killed Gandhi on 30 January, Savarkar was arrested and became one of the accused in the conspiracy prosecution. The trial took place at Delhi’s Red Fort. [24, 29]

## His response to the allegations

He denied participating in the conspiracy. His defence challenged the approver Digambar Badge’s account, treating alleged conversations and inferred instructions as uncorroborated rather than admissions of guilt. [31]

## Acquitted by the trial court

Judge Atma Charan acquitted Savarkar. The prosecution’s case did not establish his guilt; the acquittal was not overturned in the appeals involving the other defendants. [24, 26]

## Welcoming the Republic

In a message issued before Republic Day, he welcomed the inauguration of independent India’s republic and called for unity and national defence. [23]

## Detention under independent India

He was detained during the period of the Nehru–Liaquat Pact. After release with political restrictions, he resigned even primary membership of the Hindu Mahasabha. [23]

## Asking an old associate to write

His letters urged S. R. Rana to preserve memories of the revolutionary movement in Europe. The correspondence shows an active effort to shape the record future historians would inherit. [23]

## Closing Abhinav Bharat

At a public function, Savarkar announced the dissolution of the old revolutionary organisation. He presented the achievement of independence as the completion of its founding objective. [27]

## Six Glorious Epochs

His final major historical work interpreted Indian history through resistance and the survival of Hindu political power. It extended themes of nationhood, warfare and historical memory developed in earlier books. [28]

## The death of Yamuna

Yamuna Savarkar died in 1963. Her life had spanned the London years, long imprisonment, Ratnagiri restrictions and the family’s later residence in Bombay. [27]

## His final illness and death

After prolonged ill health, Savarkar stopped accepting food and medicine in his final weeks. He died on 26 February 1966, aged eighty-two, describing his decision through the idea of atmarpan. [1, 32]

## The Kapur Commission’s record

The commission examined statements from secretary Gajanan Damle and bodyguard Appa Kasar concerning visits by Godse and Apte. Their accounts formed part of its reconstruction of the conspiracy. [25]

## A finding different from the acquittal

The commission attributed the murder conspiracy to Savarkar and his group. It reported after his death and was an inquiry commission, not a criminal court conducting another trial. [25, 26]

## The acquittal and the inquiry distinguished

The Supreme Court said the commission’s observations could not overturn his acquittal. It declined to reopen the case or decide the correctness of the commission’s findings. [26]

Savarkar, *Historic Statements*, items from 1944–1952.** Includes the 1 August 1944 message favouring Mookerjee as president; statements against Partition; the January 1950 Republic message; March 1950 letters to S. R. Rana; and the July 1950 resignation following detention and restrictions. [Dated statements](https://www.savarkar.org/en/encyc/2017/5/23/2_12_15_55_historic_statements_by_savarkar.v001.pdf_1.pdf).

24. **Special Court, Red Fort, Judge Atma Charan, judgment in the Gandhi murder case, 10 February 1949.** Savarkar was acquitted. His result is distinct from the convictions of other defendants. [Trial judgment scan, reproduced by Bar & Bench](https://images.assettype.com/barandbench-kannada/2023-01/e87f775e-205c-4875-a740-3ecc70f18ee0/Godse___TC___Judgmentt.pdf); [High Court’s appellate judgment in its official archive](https://highcourtchd.gov.in/landmark_judgments/HC/English/criminal%20appeal_66_1949.pdf).

25. **Justice J. L. Kapur, *Report of the Commission of Inquiry into the Conspiracy to Murder Mahatma Gandhi* (1969), volume II, chapter 25, especially paragraphs 25.92–25.106, culminating on printed p. 303.** The report discusses Damle and Kasar’s statements and reaches its own conclusion about the conspiracy. This was a posthumous commission report, not a judgment overturning the acquittal. [Full report scans](https://archive.org/details/JeevanlalKapoorCommissionReport).

26. **Supreme Court of India, *Dr. Pankaj Kumudchandra Phadnis v. Union of India*, 28 March 2018, especially paragraphs 6–8.** The Court distinguished the commission’s observations from an enforceable criminal judgment and declined to reopen the matter or rule on the correctness or fairness of the report’s observations. [Official judgment](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2017/15103/15103_2017_Judgement_28-Mar-2018.pdf).

27. **Savarkar memorial, “Chronology of Savarkar’s Life.”** Used for selected family and later-life dates and the Ratnagiri restrictions, with cross-checks against other records. This commemorative chronology contains inconsistencies: for example, it dates a Bose visit to June 1941 when he was already abroad, differs over the second sentence’s day, and states an age at death inconsistent with the birth and death dates. Those claims are not reproduced here. [Chronology](https://savarkar.org/en/Encyc/2017/5/22/Chronology-Of-Savarkar-s-Life.html).

28. **V. D. Savarkar, *Six Glorious Epochs of Indian History*, English translation of his late Marathi work.** Used to identify his historical method and continuing emphasis on Hindu political resistance, not as independent proof of every ancient or medieval episode it narrates. [Text via the author’s collection](https://savarkar.org/en/Encyc/2017/5/26/Download.html).

29. **Central Information Commission, record concerning access to the Gandhi assassination files, especially the chronology of trial and appeals.** An official later account of the case records rather than a new investigation into criminal guilt. [Official decision and record summary](https://www.dsscic.nic.in/files/upload_decision/1055_Corrected_Gandhi_Assassination_Hemanta_V._Mo_Culture__16.2.pdf).

30. **Government of India, Home Department proceedings, June 1921, proceeding no. 201.** Contains material on the amnesty and the Savarkar brothers, including Yamunabai’s petition of 18 April 1921. The scan’s modern filename is not the original document title. [Reproduced proceedings](https://savarkar.org/en/pdfs/Petition%20regarding%20conditional%20release%20of%20Savarkar.pdf).

31. **Savarkar’s written statement in the Gandhi assassination trial (1948).** A defence statement challenging the prosecution’s alleged conversations and the approver’s inference. Its denials are identified as his defence rather than an independent finding. [Statement](https://savarkar.org/en/encyc/2017/5/28/Written-Statement-of-Savarkar.html).

32. **V. D. Savarkar, “Suicide and Self-Sacrifice,” translated from his late essay.** Used for his own explanation of the term *atmarpan*. [Translated essay](https://savarkar.org/en/pdfs/Suicide_and_Self_Sacrifice.pdf).
